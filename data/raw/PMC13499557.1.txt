Background

Rabies is still a public health concern, especially in low resource settings with poor animal health management, stock out of rabies vaccine and immunoglobulin. Only 20% of patients present with paralytic rabies and it is at times difficult to distinguish from Guillain-Barre syndrome. In malaria endemic areas, rabies may have atypical presentation. Rabies encephalitis is almost always fatal.

Case Presentation

We report an 8‑year‑and‑6‑month‑old African boy from Katakwi district in North Eastern Uganda who presented with headache, fever and waist pain followed by progressive lower‑limb weakness 28 days after a bite by a stray dog. There was no trauma, and he had no signs of meningeal irritation. He received anti-rabies vaccine on day 0.7 and 16 but did not get rabies immunoglobulin. He received supportive care including intravenous fluids and antibiotics at the hospital and died 7 days after the onset of symptoms.

Conclusion

Paralytic rabies in children can be fatal even after vaccination, especially if rabies immunoglobulin is not given when there is a high risk exposure. There are also diagnostic challenges if there is an atypical presentation, particularly in children. Health system gaps around animal health can lead to preventable deaths from rabies.

Background

Rabies remains a major public health problem in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where canine rabies is endemic.1–3 Human rabies is almost invariably fatal once clinical disease manifests.3,4 However, it is almost entirely preventable through prompt wound care, administration of rabies vaccine and infiltration of rabies immunoglobulin (RIG) after animal/dog bite.2 Most cases of rabies are classified as furious or encephalitic.4,5 Furious rabies often presents with agitation, hydrophobia, hypersalivation and in late stages barking and dog-like behavior. It accounts for about 80% of the cases and has been reported to be fatal, with case fatality rates of almost 100%.2,5,6 Paralytic (dumb) rabies, on the other hand, is less common and accounts for only about 20% of human rabies cases. It lacks the classical features of hydrophobia and aerophobia seen in furious rabies. Paralytic rabies frequently presents with ascending flaccid paralysis and autonomic dysfunction, closely mimicking Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS). This atypical presentation may delay recognition, particularly in resource-limited settings where confirmatory diagnostic testing is often unavailable.6

In furious/encephalitic rabies, the virus targets the limbic system that controls emotion and aggression and the cerebral cortex,4 while in paralytic rabies, it targets the spinal cord, causing myelitis, and the peripheral nervous system, causing neuritis especially at the site of the bite.4 In paralytic rabies, the virus enters the body through a break in the skin following a bite, scratch or lick from an infected animal, commonly dogs, especially in LMIC. The virus does not enter the bloodstream; rather, it replicates in the muscles and connective tissue near the site of the bite. This incubation period can last a few weeks to months5 as the virus moves in a retrograde fashion along the axons of the peripheral nervous system at about 50–100 mm per day.7 In the spinal cord the virus replicates massively and causes myelitis in the ventral horns, leading to flaccid paralysis of the muscles supplied by those nerves.8 The damage is normally worse on the side where the bite has occurred. The paralysis typically spreads and ascends to affect the contralateral side of the body, abdominal muscles, diaphragm and eventually the brain. In paralytic rabies, even when the virus reaches the brain, the aggression and hydrophobia are not pronounced as the patients are deeply paralyzed.9 The most common causes of death are normally due to paralysis of the diaphragm, chest wall muscles, cranial nerves involving the airway and respiratory centers and aspiration pneumonitis.5 Where post mortem is possible, a finding of Negri bodies in the hippocampal pyramidal cells, cerebellar purkinje cells and brainstem nuclei is confirmatory.10 There is limited case reports on paralytic rabies with a few cases reported in India following dog bites, notably mimicking Guillain–Barré syndrome.11,12

Whereas the clinical course of rabies encephalitis is clearly documented, there is paucity of data on the epidemiological distribution of the cases, especially for paralytic rabies. For example, in low-and middle - income countries where animal control regulations are not strongly adhered to, there is more need for contextual data on dog bites.6 Moreover, epidemiological data on areas, seasons and times of the year when there are more dog bites would improve surveillance eventual public health control measures.

We describe a fatal case of paralytic rabies in an 8‑year‑and‑6‑month‑old boy managed at Katakwi General Hospital (KGH), where a total of 223 dog‑bite cases have been reported between February 2025 and February 2026 (Katakwi General Hospital records, unpublished data). Katakwi General Hospital is a government-owned district hospital in the Teso sub-region of North‑Eastern Uganda. Established as a Health Centre IV in 2004, and upgraded to a general hospital in 2011, Katakwi General Hospital serves a predominantly rural population from Katakwi District and neighboring areas, acting as a key referral facility within the district health system. The hospital provides general medical, pediatrics, maternity and basic surgical services. However, like many public hospitals in rural Uganda, it faces challenges including limited diagnostic capacity, drug stock-outs staffing shortages and resource constraints, which affect timely diagnosis and management of complex conditions such as rabies encephalitis. The hospital has an emergency department managed by an emergency physician and patients with dog bites are first received here before being taken to their respective wards. Despite frequent stock-outs of anti-rabies vaccine, many patients buy from nearby private pharmacies.

The hospital uses WHO guidelines for management of dog bites.13 In the guidelines, a case definition of rabies includes; 1) Suspected case – acute neurological syndromes characterized by hyperactivity or paralytic syndromes not explained by other common causes, 2) Probable case – suspected case with credible history of exposure (animal bite, scratch, or saliva contact on broken skin) in a non-vaccinated person, 3) Confirmed case – suspected or probable case confirmed through laboratory studies.13 WHO also recognizes three exposure categories: a) Category I: Touching/feeding animals, licks on intact skin, b) Category II: Nibbling of uncovered skin, minor scratches or abrasions without bleeding, c) Category III: Single or multiple transdermal bites or scratches, licks on broken skin, contamination of mucous membrane with saliva, and contacts with bats.6,13,14

Case Presentation

Clinical Presentation

An 8-year-6-month-old Ugandan boy from Ngariam County in Katakwi District in North Eastern Uganda presented with a five‑day history of headache and fever and a four‑day history of waist pain followed by progressive lower‑limb weakness. He had been well until 28 days prior to admission when he suffered a dog bite on the distal lateral aspect of his right thigh. He sustained three bite wounds that pierced through his skin, a WHO category III dog bite.13 The dog, which was previously tame and friendly, was reported to have become restless and aggressive. It had also bitten another child 12 hours later, and it was subsequently killed. Immediately after the bite, the wounds were cleaned and dressed at a health facility, and the patient purportedly received an inactivated rabies vaccine (purified chick embryo cell vaccine) on days 0, 7, and 16 given intramuscularly in the deltoid muscle. Whereas WHO recommends a 4 dose regimen for post exposure prophylaxis on days 0, 3,7 and 14, we deviated because the vaccine was being procured privately at a cost to the patient. Rabies immunoglobulin was not administered.

Seventeen days after the bite, he developed gradual-onset headache that became continuous and worsened at night, associated with low-grade intermittent fever. He was given over‑the‑counter paracetamol from home and no medical care was sought for this. He progressively also developed lower back pain followed by progressive lower‑limb weakness and failure to walk and stand well over the course of about five days. He, however, had no tingling sensations and his bowel and micturition habits were normal. The caretakers reported no convulsions, hydrophobia, aerophobia, hypersalivation, or fasciculations at the bite site. His level of consciousness progressively declined, and this prompted them to bring him to hospital.

In the past medical history, he had no history of any chronic illness, no previous surgeries, and no known drug allergies. He had no history of a tick bite. He was reported to have received all other vaccines as per the Uganda National Expanded Program on Immunization (UNEPI) schedule, though documentation was unavailable. Developmental milestones had been appropriate for age, and there was no family history of neurological disease. He was in primary two and performing well in class.

Examination

On admission, he was acutely ill-looking, febrile (38.1°C), tachycardic (141 bpm), and hypoxic (SpO2 87% on room air). His blood pressure was 118/70 mmHg and respiratory rate was 22 breaths per minute. He had mild pallor and mild dehydration. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13/15 on day 1 (E-4, V-4, M-5), which declined to 4/15 (E-1V-1M-2) by day 4. Pupils were initially dilated and reactive but became constricted and poorly reactive. The lower limbs had reduced tone and power (1/5) on day 1. The upper limbs had normal tone and power (5/5) on day 1. Neck stiffness and Kerning’s sign were absent.

Cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds without murmurs. Respiratory examination showed reduced bilateral air entry with use of accessory muscles but no wheeze or crepitations. The abdominal examination revealed distension with a palpable bladder, relieved by catheterization.

Based on the history of a confirmed dog bite, incomplete post-exposure prophylaxis (absence of rabies immunoglobulin), prodromal febrile illness, ascending limb paralysis, autonomic dysfunction (urinary retention), poor mentation with no meningeal signs, a clinical diagnosis of paralytic rabies was made.

Our differential diagnoses were Guillain–Barré syndrome due to the progressive paralysis and cerebral malaria due to altered mentation and a positive malaria smear. A full blood count was not done, and neither was a lumbar puncture and CSF analysis. However, a positive history of a dog bite and an almost classical presentation made paralytic rabies a more likely primary diagnosis.

Management

A blood smear showed trophozoites of plasmodium falciparum, and he received intravenous artesunate. He was managed with supportive care, including oxygen via nasal prongs, intravenous fluids (normal saline, Ringer’s lactate, and 5% dextrose) and antipyretics. A repeat blood smear for malaria done 24 hours later showed no malaria parasites. The ward had no formal isolation room; therefore, he was admitted to a corner on the ward as far away as possible from the other patients. No confirmatory rabies diagnostic tests (RT-PCR from saliva or CSF, skin biopsy immunofluorescence, or CSF analysis) were performed due to limited resources.

Patient Progression

On the second hospital day, he became restless, had incoherent speech, and developed abnormal movements on the upper limbs but no aggression or need for restraint. His muscle tone and power had also declined. On the third day, he was unable to pass urine and had a full bladder on examination, with 750 ml of urine drained upon catheterization. By the fourth day, he had become unconscious and developed progressive respiratory depression. He subsequently deteriorated and died at home on 29 September 2025, 4 days after presenting to hospital. Discussion

Rabies is a zoonotic viral disease that is almost always fatal once the clinical manifestations have developed. However, it is preventable through the timely and correct administration of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).14 The disease still poses a significant public health burden in low- and middle-income countries, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing the highest percentage of rabies-related mortality, of which 40% of the deaths are experienced among children below the age of 15.15 This is a case report of a fatal rabies infection in a child from rural North Eastern Uganda that was characterized mainly by a paralytic presentation. In this patient, rabies still occurred despite the administration of anti-rabies vaccination, but no rabies immunoglobulin (RIG) given.

The patient developed progressive neurological deterioration about 4 weeks after being bitten by a stray dog. This duration is consistent with the well-established incubation period of rabies, which is usually in the range of one to 3 months, but which may be shorter in severe cases, or when the bites occur closer to the central nervous system.15,16 Despite receiving immediate wound care and being given a course of anti-rabies vaccination, the RIG was not available and was not given. For this patient, the exposure falls under the World Health Organization (WHO) category III classification, which requires RIG as a key component of the PEP.16 The absence of RIG is associated with failure of PEP, as passive immunization provided by the RIG is important for neutralizing the virus at the wound site before the vaccine-induced active immunity begins to take effect.17 Previous reports have demonstrated that the use of vaccine-only prophylaxis may delay disease onset, but is insufficient to prevent rabies in high-risk exposures, especially in children.17,18 This is the likely scenario in this case.

The clinical features observed in this case are consistent with the paralytic (or “dumb”) form of rabies. This represents about 20% of all cases of rabies in people and is often less recognized, particularly in children.19 In contrast, the more commonly described furious form is associated with characteristic features such as hydrophobia, aerophobia, hypersalivation, and restlessness. Paralytic rabies more often presents with ascending flaccid paralysis, loss or weakness of the tendon reflexes, and features of autonomic dysfunction, and is often clinically indistinguishable from Guillain-Barré syndrome.20,21 In this patient, the occurrence of progressive weakness of the lower limbs leading to paralysis, together with sensory loss, urinary retention, and symptoms related to an evolving encephalopathy, more supported a diagnosis of paralytic rabies over other possible differentials.3 The rapid progression to loss of consciousness further ruled out Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), which rarely causes early onset of loss of consciousness. CSF studies may be supportive to distinguish GBS from any other encephalopathy. Transverse myelitis and cerebral malaria have been noted to cause atypical presentations of rabies according to a study done in Malawi, a malaria‑endemic area like North‑Eastern Uganda.22 In this patient, the presence of malaria could have worsened the clinical picture and contributed to the death.

Although the classic features of rabies, for example hydrophobia, hypersalivation, and aerophobia, were absent in this patient, their absence should not preclude consideration of rabies, especially in endemic settings with low resources where rabies remains mostly a clinical diagnosis. Access to confirmatory investigations, including reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, immunofluorescence assays, or advanced neuroimaging, is often limited or unavailable.3,23,24 A clear history of exposure and recognition of characteristic patterns of disease progression are critical in providing a timely diagnosis, proper clinical decision-making, and ensuring that appropriate infection control measures are taken.

Given that rabies occurred in a child who sought healthcare in time after being exposed, and reportedly received PEP, this case reveals a broader public health challenge.2,3,17 The limited availability of RIG, inadequate risk assessment at peripheral health facilities, and the absence of continuing medical education to adopt rabies management guidelines are some of the major barriers in many rural settings.2 Furthermore, the dog that bit the child was reported to have attacked another child and was later killed without veterinary assessment, which represents a missed opportunity for surveillance, contact tracing, and intervention at the community level. All these gaps together stress the need for a strengthened One Health approach that integrates human, animal and environmental health services to reduce preventable rabies transmission and mortality.16,25

On a population level, deaths due to rabies that occur among children serve as key indicators of failures in prevention systems rather than limitations of clinical care alone. Evidence-based, cost-effective prevention strategies, including the mass dog vaccination, better access to complete PEP, and community education, significantly control rabies and support eradication efforts.13 In order to realize the global target of zero human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by the year 2030, this will require strengthening health-worker training, ensuring consistent availability of vaccines and rabies immunoglobulin, and better integrated surveillance.13,16 Clinicians working in endemic settings with low resources should always maintain a high index of suspicion for rabies in children presenting with acute flaccid paralysis or unexplained encephalopathy and ensure timely, complete PEP following exposure to animal bites.13,20

Patient Perspective

The parents were stressed throughout the period of admission, especially since they had brought the child soon after the dog bite. Also, the fact that rabies was inevitably fatal prompted them to ask for an early discharge to avoid expenses related to a hospital death and transportation of the body.

Conclusion

This case report describes a most likely suspected fatal paralytic rabies infection in a child following incomplete PEP, emphasizing that rabies vaccination without RIG may be insufficient in serious, high-risk exposures.

There are diagnostic challenges posed by the atypical presentation of rabies, particularly in children. Health system gaps, especially around animal control, may lead to preventable deaths from rabies. Strengthening early recognition, ensuring access to complete prophylaxis, and reinforcing public health prevention measures will be important in reducing rabies-related mortality in endemic regions.
