Background

Cutaneous nocardiosis differs from pulmonary and disseminated nocardiosis in that it can occur in immunocompetent individuals and is usually localized with a favorable prognosis. However, the phylogenetic relationships, genomic characteristics, and virulence and antimicrobial resistance profiles of human cutaneous Nocardia isolates remain poorly understood.

Purpose

This study aimed to characterize the phylogenetic relationships and genomic features of human cutaneous Nocardia isolates and to investigate their virulence-associated and antimicrobial resistance determinants, as well as their phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility profiles.

Materials and Methods

A total of 31 human cutaneous Nocardia genomes were included for comparative genomic analysis, comprising nine clinical isolates sequenced in this study and 22 publicly available genomes retrieved from the NCBI database. Phylogenetic reconstruction, average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis, and pan-genome analysis were performed to investigate the genomic relationships and diversity of the isolates. Putative virulence-associated and antimicrobial resistance genes were identified through genome-based annotation. In vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed for 13 isolates against 15 antimicrobial agents.

Results

Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated clear species-level clustering and identified a potentially novel cutaneous pathogen, Nocardia sp. NK_136. Pan-genome analysis revealed an open pan-genome with substantial genomic plasticity. Virulence profiling identified a broadly conserved core framework mainly associated with stress response, immune evasion, iron acquisition, secretion systems, and host interaction, together with variable virulence-associated modules distributed in a species- or strain-specific manner. A total of 40 antimicrobial resistance genes were identified, showing marked species-dependent distribution. Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing demonstrated that all tested isolates were susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) and linezolid, whereas variable resistance was observed to cefepime, cefoxitin, ciprofloxacin, tobramycin, and clarithromycin.

Conclusion

Human cutaneous Nocardia isolates possess a conserved core virulence-associated genomic framework accompanied by variable virulence modules, while their antimicrobial resistance determinants and phenotypic resistance profiles show substantial species-dependent variation. These findings provide genomic insights into the diversity, potential pathogenicity, and antimicrobial resistance of cutaneous Nocardia isolates and provide a basis for further investigation of the pathogenesis and clinical management of cutaneous nocardiosis.

Introduction

Nocardia are Gram-positive actinomycetes widely distributed in soil, water, and decaying vegetation and are recognized as opportunistic pathogens in humans.1 Infection may involve the skin and lungs and can disseminate hematogenously to the central nervous system and other organs, leading to systemic disease with substantially increased morbidity and mortality.2,3 Risk factors for severe disseminated nocardiosis include human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, alcoholism, organ transplantation, and autoimmune disease.4 Human infection occurs mainly through inhalation or by traumatic inoculation via disrupted skin.5 Although nocardiosis predominantly affects immunocompromised patients, about 40% of cases occur in immunocompetent individuals; disseminated infection is typical in the former, while primary cutaneous infection is more common in the latter.6,7 Cutaneous nocardiosis is associated with low mortality and responds well to 3–6 months of antibiotic therapy, such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), resulting in an overall favorable prognosis.8 However, untreated or inadequately treated cases may progress to disseminated disease, significantly increasing the risk of death.9 Therefore, promptly identifying the infecting Nocardia species and administering appropriate antibiotics is critical. In recent years, improvements in clinical awareness, diagnostic approaches including genomic technologies, and healthcare resources have substantially increased the detection of nocardiosis.8,9 Current research on Nocardia has mainly focused on case reports,10–12 epidemiological investigations,8,13,14 and genomic analyses of individual species or a limited number of strains,15–18 whereas systematic comparative genomic studies of clinically derived cutaneous isolates remain lacking.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) provides a high-throughput approach for comprehensively identifying antimicrobial resistance genes and characterizing the pathogenic mechanisms and evolutionary relationships of Nocardia strains.18 In addition, WGS can reveal the genetic architecture and functional potential of isolates, thereby supporting accurate species identification and informing clinical treatment decisions. This technology also facilitates the development of novel diagnostic strategies and improves the efficiency and diagnostic capacity of clinical microbiology laboratories.17

In this study, we performed whole-genome sequencing and annotation of nine clinical Nocardia strains isolated from patients with skin infections. To further elucidate their molecular characteristics, we conducted a comparative genomic analysis of 31 Nocardia strains, including 22 publicly available genomes retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database and the nine clinical strains sequenced in this study. The analysis focused on phylogenetic relationships, genomic features, potential virulence-related genes, and antimicrobial resistance gene profiles. This study aimed to characterize the phylogenetic relationships, genomic features, and virulence and resistance gene profiles of cutaneous Nocardia isolates, and to preliminarily investigate the potential mechanisms underlying the occurrence of cutaneous nocardiosis in immunocompetent hosts and its relatively favorable prognosis.

Methods

Isolates Collection and Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS)

Nine Nocardia isolates were isolated from skin samples collected in four provinces in China (Beijing, Hunan Province, Zhejiang Province, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region). After purification, the bacterial isolates were preserved as stock cultures in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 25% glycerol and stored at −80°C. For routine cultivation, isolates were streaked onto BHI agar plates containing 5% sheep blood and incubated at 37°C for 48–72h with agitation.

All isolates were subcultured for two consecutive generations in brain heart infusion (BHI) liquid medium. Bacterial cells were then harvested by centrifugation to obtain cell pellets, from which genomic DNA was extracted using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Whole-genome sequencing of DNA samples was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform in PE150 mode (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), ensuring that the sequencing depth of all samples exceeded 100×. Raw reads were subjected to quality control using Fastp v0.23.4,19 followed by de novo assembly with SPAdes v3.15.5.20 Genome annotation was performed using Prokka v1.12.21 The raw sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA), National Genomics Data Center under accession numbers CRA028987, CRA011633, and CRA003805. Genome size and GC content were calculated with Quast v5.0.2,22 and genome quality, including completeness and contamination, was evaluated using CheckM v1.1.3.23

Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined for 13 dermally isolated Nocardia strains (including 9 clinical isolates obtained in this study and 4 reference strains purchased from standard culture collections: N. brasiliensis NCTC11294, N. mexicana DSM 44952, N. transvalensis DSM 43582, N. vulneris W9851) using the Sensititre RAPMYCO microdilution panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The results were visualized using the R package ggplot2. The AST plate included 15 antibiotics: amikacin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, cefepime, cefoxitin, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, imipenem, linezolid, minocycline, moxifloxacin, tigecycline, tobramycin, TMP-SMX. The selection of these antimicrobial agents was based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline M24S for aerobic actinomycetes and supported by published clinical studies and treatment recommendations for nocardial infections, which highlight trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole as first-line therapy and recommend combination or alternative agents in severe or refractory cases.24,25 Quality control was performed using Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 as reference strains. The susceptibility results were interpreted according to the Nocardia spp.-specific breakpoints outlined in the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) supplement M24S.24 Accordingly, the AST panel was designed to cover major antimicrobial classes relevant to the clinical management of Nocardia infections, including folate pathway inhibitors, beta-lactams, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, and macrolides. To confirm the results for TMP-SMX, whose MIC endpoint is defined as 80% growth inhibition, an additional verification was performed using the MIC Test Strip (MTS) method (Liofilchem Ltd., Roseto Degli Abruzzi, TE, Italy).

Phylogenetic Tree Construction and Average Nucleotide Identity Analysis

To infer the phylogenetic relationships of human cutaneous Nocardia isolates, whole-genome sequences of 22 human cutaneous Nocardia isolates were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/). The inclusion criteria for the public genomes were as follows: (1) isolated from human hosts; (2) isolation source explicitly documented as skin, wound, abscess, or related cutaneous tissues; and (3) high-quality genome assemblies meeting the thresholds of >95% completeness and <5% contamination. Genomes lacking explicit metadata regarding the isolation source or host were excluded from the analysis. Single-copy core genes were identified using BLASTn, and a phylogenetic tree based on these genes was reconstructed using FastTree. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) (https://itol.embl.de/). Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) values among all genomes were calculated using pyani v0.2.1226 to assess overall genomic similarity, and were visualized using the R package pheatmap.

Pan-Genome Analysis

Pangenome analysis of cutaneous Nocardia isolates was performed using Roary v3.13.0,27 with Prokka-annotated GFF3 files as input, to characterize their gene composition and pangenome features. Gene clustering was carried out with BLASTp using a minimum protein sequence identity threshold of 95%, and genes present in at least 99% of strains were defined as core genes. A gene presence-absence heatmap was generated using the R package pheatmap, and a pangenome accumulation curve was constructed with ggplot2 to assess changes in the numbers of total, core, and accessory genes as the number of strains increased. Pangenome openness was evaluated according to Heap’s law, in which the parameter α indicates an open (α<1) or closed (α>1) pangenome.28,29

Virulence Factor and Antibiotic Resistance Gene Identification

Virulence factors were identified by performing a BLASTp alignment of all predicted protein sequences against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB), with cutoffs of ≥40% sequence identity and ≥70% query coverage. Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were predicted by aligning sequences against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) v6.0.2, with cutoffs of ≥60% sequence identity and ≥70% query coverage. The distribution of virulence factors and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) was visualized using Microsoft Excel 2019. To further investigate specific genotype-phenotype discordances, full-length sequences of candidate target genes (eg, gyrA) were manually extracted from the annotated genomes and aligned using BLASTn to identify potential resistance-associated mutations.

Results

The antimicrobial susceptibility of 13 cutaneous Nocardia isolates was evaluated against 15 antimicrobial agents (Figure 1 and Supplementary Table S1). Susceptibility to TMP-SMX was determined using the MTS method to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results (Supplementary Table S2). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed distinct susceptibility profiles among the 13 cutaneous Nocardia isolates. All isolates were susceptible to TMP-SMX and linezolid (13/13, 100%), whereas all isolates were resistant to clarithromycin (13/13, 100%). Among fluoroquinolones, ciprofloxacin showed limited activity, with a susceptibility rate of 38.5% (5/13). In contrast, moxifloxacin showed susceptibility in 76.9% (10/13) of isolates, with resistance restricted to N. cyriacigeorgica CDC322. Marked variability in susceptibility was observed among β-lactam antibiotics. The susceptibility rate to imipenem was 46.2% (6/13), while 4 isolates (30.8%) were resistant. Cephalosporins showed generally low activity, with susceptibility rates of 23.1% (3/13) for cefepime, 7.7% (1/13) for cefoxitin, and 46.2% (6/13) for ceftriaxone. By comparison, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid retained relatively good in vitro activity, with 69.2% (9/13) of isolates being susceptible. For tetracycline-class antibiotics, doxycycline and minocycline were characterized predominantly by intermediate susceptibility, with only N. brasiliensis CDC196 showing susceptibility to doxycycline (7.7%). Tigecycline demonstrated excellent in vitro activity, with 12 isolates (92.3%) classified as susceptible; resistance was observed only in N. farcinica CDC178. Notably, marked interspecies differences in antimicrobial susceptibility were observed. N. farcinica was fully susceptible to imipenem (100%), whereas N. brasiliensis was completely resistant (0%). In contrast, both species remained fully susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid.

Genomic Characteristics of Human Cutaneous Nocardia Isolates

This study analyzed 31 clinical cutaneous Nocardia isolates, including nine isolates sequenced in this study and 22 publicly available isolates retrieved from the NCBI database. The isolation metadata of the 31 isolates, including year, geographic location, patient sex, age, and body site, together with their genomic characteristics, including genome size, GC content, total gene count, and number of coding sequences (CDSs), are summarized in Table 1. The genome sizes of the analyzed isolates ranged from 6.26 to 10.03 Mb, with an average of 8.24 Mb. The GC content varied between 67.39% and 70.69%, averaging 68.68%. The total gene count ranged from 5812 to 9250, with a mean of 7577, while the number of CDSs ranged from 5748 to 9174, with a mean of 7504. Notably, Nocardia isolates of the same species exhibited highly consistent GC contents. For instance, all Nocardia farcinica isolates showed a consistently high GC content that clustered within an extremely narrow range of 70.66% to 70.69%. Phylogenetic Relationships and Average Nucleotide Identity Analysis

To further investigate the phylogenetic relationships and genomic relatedness of cutaneous Nocardia isolates, a genome-wide phylogenetic tree was constructed based on single-copy core genes identified from the whole-genome sequences of 31 isolates, and ANI analysis was performed among these isolates (Figure 2). Phylogenetic analysis divided the 31 Nocardia isolates into several distinct clades. Notably, isolates assigned to the same species consistently clustered together, indicating strong concordance between phylogenetic relationships and species classification. Consistent with the phylogenomic analysis, ANI analysis revealed clear species-level clustering in the heatmap, particularly among isolates of N. brasiliensis, N. farcinica, and N. terpenica, which displayed high within-species genomic similarity. The phylogenetic framework and ANI-based genomic relatedness established in this study provide an evolutionary basis for the comparative analysis of virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes. Notably, the unclassified strain Nocardia sp. NK_136 formed a distinct branch in the phylogenetic tree and remained clearly separated from all recognized species. Consistently, pairwise ANI values between Nocardia sp. NK_136 and its closest phylogenetic neighbors were all below the accepted species delimitation threshold of 95–96%.41 Together, these results indicate that Nocardia sp. NK_136 likely represents a putative novel Nocardia species associated with human cutaneous infection.

Pan-Genome Analysis of Human Cutaneous Nocardia Isolates

Pan-genome analysis of 31 cutaneously isolated Nocardia isolates identified a total of 118,253 gene clusters. Among these, only 59 genes (0.05%) were classified as core genes, present in 99–100% of the isolates, whereas 8 genes were identified as soft-core genes, present in at least 95% but less than 99% of the isolates. The vast majority of genes (118,186; 99.95%) constituted the accessory genome, including 12,146 shell genes (15% to <95% of isolates) and 106,040 cloud genes (<15% of isolates).

The gene presence–absence heatmap revealed substantial genomic heterogeneity among the Nocardia isolates (Figure 3A). Although a limited set of core genes was highly conserved, most genes showed a discontinuous and low-frequency distribution across the genomes. Notably, isolates that were phylogenetically closer tended to exhibit more similar gene distribution patterns. For example, N. brasiliensis, N. farcinica, and N. terpenica each formed clusters with relatively consistent gene presence signals, reflecting species-specific genomic features. Pangenome accumulation analysis further showed that the Nocardia isolates had an open pangenome (Figure 3B and C). As the number of genomes increased, the number of unique genes continued to rise, whereas the number of new genes introduced by each additional genome gradually decreased and approached zero (Figure 3B). At the same time, the total pangenome size continued to expand without reaching saturation, while the number of core genes stabilized at an early stage, supporting the presence of a stable core genome together with extensive accessory and strain-specific gene diversity (Figure 3C). Virulence Factor

To characterize potential virulence factors and assess the pathogenic potential of cutaneous Nocardia isolates, the virulence gene repertoire of 31 isolates was systematically profiled by BLASTp-based homology searches against the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB). A total of 619 putative virulence-associated genes were systematically identified and classified into nine functional categories: Adherence, Effector, Exoenzyme, Exotoxin, Immune, Motility, Nutritional/Metabolic, Regulation, Stress, and Others. Among these, virulence genes related to adherence (eg, dnaK, groEL), immune functions (eg, mps1, ndk, nuoG, ptpA), nutritional/metabolic processes (eg, carB, entE, fbpC, irtB, kasB), regulation (eg, devR/dosR, sigH), and stress response (eg, ahpC, clpC, hspX, katA, katG) were widely distributed across the analyzed isolates, with many of these genes detected in most genomes (Figure 4). The mbt genes involved in the core steps of mycobactin biosynthesis were highly conserved among the cutaneous Nocardia isolates. Specifically, mbtA, mbtB, mbtC, mbtE, mbtF, mbtG, mbtH, mbtI, and mbtN were detected in all 31 isolates (100%). In addition, mbtD and mbtM were each detected in 96.8% (30/31) of the isolates. In contrast, several mbt genes showed a more restricted distribution. For example, mbtJ was detected in 83.9% (26/31) of the isolates, whereas mbtK and mbtL were present at lower frequencies, occurring in only 32.3% (10/31) and 35.5% (11/31) of the isolates, respectively.

The mce family gene clusters were widely distributed among the 31 cutaneous Nocardia isolates, although their distribution patterns varied markedly across clusters (Supplementary Figure 1). Several clusters were highly conserved, particularly mce3, mce4, mce7, and mce8. Specifically, mce3B–F, mce4B–F, and mce8A–F were detected in all 31 isolates. In addition, mce4A was identified in 93.55% (29/31) of the isolates, further indicating that the mce4 cluster was highly conserved among these cutaneous isolates. By contrast, some mce genes displayed substantial distributional variability across isolates. For example, members of the mce2 cluster showed variable prevalence: mce2C was detected in 12 isolates (38.71%), predominantly in N. brasiliensis (8/12); mce2E in 22 isolates (70.97%), mainly in N. brasiliensis and N. farcinica; and mce2F in 17 isolates (54.84%), primarily in N. terpenica and N. farcinica. In addition, mce9-related genes were detected in only a small number of isolates and showed a scattered distribution pattern. Notably, mce1 and mce2 were identified exclusively in Nocardia cerradoensis CNM20130759.

The effector-associated genes eccC4, encoding a homolog of the core component of the Type VII secretion system; espR, a homolog of an ESX-associated transcriptional regulator; and mycP5, encoding a homolog of the mycosin protease involved in the stability and function of the ESX secretion apparatus, were detected in all isolates. In addition, the exoenzyme-associated gene eno, encoding enolase, and the motility-associated virulence factor flmH were also present in all isolates. In contrast, the plcC gene, which is associated with membrane damage, tissue invasion, and host–pathogen interactions, was detected in 22 of 31 isolates (71.0%) and was mainly distributed in N. brasiliensis and N. terpenica. Collectively, these findings suggest that cutaneous Nocardia isolates harbor a set of highly conserved core virulence-associated genes, together with several secretion- and toxin-related genes that exhibit variable distribution across isolates.

Antibiotic Resistance Genes

Antibiotic resistance gene analysis of the 31 cutaneous Nocardia isolates was conducted using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI v6.0.2) in conjunction with the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). A total of 40 resistance genes were identified, covering nine classes of antibacterial drugs: β-lactam antibiotics, aminoglycoside antibiotics, macrolide antibiotics, tetracycline antibiotics, peptide antibiotics, multi-antibiotics, rifampicin antibiotics, aminocoumarin antibiotics, and phosphonic acid antibiotics (Figure 5). The distribution of antibiotic resistance genes showed clear species specificity. The β-lactam resistance gene blaFAR-1 was mainly detected in N. farcinica (5/5), whereas blaAST-1 was absent from both N. farcinica and N. terpenica. Among peptide antibiotic resistance determinants, vanSO and vanRO were mainly found in N. brasiliensis, N. terpenica, and Nocardia cyriacigeorgica. The tetracycline resistance gene tetA/B(58) and the rifamycin resistance-related gene RbpA were widely distributed across Nocardia species. The rifamycin resistance determinant annotated as Nocardia farcinica rox (Rox-nf) was mainly detected in N. farcinica (5/5), whereas the determinant annotated as Streptomyces venezuelae rox (Rox-sv) was present in most N. brasiliensis isolates (6/8). The aminocoumarin resistance gene novA was detected in all tested isolates of N. brasiliensis and N. terpenica. The aminoglycoside resistance gene aph(3”)-Ia was identified in the novel Nocardia species and in Nocardia puris DSM 44599. In addition, the glycopeptide resistance gene vph was found only in N. puris DSM 44599. Notably, no sulfonamide resistance genes were detected in any of the isolates examined.

Furthermore, partial concordance between genotype and phenotype was observed. All 13 Nocardia isolates were susceptible to TMP-SMX consistent with the absence of sulfonamide resistance genes. Isolates showing intermediate resistance to tetracycline generally carried tetA/B(58). However, resistance-related phenotypes were also observed in several isolates without an obvious corresponding genetic determinant. To further investigate this genotype–phenotype discordance, supplementary candidate-mechanism screening was performed for tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, and clarithromycin. Among the six tobramycin-resistant isolates, aph(3”)-Ia was detected only in Nocardia sp. NK-136, suggesting that known aminoglycoside-NK_136associated determinants explain only part of the observed tobramycin resistance. For ciprofloxacin, additional screening of candidate gyrA and gyrB fragments showed that variation across all isolates largely reflected interspecies divergence. More importantly, no obvious candidate difference was identified when the ciprofloxacin-intermediate N. farcinica CDC178 was compared with two susceptible isolates of the same species (N. farcinica CDC173 and N. farcinica CDC359). For clarithromycin, all 13 tested isolates were resistant, yet no clear corresponding resistance determinant was identified; exploratory comparison of the rrl fragment likewise failed to reveal an obvious shared difference that could consistently explain the uniformly resistant phenotype. In addition, genomic analysis identified rifampicin resistance-associated genes which, although not assessed phenotypically in this study, suggest the presence of potential resistance mechanisms in Nocardia that remain to be experimentally validated. The underlying molecular basis and functional significance of these findings will require further investigation through more systematic phenotypic testing and targeted functional studies.

Discussion

This study included 31 clinical cutaneous Nocardia isolates, comprising 22 isolates retrieved from the NCBI database and 9 isolates collected in this study. Through integrated phylogenetic analysis, average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis, and profiling of virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes, we systematically characterized the phylogenetic relationships and genomic features of cutaneous Nocardia isolates. In addition, we preliminarily explored the potential mechanisms underlying the occurrence of cutaneous nocardiosis in immunocompetent hosts and its relatively favorable prognosis.

The clinical cutaneous isolates in this study mainly belonged to three species: N. brasiliensis (25.81%, 8/31), N. farcinica (16.13%, 5/31), and N. terpenica (12.90%, 4/31). Together, these three species accounted for 54.84% of all isolates. Furthermore, large-scale studies on clinical Nocardia isolates from New Zealand42 and the United States43 have identified Nocardia nova, N. cyriacigeorgica, and N. farcinica as the predominant species. In contrast, two major studies from China17,44 reported N. farcinica, N. cyriacigeorgica, Nocardia abscess, and Nocardia otitidiscaviarum as the most prevalent species. In the present study, N. brasiliensis accounted for the largest proportion among cutaneous Nocardia isolates; previous reports have likewise shown that this species is one of the most common causative agents of primary cutaneous nocardiosis.45–47

This study showed that cutaneous Nocardia isolates generally exhibited relatively large genomes and high GC content, with interspecies differences in genome size, total gene number, and CDS count, similar to the genomic patterns described in earlier studies of Nocardia.48,49 In addition, all N. farcinica isolates in this study showed a narrowly restricted GC content range of 70.66%–70.69%, in agreement with previous complete genome reports, suggesting that GC content is relatively conserved within this species.34,50 The whole-genome phylogenetic tree based on single-copy core genes was broadly consistent with the ANI results, with isolates from the same species forming clear clusters. This finding suggests that the phylogenetic relationships of cutaneous Nocardia isolates at the genomic level are largely consistent with current species classification and also indicates that whole-genome analysis provides higher resolution than conventional single-gene identification.15,41,51 Notably, Nocardia sp. NK-136 exhibited marked genomic distinctiveness among the cutaneous isolates analyzed in this study. In addition to its relatively large genome (9.71 Mb), it also displayed aNK_136 unique resistome profile, including the aminoglycoside resistance gene aph(3”)-Ia, which was not broadly shared by other major cutaneous clades. Further comparative genomic and phenotypic studies are needed to clarify its taxonomic status and pathogenic characteristics. In addition, pan-genome analysis showed that cutaneous Nocardia isolates retained a relatively stable core genome while still exhibiting considerable accessory gene diversity and genomic plasticity.15,48

Unlike pulmonary or disseminated nocardiosis, which is more commonly observed in hosts with impaired cell-mediated immunity, primary cutaneous nocardiosis can occur in immunocompetent individuals.6 It is usually associated with direct inoculation after trauma, as well as gardening or agricultural exposure.52–54 Previous studies suggest that cutaneous nocardiosis is frequently associated with traumatic percutaneous inoculation, indicating that disruption of the local skin barrier may contribute to disease onset; meanwhile, Nocardia exhibits a degree of antiphagocytic and immune-evasive capacity.55 In addition, some patients previously classified as immunocompetent had not undergone systematic immunological or genetic evaluation, and therefore a potential underlying host susceptibility cannot be fully excluded.3 A total of 619 putative virulence genes were identified in this study, including known Nocardia virulence-associated factors such as catalases, superoxide dismutases (SODs), secreted toxins, and cell wall proteins.56,57 eccC4, espR, and mycP5 were conserved in all cutaneous isolates examined in this study. Although direct evidence for their functional roles in Nocardia remains limited, these genes are associated with the ESX/type VII secretion system and host–pathogen interactions in mycobacteria. Their uniform conservation among cutaneous isolates may indicate a conserved genetic framework potentially associated with host adaptation during the establishment of cutaneous infection.2,58 Notably, genes related to stress response and antioxidant defense, including the catalase genes katA and katG and the superoxide dismutase genes sodA, sodB, and sodC, were widely distributed among the isolates. Catalases and SODs are thought to counteract phagocyte-mediated oxidative killing,56 suggesting that cutaneous Nocardia isolates may commonly possess the ability to resist host oxidative stress and enhance survival within phagocytes. Previous studies have shown that Nocardia can persist within host cells and resist innate immune clearance through antioxidant enzyme systems and immune evasion-related mechanisms, which may provide an important basis for chronic localized cutaneous infection even in immunocompetent hosts.59,60 Overall, the cutaneous isolates analyzed here consistently retained core factors involved in oxidative stress resistance, intracellular survival, and host interaction, suggesting a shared genetic basis potentially contributing to persistence in local skin tissue.2,56 Whether these cutaneous isolates differ systematically from pulmonary or disseminated isolates in determinants related to invasive potential, however, remains to be clarified by further comparative genomic studies.

Previous studies in Nocardia have shown that Mce proteins are involved in host cell adhesion, invasion, and intracellular survival within macrophages.61 In particular, mce1E, mce1C, and mce1D in N. farcinica have been experimentally shown to promote host cell entry and attenuate innate immune responses through modulation of the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways.60,61 In addition, another study showed that N. brasiliensis isolates lacking part of the mce-associated region exhibited markedly reduced, or even abolished, virulence in BALB/c mice.62 This study showed that most isolates carried mce4, mce7, and mce8, whereas mce1 and mce2 were detected only in N. cerradoensis CNM20130759. In addition, all isolates carried mce4F, and all 31 isolates also harbored mce3C. Previous studies have shown that mce4F is critical for the virulence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis,63 whereas mce3C promotes macrophage adhesion and invasion through a β2 integrin-dependent pathway.64 A whole-genome study of ocular Nocardia isolates also suggested that mce3C and mce4F may contribute to the pathogenesis of nocardial keratitis and that mce family distribution is associated with lesion severity.65 The high conservation of mce4, mce7, and mce8 in this study may suggest a role in host adaptation or infection-related processes in cutaneous isolates, although their precise functions require further experimental validation. While previous studies have primarily highlighted the role of these virulence factors in pulmonary or disseminated nocardiosis, their high conservation in our cutaneous isolates may be associated with the establishment of primary cutaneous infections, particularly in immunocompetent hosts. Cutaneous nocardiosis frequently follows traumatic inoculation, introducing the pathogen directly into the local skin microenvironment.52–54 Upon entry, Nocardia encounters an immediate barrage of reactive oxygen species (ROS) from recruited local phagocytes, such as dermal macrophages and neutrophils.59 The ubiquitous presence of robust antioxidant defense systems, including catalases (katA, katG) and superoxide dismutases (sodA, sodB, sodC), likely enables these isolates to withstand this initial localized oxidative burst.56,57 Concurrently, the highly conserved mce operons (particularly mce4, mce7, and mce8) may facilitate the adhesion and invasion of local resident cells, allowing the bacteria to establish an intracellular niche.60,61 This synergy of resisting localized immune clearance and exploiting intracellular survival mechanisms may be critical for Nocardia to establish chronic, localized cutaneous lesions, preventing immediate eradication by a healthy immune system while not necessarily possessing the broader hypervirulence required for systemic dissemination.

All isolates carried the nitrosative stress-related genes narG and narH, as well as the immune evasion-related genes ptpA and ndk. In addition, plcC was detected at a relatively high frequency and was mainly distributed in N. brasiliensis and N. terpenica. Previous studies have shown that phospholipase C proteins are involved in membrane damage, tissue invasion, and host cell injury in various bacteria and may represent an important virulence factor in N. brasiliensis.66 However, the virulence role of phospholipase C has mainly been demonstrated in other microorganisms, and its specific role in Nocardia remains to be clarified.67

This study showed that the distribution of resistance genes was markedly species-specific. The β-lactam resistance gene blaFAR-1 was mainly detected in N. farcinica and was associated with resistance to some cephalosporins, in line with previous genomic reports.17 Notably, N. farcinica has been associated with a higher risk of mortality.68 Therefore, even when recovered from cutaneous infection, its potential invasiveness and resistance risk warrant attention. For tetracycline- and rifamycin-related resistance determinants, tetA/B(58) and RbpA were widely detected in all isolates. A large genomic study of clinical Nocardia likewise identified tetA/B(58) and RbpA as conserved core resistance-associated genes.17 In addition, rox was more frequently detected in N. farcinica, whereas a rox-sv-like determinant was more common in N. brasiliensis. Previous studies have shown that although rox encodes a rifampin monooxygenase involved in rifampin inactivation, rox deletion alone does not markedly reduce rifampin resistance in N. farcinica, suggesting that rpoB2 may play a more dominant role and that rifamycin resistance in Nocardia is likely multifactorial.69,70 Because phenotypic testing for rifamycins was not included in this study, the actual contribution of these genes in cutaneous isolates still requires further evaluation by MIC testing and transcriptional or functional assays. Notably, no sulfonamide resistance genes were detected in any isolate in this study, and all 13 tested isolates were susceptible to TMP-SMX, showing good concordance between genotype and phenotype. TMP-SMX has long been the first-line treatment for nocardiosis,25 and large susceptibility studies have shown that it remains active against most Nocardia isolates, although susceptibility may vary across species.71,72 Therefore, the absence of sulfonamide resistance determinants together with the observed phenotypic susceptibility suggests that TMP-SMX remains of important therapeutic value for cutaneous Nocardia infection. Linezolid showed good in vitro activity against all tested isolates, consistent with previous large susceptibility studies, further supporting its role as an important alternative when TMP-SMX is not tolerated, treatment fails, or combination therapy is required.72,73 However, its long-term use is limited by adverse effects such as myelosuppression and peripheral neuropathy, and it should therefore be used cautiously in localized cutaneous infection according to disease severity and treatment duration.74 In contrast, clarithromycin showed resistance in all tested isolates in this study. Recent susceptibility studies have likewise reported high rates of clarithromycin resistance in Nocardia, with marked variation across species.75,76

In this study, known acquired resistance determinants and the candidate target regions screened here accounted for only part of the resistance phenotypes observed for tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, and clarithromycin. A recent genomic-phenotypic study of Nocardia likewise showed that some resistance phenotypes cannot be fully explained by currently recognized determinants.17 This inconsistency may reflect multiple factors, including complex gene expression regulation, gene-inactivating mutations that silence resistance determinants, uncharacterized resistance mechanisms such as efflux or target modifications outside the sequenced regions, and limitations in current database annotations.77,78 Previous work has linked fluoroquinolone resistance to alterations in DNA gyrase targets;79 however, no obvious candidate difference was identified in the gyrA or gyrB fragments examined here, suggesting that the screened regions were insufficient to explain the ciprofloxacin non-susceptible phenotype observed in this study. For clarithromycin, macrolide resistance has been associated with changes in 23S rRNA,80,81 yet comparison of representative rrl fragments in the present study did not yield a clear signal, and the underlying molecular basis therefore remains unresolved. In addition, the relatively small number of isolates subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing (n=13) may limit the robustness of genotype–phenotype correlation analyses.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of cutaneous Nocardia isolates included was relatively limited, and some isolates retrieved from the NCBI database lacked complete and well-defined clinical source information, which may have affected the representativeness and accuracy of the results. Future studies should include a larger number of isolates with clearer origins, more complete clinical metadata, and a more balanced species composition to further validate the robustness of these findings. Second, the analysis of virulence and resistance genes was primarily based on genomic data, whereas only a limited number of isolates underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing, precluding a full assessment of genotype-phenotype correlations. Moreover, this study exclusively focused on cutaneous isolates and did not include strains derived from pulmonary or disseminated nocardiosis as comparative controls, which limits the ability to determine whether the identified conserved virulence factors are specific to cutaneous infection or represent core virulence determinants widely shared across the genus Nocardia. In addition, the specific pathogenic roles of these virulence factors and their interactions with the host remain to be functionally validated. Further studies integrating transcriptomics, molecular experiments, and infection models are needed to better define their biological functions and the genetic basis of resistance. Finally, this study did not systematically integrate genomic features with clinical presentation, treatment, or outcome, and the clinical relevance of some virulence and resistance traits therefore remains unclear. Future genome-phenotype-outcome association analyses with more complete clinical data may improve the clinical interpretability and translational value of these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically characterized the evolutionary relationships and genomic features of cutaneous Nocardia through single-copy core gene phylogeny, average nucleotide identity, and pan-genome analyses. Based on VFDB and CARD annotations, we further defined the profiles of putative virulence-associated and resistance genes and revealed their interspecies distribution patterns among human cutaneous isolates. These results provide new genomic clues for understanding why cutaneous nocardiosis can occur in immunocompetent hosts and is generally associated with a favorable prognosis, although the precise functions of the candidate genes identified here still require further investigation.
