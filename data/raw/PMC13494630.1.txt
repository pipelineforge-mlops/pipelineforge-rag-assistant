==============================
Advances in T cell-based immunotherapy for cancer treatment, coupled with breakthroughs in artificial intelligence (AI), have led to a surge in AI-driven analysis and prediction algorithms. However, significant challenges remain in translating these computational methods into clinical practice. This paper reviews the technical trends in AI-based approaches for T cell-based immunotherapy, identifies key limitations and potentials for improvement, and proposes future research directions to bridge the gap between computational methods and clinical implementation.

1 Introduction

In immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been considered as a standard of care across multiple solid tumors, acting on inhibitory pathways directly linked to T cell reinvigoration. Clinically, the approved ICIs have been categorized as CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1 inhibitors; and more recently, the LAG-3-blocking antibody relatlimab in fixed-dose combination with nivolumab1 received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in unresectable and metastatic melanoma, further expanding this class.2 In addition, as the uptake of ICIs has risen sharply for non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ICIs seem to demonstrate therapeutic efficacy.3 However, as ICIs primarily restore pre-existing tumor-reactive T cell function, thus efficacy often depends on restrictive biomarkers that are suggestive of responsiveness (e.g., hot tumor microenvironment (TME), high PD-L1 expression).

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT), including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) therapy, and T cell receptor-engineered T cells (TCR-T), enhances antitumor immunity by supplying defined, high-avidity effector T cells. CAR-T cells are ex vivo engineered autologous T cells that bind to tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) on the surface of the target. This method has demonstrated examples in treating hematological malignancies (e.g., B-cell lymphoma,4 B-acute lymphoblastic leukemia,5 multiple myeloma6,7) but do not target intracellular neoantigens.8

For TIL therapy, TIL refer to lymphocytes, including T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, located within the tumor or the TME. The potential of TIL therapy was first demonstrated in the late 1980s by Rosenberg et al.,9 who reported significant efficacy in treating metastatic melanoma using TIL therapy in combination with IL-2. More recently, Rohaan et al.10 have further elucidated the superiority of TIL therapy in cancer treatment. In 2024, AMTAGVI(lifileucel)11 received FDA accelerated approval for adult patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma, marking a significant milestone in TIL therapy. Specifically, AMTAGVI has shown activity after anti-PD-1 failure in metastatic melanoma. However, TIL therapy has challenges due to the required time consumption in harvesting and expanding the lymphocytes population.12

TCR-T therapy employs T cells engineered with TCRs that recognize peptide-human leukocyte antigen (pHLA) complexes, to enable targeting both exogenous and endogenous antigens.13 Recently, TECELRA(afamitresgene autoleucel)14 received FDA accelerated approval for adults with unresectable or metastatic synovial sarcoma who have received prior chemotherapy, are HLA-A02:01P, -A02:02P, -A02:03P, or -A02:06P positive, and whose tumor expresses the MAGE-A4. It is the first regulatory approval of a TCR-T therapy for a solid tumor, providing proof-of-concept for TCR-T therapy in solid tumors. In addition, this highlights the clinical significance of TCR-T for ICI-poor indications.

While these suggest advantages over ICIs and related research are growing, ACT development requires substantial time and resources. In response, studies are increasingly applying artificial intelligence (AI) to accelerate development.15,16 For example, KSH01/KSX01 TCR-T products17 (NCT05539833, NCT05811975, NCT06150365) from TCRx Therapeutics are designed using the TCR-XFinder AI platform. These have first entered phase I trials for advanced solid tumors as AI-designed TCR-T and show promising initial safety and efficacy results.18 In addition, TCRact19 has used AI to optimize high-throughput TCR-pHLA pairing, enabling antigen-agnostic identification of tumor-reactive T cells, while formal registration for clinical trials is pending, observational protocols are in preparation. MixTRTpred,20 developed by Ludwig Cancer Research, ranks high-avidity TIL TCRs using deep learning and has demonstrated strong preclinical results for patient-specific TCR selection.

While most work remains pre-clinical or at the early-clinical (phase I) stage, these platforms exemplify how AI is driving the transition from laboratory discovery to personalized therapies. If AI can improve the proportion of genuinely effective drug candidates among the top N predicted combinations, it would reduce the number of repetitive experiments, enable reallocation of specialized personnel, and ultimately enhance verification and safety processes with greater efficiency.

This review aims to identify the technical factors that critically influence the practicality and performance of recently developed AI-based algorithms for TCR-T therapy. Accordingly, it summarizes the performance comparison results based on benchmark results presented in the original papers for each algorithm, discusses their fundamental limitations, and review ongoing efforts to overcome these challenges.

2 Review

2.1 Recent comprehensive review

There have been comprehensive reviews about applying AI to TCR-T therapy. These include discussions on the essential components and directions for improvement needed to predict T cell specificity using ML-based algorithms,21 an introduction to the overall workflow and AI-based algorithms for applying AI to personalized immunotherapy,22 a comprehensive review of various attempts to apply AI to immunotherapy and their limitations,23 a comprehensive review of the origins of neoantigens and their use in immunotherapy,24 and an introduction to neoantigen prediction and TCR-epitope binding prediction tools used in TCR-T therapy, as well as workflows for clinical applications and a comprehensive review of clinical trials.25

In this technical review, the remaining parts are organized as follows. The remaining parts of Section 2 are examinations of the elements that constitute the latest AI-based technologies and describe the research stages of TCR-T therapy that new AI technologies are primarily being applied to. Section 3 analyzes the limitations of recent studies based on the findings from the analysis. Section 4 introduces various research efforts aimed at overcoming the limitations. Finally, Section 5 summarizes this review and concludes with future research expectations.

2.2 AI models

Recent AI-driven research trends are centered around keywords such as transformers,26, 27, 28, 29, 30 graph neural networks (GNN),31, 32, 33, 34 transfer learning,18,35,36 multi-task learning,37,38 and multi-modal AI.39

Based on model architecture, transformers40 enable improved data efficiency through self-supervised learning, and their self-attention mechanism allows for more effective predictions by considering not only local patterns but also the global context of the data.41,42 GNN is a representative network architecture capable of directly processing graph-structured biological data.43 The level of effectiveness that a GNN system may have in interpreting complex physicochemical interactions between molecules as structural relationships is still being investigated in recent research.44,45

Among AI learning methods, transfer learning46 is an approach that aims to achieve high performance quickly and efficiently by reusing the patterns and features learned by a pre-trained model for a new problem. The advantage of this method is that it can achieve a high performance even with limited data and computational resources. Multi-task learning is a type of transfer learning that simultaneously trains on multiple related tasks, allowing useful information to be shared and enables to achieve a better generalized performance than single-task learning. This approach also has the advantage of improving the performance in environments with limited data. Multi-modal learning47 involves learning from different types of data simultaneously and understanding the interactions between them, which allows for the utilization of contextual information to more accurately grasp complex situations and make more sophisticated predictions. In context of the complex immune system, multi-modal AI can enable more accurate predictions of neoantigens and immune responses. For example, there have been developed integrated pipelines with the goal of utilizing contextual information obtained from multi-modal data.48,49 These approaches predict immunogenic neoantigens and immune responses by integrating features acquired from existing single-stage prediction algorithms.

However, despite the proliferation of algorithms that utilize similar model architectures, learning methods, and datasets, there remains a lack of comprehensive technical review that systemically compares and analyzes the effectiveness of various model architectures, learning methods, and evaluation metrics. Therefore, it is crucial to explore system analyses to find AI applicable points, and comparative analyses, to determine which approaches are most effective and to identify promising directions for future research.

2.3 Evaluation metrics

Most studies use diverse performance metrics to capture different aspects of model effectiveness and must be interpreted in context. In candidate screening pipelines (e.g., TCR-T therapy peptide or TCR selection), precision (also known as positive predictive value, PPV) is crucial when false positives impose high experimental costs. For binding prediction task where missing a true signal is unacceptable, recall (which is equivalent to sensitivity and true positive rate, TPR) is essential. Although accuracy is intuitive, it can be misleading in imbalanced datasets; a classifier that predicts all negatives may achieve high accuracy while offering no clinical utility. F1-score balances precision and recall but may be less informative collapsing distinct error costs into a single number. In clinical prioritization with limited wet-lab capacity, rank-focused metrics such as PPV@N or recall@N are often more decision-aligned. In addition, metrics addressing class imbalance, including Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), provide more holistic assessment but are harder to interpret and unstable. The area under the curve (AUC), based on false positive rate (FPR) and specificity, remains widely used but can overestimate performance for rare positives, making area under the precision-recall (AUPR) a more meaningful metric in highly imbalanced datasets.

These performance metrics are based on the confusion matrix parameters of true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), false negative (FN), false positive rate (FPR), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which are defined in (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), (6), (7).(1) Precision=TP/(TP+FP)

(2) Recall=TP/(TP+FN)

(3) FPR=FP/(FP+TN)

(4) Specificity=TN/(TN+FP)

(5) Accuracy=(TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN)

(6) F1=TP/{TP+(FP+FN)/2}=2×(Precision×Recall)/(Precision+Recall)

(7) MCC=(TP×TN−FP×FN)/(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)

These parameters highlight complementary trade-offs, and careful selection is necessary to align evaluation with practical constraints.

2.4 System analysis

Before applying AI in TCR-T therapy, understanding the overall flow of in-vivo immune response is helpful. The entire T cell-based immune response process can be divided into five stages; antigen processing and presentation, T cell priming and initial clonal expansion, trafficking and infiltration, target engagement and effector function, and regulation and exhaustion.50, 51, 52, 53

The antigen processing and presentation stage can be separated into the HLA-I and HLA-II pathways.54 In the HLA-I pathway, peptides are processed mainly from endogenous proteins by the proteasome/immunoproteasome, which typically establishes the C-termini of antigenic peptides. The peptides are then transported into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) by the transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP).55 The endoplasmic reticulum aminopeptidase 1/2 (ERAP 1/2) trim N-termini to optimal length, and with the assistance of the peptide-loading complex (PLC) (e.g., tapasin, calreticulin, ERp57), peptides are loaded onto HLA-I and transported to the cell surface.56 Meanwhile, in cross-presentation, exogenous antigens are either routed through a cytosolic pathway, escaping endosomes to undergo proteasomal processing followed by TAP/ERAP-dependent ER loading, or processed and loaded within endosomal/vacuolar compartments, where insulin-regulated aminopeptidase (IRAP) contributes to N-terminal trimming.57 In the HLA-II pathway, exogenous antigens are predominantly degraded by endosomal/lysosomal cathepsins.58,59 The invariant chain bound to HLA-II (e.g., HLA-DR, HLA-DQ, and HLA-DP)60 in the ER is partially degraded, leaving class II-associated invariant chain peptide (CLIP). HLA-DM then removes CLIP and performs peptide editing to replace with higher-affinity peptides, while HLA-DO fine-tunes HLA-DM activity.61,62 As illustrated in Fig. 1, these pathways are intrinsically complex and obtaining observable data across the detailed steps of antigen processing remain limited in practice.Fig. 1 Antigen presentation pathway of HLA class I and HLA class II within the TME. Extracellular proteins can also be processed in endosomes or IRAP before HLA class I loading. In the T cell priming and initial clonal expansion stage, naïve T cells encounter professional APCs (e.g., cDC1, cDC2) within secondary lymphoid organs. The cDC1 supports both HLA-I and HLA-II antigen presentation but is specialized for HLA-I cross-presentation to prime CD8⁺ T cells, whereas cDC2 is relatively specialized for HLA-II mediated CD4⁺ T priming. The APCs deliver three signals; TCR-pHLA binding,63 co-stimulation (CD-28-B7 for initiation, 4-1BB/OX40 for survival/persistence),64 and cytokines (e.g., IL-12, type I IFN, IL-2) that imprint effector and memory fates.65 Integrating these signals triggers proximal TCR signaling, metabolic reprogramming, and entry into cell cycle for initial clonal expansion. In TCR-T, much of this priming and initial clonal expansion is done ex vivo.

In the trafficking and infiltration stage, activated T cells are recruited to tumors under the vascular adhesion programs and chemokine axes. For example, in solid tumors, CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 and CCL5-CCR5 are well known recruitment pathways.66,67 However, redundant or compensatory routes vary by organ and disease context, so the essentiality of any single receptor is context dependent.68 In addition, within the TME, interstitial pressure, aberrant vasculature, TGF-β signaling, and fibrotic barriers act as physical and immunologic obstacles that hinder infiltration.69,70

In the target engagement and effector function stage, pHLA complexes are re-engaged by TCRs, to form an immunological synapse, which triggers proximal signaling and activates transcriptional programs that upregulate cytokines and cytotoxic machinery. The CD8⁺ T cells execute cytolysis predominantly via granule exocytosis71,72 and death receptor pathway (e.g., caspase cascades by Fas-FasL engagement).72 The CD4⁺ T cells amplify antitumor immunity by secreting cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α), enhancing antigen presentation, promoting vascular normalization, and driving M1 polarization.73 Recently, pyroptosis74 and ferroptosis75 have been implicated in tumor cell killing, broadening the effector repertoire.

In the regulation and exhaustion stage, immunosuppression arises from regulatory T cells (Tregs)76 and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs),77 PD-L1 on tumor/stromal cells,78,79 and adenosine80 and lactate-driven81 metabolic inhibition. Chronic antigen exposure then enforces T cell exhaustion, characterized by functional decline, impaired proliferation, metabolic rewiring, and persistent inhibitory receptor upregulation.82,83

In patients, this cascade is frequently incomplete, culminating in weak activation, expansion, or dominant exhaustion within the TME. TCR-T therapy addresses this by defining and expanding antigen-specific T cells ex vivo and reinfusing them to raise precursor frequency and antigen recognition competence. Although TCR-T does not inherently block suppressive checkpoints, its activity is augmented by lymphodepleting preconditioning, which creates a cytokine environment (e.g. IL-7 and IL-15).84 In addition, combination strategies (e.g., cytokine support,85 checkpoint blockade86) can be used to mitigate regulatory pressure. In this way, TCR-T partially compensates for the incomplete cascade, while antigen/HLA down-modulation and a hostile TME remain key challenges for durable responses.

2.5 Protocol of TCR-T therapy with AI

With the understanding of the T cell-based immune response process, Fig. 2 links the entire workflow for developing TCR-T therapy. The data collection stage with peptide processing along the HLA-I and HLA-II pathways clarifies that patient material is obtained by biopsy from TME (yielding tumor cells, and intratumoral/peritumoral APCs such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells) and by blood draw for peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). TILs isolated from tumor tissue are used for TCR sequencing. Data collection thus encompasses detecting somatic mutations, inferring the sequences of mutated proteins based on mutation calling, analyzing peptide-generation patterns to derive candidate peptides, identifying the individual’s HLA types, and finally predicting whether the peptide will bind to the HLA molecule. Mutations detection87 and HLA typing88 are typically performed based on whole-exome/genome sequencing (WES/WGS), or RNA- sequencing.89 Mutated protein sequence inference is generally conducted using tools such as ANNOVAR90 or variant effect predictor,91 which reconstruct protein sequences; recent studies have also utilized Alphafold92 to consider isoforms of the same sequence.93 For peptide candidate generation, in addition to experimentally validated peptides obtained by methods such as MS, AI-based analysis of peptide generation patterns, including proteasome cleavage prediction,94 is still being studied.95, 96, 97, 98 In parallel, RNA-seq from tumor/APC samples supports transcript expression checks and detection of fusion/splice events, and copy-number analysis can identify HLA-LOH to contextualize HLA typing. 2.5.1 Antigen processing

In the HLA-I pathway, prior to peptide-HLA binding and surface presentation, AI has been applied in several areas, including proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport, and ER trimming. Proteasomal cleavage aims to predict where the N-terminal or C-terminal ends of epitopes are generated from antigenic proteins, and there have been many AI algorithms developed for cleavage site prediction, including Pepsickle,100 PUUPL,101 and NetCleave.102 Pepsickle is an ensemble deep learning algorithm, trained on in-vitro proteasome digestion data from 35 studies, including constitutive and immunoproteasome, and in-vivo HLA-I complex data. It uses both amino acids identity and physicochemical property encodings, achieving an AUC of 0.82-0.88. However, due to label noise, the C-terminus of HLA-I ligands is often used as a proxy for proteasome cleavage sites, but this may not reflect the true cleavage position and can limit the prediction accuracy. In addition, the lack of large-scale immunoproteasome data and reliance on synthetic negative examples restricts model generalizability. PUUPL is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) based deep learning model trained on large scale HLA-I ligand datasets. It addresses the lack of true negative data in HLA ligandomics using positive unlabeled learning to iteratively pseudo-label negatives based on model confidence. Although it achieves a high AUC of 0.87 on C-terminal cleavage sites, it is limited by the absence of experimentally confirmed negatives. NetCleave is an MLP-based neural network algorithm designed to predict C-terminal antigen processing for both HLA-I and HLA-II pathways. It is trained on MS-identified eluted peptides from the IEDB,103 using residues around assumed cleavage sites encoded with physicochemical amino acids descriptors. NetCleave achieved high accuracy for HLA-I based on an AUC of 0.91. However, it still has challenges with synthetic non-cleavage sites and modeling biological complexity of antigen processing. In addition, Ziegler et al. in 202395 showed that, even with state-of-the-art neural architectures, there is little performance difference compared to traditional methods.

For TAP transport, there are TAPPred104 and DeepTAP.105 TAPPred is a support vector machine (SVM) based peptide-TAP binding affinity prediction model, trained on experimentally validated TAP-binding and non-binding peptides from public databases (e.g., JenPep,106 AntiJen,107 MHCBN108). It achieved an AUC of 0.78-0.82 showing a robust performance for small datasets, and therefore is widely used as a baseline. DeepTAP is a recurrent neural network (RNN)109 based peptide prediction which binds to TAP, trained on curated TAP-binding and non-binding peptides datasets, including public databases (e.g., JenPep, AntiJen, MHCBN). It achieved an AUC of 0.95 outperforming TAPPred.

For ER trimming, there is no AI algorithm, but only the position weight matrix-based ERAMER,110 which scores peptides using experimentally derived residue preferences at each position for ERAP1 trimming likelihood. On the one hand, MHCflurry 2.0 AP111 is a neural network-based allele-independent antigen processing predictor trained on MS-identified HLA-I, developed for modeling proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport, and ER trimming that influence which peptides are available for peptide-HLA binding, beyond binding affinity alone. The model shows a mean AUC of 0.85-0.86 on held-out multiallelic MS benchmarks, although trained on monoallelic MS datasets.

In the HLA-II pathway, AI applications prior to peptide-HLA binding or presentation are still limited, without NetCleave. NetCleave, which covers C-terminal processing by cathepsins in HLA-II pathway, has shown a relatively low AUC of 0.66.

2.5.2 Peptide-HLA binding prediction

Peptide-HLA binding prediction aims to predict which HLA a peptide (presumed to be generated from a neoantigen) will bind to. Table 1 includes benchmark results from recent papers proposing state-of-the-art models. GraphMHC112 is a model that combines a GNN with a convolutional neural network (CNN), utilizing molecular structural information to classify whether a peptide binds to major histocompatibility (MHC). For both MHC class I and II, benchmark tests were performed using NetMHCpan-4.1 and NetMHCpanII-4.0113 with IEDB data. Based on these results, GraphMHC tends to predict many peptide-MHC combinations as positive in order not to miss true binders, but this also means that it predicts non-binding combinations as positive. TranspMHC28 is a transformer-based model. When benchmarked using NetMHCpan-4.0,114 ACME,115 BigMHC,116 pMTnet,117 CapsNet-MHC,118 TransPHLA,119 and STMHCpan120 with IEDB data, TranspMHC achieved the highest performance in AUC, AUPR, balanced accuracy, F1-score, and MCC, except for accuracy. Various architectures such as CNN, attention mechanism, capsule network, and transformer were compared, and transformer-based models generally showed a superior performance. In particular, the use of AUPR, balanced accuracy, and MCC as performance metrics allowed for more reliable evaluation under data imbalance. However, since models like NetMHCpan, CapsNet-MHC, and STMHCpan could not adapt to peptides of certain lengths, it cannot be definitively concluded that TranspMHC is the best performer.

TransHLA30 is a model that utilizes features obtained from both transformers and CNN. For the HLA-I case, it was compared to MHCflurry,111 MHCnuggets,121 TransPHLA,119 Anthem,122 NetMHCpan-4.1,113 and MixMHCpred.123 For HLA-II case, it was compared to DeepSeqPanII,124 MHCnuggets, NetMHCIIpan-4.3,113 and MixMHC2pred.125 Data from CEDAR,126 VDJdb,127 ImmuneCode,128 and dbPepNeo2.0129 are used for benchmarks. TransHLA showed a decent performance in accuracy, F1-score, MCC, precision, and specificity, but had a lower recall. This suggests that it may misclassify true peptide-HLA binders as negative. This could be due to insufficient representation of positive cases in the training data or a lack of data diversity, leading to poor learning of patterns involved in peptide-HLA binding.

2.5.3 TCR-pHLA binding prediction

TruNeo48 is an integrated pipeline that combines existing algorithms, especially NetMHCpan-3.0141 for peptide-HLA binding prediction. It identifies high-confidence neoantigens by considering peptide-MHC class I binding affinity, proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport efficiency, expression abundance, tumor heterogeneity, clonality, and HLA-LOH. Benchmark datasets are collected from published literature.142, 143, 144 The performance of TruNeo is compared with NetMHCpan-4.0,114 MHCflurry,145 PSSMHCpan,146 DeepHLA,147 and EDGE.148 Additionally, the performance of TruNeo was compared to MHCflurry using WES and RNA-seq data from an actual small cell lung cancer patient. Notably, when comparing TruNeo to the use of only the NetMHCpan-4.0 algorithm, TruNeo achieved higher recall, and in real lung cancer patient data, it also significantly outperformed the MHCflurry algorithm. This demonstrates that TruNeo is more effective at identifying neoantigens with true immunogenicity than single-step prediction methods.

BigMHC116 is an ensemble model composed of seven pan-allelic deep neural networks. It is designed to predict both peptide binding to MHC class I molecules and immunogenicity. Since experimentally obtaining pHLA data recognized by T cells is very difficult and the amount of accumulated data is limited, BigMHC addresses this by first pre-training on large-scale MS-based MHC class I eluted ligand data and then conducts fine-tuning on a small set of immunogenic data using a transfer learning approach. This strategy is significant in that it enables the model to learn immunogenic features associated with T cell recognition during training. In benchmark tests, BigMHC demonstrated a significantly superior performance in PPV at n and AUPR metrics. However, a limitation of BigMHC is that it does not consider the TCR binding process in its immunogenicity predictions.

NeoDisc49 is a proteogenomics-integrated pipeline for personalized neoantigen discovery. It builds upon existing ML-based algorithms149 by incorporating peptide-HLA binding affinity, presentation probability, mutation information, and immunogenicity data as inputs to train a model that predicts the immunogenicity of candidate neoantigens. A key point is that NeoDisc performs neoantigen prediction through integrated multi-omics data analysis and can account for tumor heterogeneity. When benchmarked against other algorithms, which include pVACseq,150,151 pTuneos,152 and Gartner et al.,153 NeoDisc demonstrates a significantly superior performance in predicting immunogenic neoantigens.

CapHLA154 is a convolution and attention-based deep learning algorithm that predicts peptide-HLA binding affinity and surface presentation probability for both HLA class I and II. It outperforms the ensemble model BigMHC,116 TripHLApan,35 MixMHC2pred,149 TransPHLA,119 DeepNeo-MHC,155 NetMHCpan-EL,113 and MHCflurry146 in terms of accuracy, F1-score, and MCC. The significance of CapHLA lies in its ability to simultaneously predict the peptide-HLA binding affinity and surface presentation probability by utilizing EL data. However, it only uses the sequence information of peptide-HLA pairs and does not directly incorporate additional biological factors that affect presentation, such as peptide processing, abundance, TAP transport, or proteasome cleavage. Like BigMHC, it also has the limitation that it cannot predict T cell recognition, which is necessary for immunogenicity prediction.

HLAIImaster156 is a multi-modal two-stage attention-based deep learning model for HLA-II antigen presentation and immunogenicity prediction. The first stage is based on a transformer and attention mechanism to predict the peptide-HLA presentation using the peptide sequence, HLA-II gene sequence, protein expression values, and protease cleavage score, while the second stage uses transfer learning and domain adaptation to estimate the immunogenicity of presented peptides with additional immune response data. This model has shown the highest performance in differentiating experimentally validated melanoma associated peptides using the PPV@N(2%) metric over MARIA,157 which is also a multi-modal AI scheme based on a RNN network. However, this model has limitations such as data bias (exception of HLA-DP allele because of data scarcity) and uses experimentally set weights for multi-modal data.

2.7 Summary

AI algorithms have evolved from independent, single-step models toward integrated pipelines that jointly consider antigen processing, peptide–HLA (pHLA) binding, TCR–pHLA recognition, and immunogenicity.

In antigen processing, Pepsickle, PUUPL, NetCleave, DeepTAP, and MHCflurry 2.0 AP address proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport, and ER trimming, though proxy labeling and data scarcity still limit generalization. For peptide-HLA and TCR-pHLA binding, major performance gains now come from attention-based architectures, transfer learning on large eluted ligand datasets, and fine-tuning with limited immunogenicity labels. However, performance highly depends on dataset choice and evaluation metrics. After collecting the benchmark results proposed in each paper, the results reveal that the same algorithm can show widely varying performance metrics (e.g., AUC) depending on the dataset in different studies. For example, in the result of PISTE, the ERGO showed an AUC lower than 0.6, but in that of TranspMHC, the ERGO showed over 0.8. The validation dataset was different, underscoring the need for balanced, robust indicators such as PPV@n and further in-depth research. In addition, current AI research remains unexplored in areas such as T cell trafficking, infiltration, and exhaustion due to the scarcity of available data and lack of standardized benchmarks.

At the pre-clinical level, integrated pipelines such as TruNeo, BigMHC, NeoDisc, and CapHLA demonstrate practical advantages by combining antigen processing, clonality, tumor heterogeneity, and HLA binding into unified scores, improving the recall and prioritization of clinically actionable neoantigens. Specifically, EDGE, trained directly on tumor HLA ligandome MS data, achieved a PPV up to nine-fold higher than the binding affinity-based models with or without expression filtering at matched recall, highlighting substantial wet-lab savings per top-N ranked peptides. By contrast, while the integrated pipeline, TruNeo, improved prioritization over affinity-only baselines, EDGE consistently achieved the highest recall@5 to 20 candidate sets on a shared benchmark, IFN-γ ELISpot.158 These results have shown that presentation-aware or multi-modal integration, rather than affinity alone, is critical for decision-level metrics such as recall@N and wet-lab saves per top-N. 3 Limitations

This chapter provides a more detailed discussion of the current limitations of existing AI models and highlight areas where further improvements are needed.

3.1 Evaluation

3.1.1 Metrics

Although AUC and AUPR are widely used metrics for AI performance evaluation, the metrics do not properly reflect the deterministic characteristics of screening. For example, in binding models, the AUC evaluates only the probability that a randomly selected binding pair will receive a higher score than a random non-binding pair159 and does not account for the accuracy of prediction or the actual effectiveness within the top-N selection. Moreover, model-predicted probability values often serve merely as scores, not the desired value (e.g., binding probability). In the development of TCR-T therapy, wet-lab resources are limited. Therefore, the objective of current AI research is focused on identifying a manageable top-N subset from millions of candidates with top-N metrics such as PPV@N and recall@N.160 In addition, evaluating the reliability of predicted probabilities via calibrated risk metrics (e.g., expected or maximum calibration error),161 assessing clinical and economic value through the net benefit using decision curve analysis,162 and measuring direct work efficiency improvements, such as wet-lab saves per top-N,163 should be included.

3.1.2 Data

Current AI systems are fundamentally constrained by datasets. For antigen processing, many labels are intrinsically unobservable at scale. Proteasome cleavage, TAP transport or ER trimming are typically inferred indirectly or sampled sparsely by MS, so training labels are incomplete, noisy, and biased toward abundant peptides.164,165 For binding prediction, most TCR-pHLA resources record binders while non-binding pairs are often synthetically generated by random or heuristic re-pairing rather than assay-verification.160,166 This may inflates the AUC or AUPR and undermine generalization to unseen epitopes or HLAs, and the recent benchmarks and reviews repeatedly report this negative label gap in TCR specificity datasets. In addition, model generalizability is also constrained by HLA restriction, as datasets and clinical trials are enriched for frequent alleles (e.g., HLA-A*02:01).160

In the context of HLA-II, challenges on data are even more pronounced. Unlike HLA-I, which have closed ends, where most models treat binding as a binding classification problem, HLA-II prediction should consider several points; (i) identification of the 9-mer binding core and register within variable-length peptides167; (ii) prediction of peptide-HLA II binding and presentation across diverse combinations113; (iii) evaluation of HLA-DM mediated editing sensitivity and kinetic stability61; and (iv) assessment of CD4⁺ responses.168 However, current labels are largely derived by MS which only take a subset of presented peptides and functional assays for HLA-II presentation and immunogenicity, which remain limited and imbalanced.

Moreover, the risk of data leakage leads to performance overestimation. If data from the same epitope are mixed between training and test datasets, epitope frequency or patterns could be major features rather than true TCR specificity. Benchmark studies have shown that models with high AUC and AUPR under random splits frequently perform poorly under “leave-one-epitope-out” evaluations,160,166 indicating that a good performance is only obtainable for commonly observed targets but fails for rare ones.169 Likewise, overlap of similar CDR3 sequences or identical pairs from different databases or publications can induce memorization effects, necessitating rigorous curation to remove duplicate or closely related data.170,171 Overestimation also arises when data from the same donor, laboratory, or batch are split between train and test datasets, stressing the importance of “leave-one-study-out” or donor-level splits. The recent IMMREP23 benchmarking challenge highlights that a split design is paramount for valid model assessment.172

3.2 Complexity

Single-step predictions cannot capture the full contextual complexity of the immune system. Immunogenicity is not solely dependent on high binding affinity.173,174 Binding stability has been shown to be a more effective predictor of cytotoxic T lymphocyte immunogenicity for peptides and HLA class I175 and combining the dissociation rate and affinity is far more effective for predicting peptide immunogenicity than considering affinity alone.176,177 In addition, NetMHCstabpan178 have shown that half-life of pHLA is more related to immunogenicity than affinity. Furthermore, even when the same peptide binds to multiple HLA alleles, immune responses may be induced by some alleles but not others.167 Finally, an analysis using ML techniques revealed that features such as proteasomal cleavage, TAP import into ER, and binding stability are more strongly associated with immunogenicity than binding affinity.149

Recently, studies have been conducted to predict the immunogenicity of cancer neoantigens,179, 180, 181 which include machine learning179,181 and deep learning180 approaches that predict immunogenicity by considering several biological features (e.g., binding core, surface, structure, expression, TME). In addition, the study proposed by tumor neoantigen selection alliance (TESLA)182 identified several key parameters that influence tumor epitope immunogenicity. Among the parameters, the study demonstrated that MHC-I binding affinity, abundance in the TME, binding stability, fractional hydrophobicity, and mutation position are key immunogenic parameters for development of prediction algorithms, with validation across multiple cohort experiments. 3.3 Immune response and safety

While current AI prioritizes predicting immunogenic peptide-HLA-TCR combinations, studies that systematically address adverse effects such as on-target-off-tumor toxicity are limited.

In 2013, an affinity-enhanced TCR targeting MAGE-A3 triggered fatal cardiotoxicity because the engineered receptor cross-recognized a homologous HLA-A*01-presented Titin peptide in a human heart. This issue arose because the Titin peptide of a mouse did not cross-react in pre-clinical studies.183 A separate 2013 report described severe neurologic toxicity from MAGE-A-directed TCR-T, likely due to unappreciated MAGE-A12 expression in the human brain.184 These cases have shown that high-affinity TCRs can convert subtle normal-tissue presentation into clinical toxicity and that animal models may miss human-specific risks, emphasizing prospective, multi-tier TCR specificity screening mandatory before clinical deployment.185

From AI for TCR-T perspectives, these safety related cases translate into requirements for model development and evaluation; incorporating presentation and normal-tissue evidence beyond affinity and penalizing candidate TCRs with predicted recognition of benign ligands.186

4 Advanced TCR-T therapy

Developing an all-in-one model that can comprehensively reflect all of the massive number of variables and highly complex steps of TCR-T therapy is currently not possible. This is why recent research has been focused on applying AI to accelerate specific stages of the therapeutic process or to simulate the phenomena that are otherwise intractable through conventional mathematical modeling. While such task-specific approaches have yielded meaningful advances, relying solely on data that is observable within each isolated stage is insufficient to reflect the complexity of the immune response as an integrated system. Moreover, most AI models remain primarily data-driven, where the vastness of the solution space leads to an overwhelming number of candidate predictions. Consequently, experimental validation remains indispensable and demands substantial time and resources.

4.1 Federated learning

Federated learning (FL) is a collaborative training paradigm that enables institutions to develop a model without a centralized raw database.187 It may provide a viable strategy to overcome the data-related challenges. For example, by allowing each site to train locally and share only model parameters, FL facilitates the inclusion of geographically and demographically diverse cohorts, thereby mitigating allele skew and improving model generalization across under-represented HLA. Furthermore, because each institution can serve as an independent validation domain, FL naturally supports leave-one-study-out or leave-one-epitope-out evaluation schemes. Finally, the decentralized aggregation of model updates from heterogeneous MS pipelines may also reduce site-specific bias and enhance robustness against incomplete or noisy antigen processing labels.188

4.2 De novo TCR design

Most current AI models related to TCR stand for binding affinity prediction and finding candidate TCR from a database. Nevertheless, research on de novo TCR generation189, 190, 191, 192 is also increasingly being conducted. A recent study based on reinforcement learning189 utilized PanPep,132 a model with a high performance in binding prediction, to generate TCR sequences optimized for binding. Although PanPep has primarily been used as the reward model in reinforcement learning, incorporating additional biological factors involved in immune response into the reward model could enable accurate TCR sequence generation.

In addition, recently, physics-informed neural network193 has demonstrated the effectiveness of seamlessly integrating physical knowledge into data-driven learning, and a physics-informed diffusion model,194 which combines these principles with a generative diffusion model195 has been proposed. A generative model,196 including diffusion model, can serve as a powerful alternative in the data scarce field (e.g., TCR-pHLA binding prediction). However, the immune system is highly complex and involves many variables that must be considered. If mathematical modeling of a biological phenomena, along with structural and physical constraints and other available prior knowledge, is incorporated into the diffusion model, it should enable faster identification of candidate TCR sequences that are more clinically relevant and reliable.

4.3 Explainable AI and large language models

While large language models (LLMs) are driving global transformation, attempts to apply them to various research fields are underway. This expansion has already reached fields closely related to immunotherapy, including RNA-protein interaction,197 protein-protein interaction,198 and drug-target interaction prediction.199 This can be attributed to LLM’s advantage in comprehensively modeling the complex relationships between sequence, structure, and function. 200,201 First, LLMs enable improved data efficiency through self-supervised learning. Second, the self-attention mechanism of LLMs allows for more effective predictions by considering not only local patterns but also the global context of the data. Furthermore, recent advancements have seen LLMs incorporate the Chain of Though (CoT) reasoning technique,202 enabling evidence-based inference from unstructured data. Unlike conventional LLMs, which generate immediate responses to queries, the CoT approach allows models to explicitly outline intermediate reasoning steps, thereby clarifying how they arrive at specific conclusions. For instance, in generative AI models designed to predict TCR-pHLA binding affinity, CoT can be utilized to explain whether a particular sequence enhances or weakens the binding affinity. Additionally, when generating novel sequences, CoT ensures that the model does not merely propose optimized sequences but also provides insights into how its choices account for physiological functions, immunological properties, or correlations with TCR-pHLA interactions. It enhances the reliability of predictions by providing insights into how specific sequences may influence immune responses, ultimately achieving the objective of explainable AI.

In addition, by leveraging retrieval-augmented generation (RAG),203 LLMs can reference newly discovered data or information related to immune responses. This helps mitigate the hallucination problem inherent to conventional LLMs, which operate on static data, and enables the delivery of domain-specific customized responses. By providing up-to-date information on immune responses, RAG can improve the accuracy of neoantigen and immune response prediction.204

4.3.1 Foundation model in medicine

Recently, as efforts to build LLM in the field of medicine have increased,205, 206, 207, 208 research on foundation models for medicine has also been actively pursued.209,210 A foundation model in medicine refers to a large-scale AI model that is pre-trained on vast amounts of unlabeled medical data using self-supervised or unsupervised learning. This model learns generalized knowledge and patterns from diverse types of data, including natural language-type inputs such as protein sequences, DNA sequences, or simplified molecular input entry system (SMILES) codes211; images such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), or positron emission tomography (PET); mathematical analyses, and clinical reports. It can then be easily adapted to various downstream or specific tasks through fine-tuning or in-context learning. The foundation model can be considered an initial digital twin of the human body system, and in the medical field, it can be referred to as a digital patient twin.212

4.4 Simulation program

Although there are currently no studies that apply AI to predict trafficking and infiltration in TCR-T therapy, computational modeling approaches have been proposed to address these dynamics. Joslyn et al., 2024, employs an ordinary differential equation (ODE) based system to reflect proliferation, differentiation, trafficking and death of multiple T cell subsets, including both therapeutic TCR-T cells and endogenous T cells over time.213 Using clinical data as input, this model allows for patient-specific parameter estimation as a digital twin simulation framework. The simulation results demonstrated that an infusion product enriched in stem cell-like memory T cells yields substantially greater expansion and persistence compared with effector memory or effector T cell dominated compositions, and that therapeutic efficacy can be achieved even at lower doses. This provides a rational basis for optimizing the infusion product composition in TCR-T therapy.

In addition, mathematical modeling has been applied to quantify the intra-tumoral infiltration dynamics of T cells to evaluate both efficacy and safety.214 In this study, a microfluidic organ-on-a-chip-based 3-D intestinal tissue model was established, co-culturing patient-derived PBMC with macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells. The system enabled direct observation of T cell penetration into tissue compartments, and development of a partial differential equation (PDE) model for characterization of the reaction-diffusion-chemotaxis dynamics. This provides a simultaneous simulation of spatio-temporal variations in PBMC density and chemokine concentrations, enabling quantitative analysis of the interplay between T cell infiltration and cell death within the TME.

These studies highlight the potential of mechanistic and mathematical modeling as complementary tools for understanding and predicting T cell trafficking and infiltration in TCR-T therapy. While AI has not yet been directly applied in this context, integrating ML with ODE/PDE-based digital twin simulations may offer many benefits towards enhancing clinical translation.

4.5 Quantum computing

Since the advent of the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era in 2017, efforts to leverage quantum supremacy,215 defined as achieving exponential speedup over classical computing in specific problems have grown substantially in the field of bioinformatics. For example, D-Wave Quantum’s quantum annealing system has been applied to optimize the reconstruction of CT images.216 Hybrid quantum-classical computing has also been utilized for peptide design targeting microplastics,217 and a hybrid generative model identified potential KRAS inhibitors that were later validated experimentally.218 Furthermore, quantum algorithms have been employed to tackle protein and peptide design challenges, particularly in searching for low-energy conformational states.219

Efforts to adapt classical neural networks into quantum frameworks have also emerged. Examples include quantum long short-term memory (LSTM),220 quantum generative adversarial network (GAN),221 and quantum variational autoencoder (VAE).222 More recently, a quantum neural network was successfully applied to identify a novel genetic biomarker associated with mutational activation of CTLA-4 related pathways.223

In the context of TCR-T therapy, quantum computing offers advantages for managing enormous combinatorial and structural complexity. Quantum algorithms can efficiently explore the vast search space of TCR-pHLA interactions, identifying high-affinity and clinically relevant pairs faster than classical methods. Moreover, quantum generative models can be used to design novel TCR sequences or peptide candidates that satisfy predefined biophysical constraints, while quantum optimization enables rapid selection of pairs by minimizing energy or binding uncertainty.

Despite current limitations, including noise in quantum devices, restricted hardware scalability, and need for advanced quantum algorithms, the eventual development of large-scale quantum computers and computing algorithms hold promise for breakthroughs in TCR-T therapy.

5 Conclusions

AI is rapidly transforming the development of TCR-T therapy by enhancing the precision of antigen processing, peptide-HLA, and TCR-pHLA predictions. Despite current constraints in data quality, model generalizability, and safety validation, emerging approaches, including multi-modal integration, federated learning, digital twin, and physics-informed generative AI, may progressively bridge the gap between computational prediction and clinical translation. Through these efforts, AI can enable faster, safer, and more personalized TCR-T therapy development.
