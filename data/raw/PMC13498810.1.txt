Objective

Ventilator-associated pneumonia is a common complication among critically ill patients, particularly those with acute cerebrovascular diseases, including acute ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. This study aimed to identify the factors associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia in patients with acute cerebrovascular diseases.

Methods

A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using MEDLINE, EMBASE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Google Scholar. Studies involving patients aged ≥18 years who were hospitalized in an intensive care unit for >48 h, required mechanical ventilation, and had a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke or aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage were included. Risk of bias was assessed using a domain-based Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions framework across all included studies, with complementary Newcastle–Ottawa Scale assessments for cohort and case–control studies. Data were analyzed using Review Manager. No publication-year restrictions were applied, and all eligible studies indexed up to the date of the final search were considered for inclusion.

Results

In total, 16 studies involving 16,702 patients were included. The pooled incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia was 31.62% (95% confidence interval: 24.82%–38.42%). Older age (≥65 years) (odds ratio: 2.74, 95% confidence interval: 1.68–4.48; p < 0.001) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (odds ratio: 2.03, 95% confidence interval: 1.05–3.93; p = 0.04) were significantly associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia, whereas diabetes mellitus demonstrated a borderline association (odds ratio: 1.52; 95% confidence interval: 1.00–2.29; p = 0.05). In contrast, arterial hypertension (odds ratio: 0.99; 95% confidence interval: 0.63–1.57; p = 0.98), tobacco use (odds ratio: 1.98, 95% confidence interval: 0.25–15.49, p = 0.51), and tracheostomy (odds ratio: 2.19, 95% confidence interval: 0.73–6.58; p = 0.16) were not consistently associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia. Risk of bias across the included studies was generally low to moderate.

Conclusion

Ventilator-associated pneumonia remains a frequent complication among patients with acute cerebrovascular diseases. Older age and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were consistently associated with an increased risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia in our study. These findings may improve the understanding of ventilator-associated pneumonia risk in this understudied neurocritical population and provide a foundation for future prospective studies evaluating targeted preventive interventions.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections represent a significant global health challenge as they increase patient morbidity and mortality and contribute to higher hospital costs. 1 Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) accounts for 80% of all hospital-acquired pneumonias and is the most common infection in intensive care units (ICUs). 2 The reported incidence of VAP varies considerably across studies owing to differences in patient populations, diagnostic criteria, clinical settings, and preventive strategies. Nevertheless, VAP remains one of the most frequent healthcare-associated infections among mechanically ventilated critically ill patients, with reported incidences in critical populations approaching 30%. 3

In neurocritical care patients, the need for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) is particularly common due to specific pathophysiological features, such as altered mental status, dysphagia, compromised airway protection, and respiratory dysfunction. 4 Among neurocritical patients, those with acute cerebrovascular diseases (ACVDs) (aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and ischemic stroke (IS)) may require IMV. This is attributed to different physio-pathological processes underscoring the connection between the brain and lungs. 5 Additionally, secondary brain injury triggers (e.g. fever, hypotension, and hypoxemia) have been associated with early-onset pneumonia. Although several studies have investigated risk factors for VAP in neurocritical populations, existing evidence remains heterogeneous and often combines diverse neurological conditions, limiting its applicability to specific subgroups such as patients with ACVDs. 6 Currently, there is limited clarity regarding the primary risk factors for VAP development in patients with IS, ICH, and aSAH. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address this knowledge gap by identifying and analyzing the risk factors for VAP in this specific population.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Literature Search Extension (PRISMA-S) for reporting literature searches.7,8 The protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; ID: CRD42024600628). The review was conducted in the following predefined stages: (a) formulation of the research question; (b) identification of available studies; (c) development and refinement of the search strategy; (d) study selection; (e) data extraction; and (f) qualitative and quantitative synthesis.

Search strategy

The literature search was developed and reported in accordance with the PRISMA-S extension. Two reviewers independently searched four information sources—MEDLINE (via Ovid), EMBASE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Google Scholar—between 5 September 2024 and 10 September 2024. No restrictions on publication year were applied during the search or study selection process. The search strategy used controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and VAP. Full search strategies for all sources are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Study eligibility

All articles published in English with observational (prospective or retrospective) and randomized clinical trial (RCT) designs were included if they met the following criteria: (a) involved patients aged ≥18 years; (b) involved patients who were admitted to the ICU; (c) patients received IMV for >48 h; and (c) patients had ≥1 of the following features for VAP definition, including fever (body temperature > 38°C), changes in secretions, leukocytosis or leukopenia (thresholds as defined by the study), chest X-ray findings, or use of the Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (CPIS), American Thoracic Society (ATS) criteria for VAP diagnosis, or Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definition. Ecological studies, case reports, letters to the editor, studies exclusively reporting on etiology, and those including pediatric populations were excluded. Study screening and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers who were blinded to each other's decisions. Any disagreements were identified systematically and resolved through discussion until consensus was reached before final study inclusion and data extraction.

Data extraction

Data from the included studies were extracted and organized using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Key variables included study name, first author, publication year, country of publication, number of patients, and definition of VAP. When necessary, incidence estimates were recalculated from the original study data using a standardized approach to ensure methodological consistency and comparability across studies.

Assessment of risk of bias

Risk of bias was assessed using design-appropriate tools. A domain-based Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) and Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Exposures (ROBINS-E) tool assessment were performed. 9 In addition, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used as a complementary design-specific assessment for cohort and case–control studies; detailed study-level results have been provided in Supplementary Appendix No 2. 10

Certainty of the evidence

The certainty of the evidence for the main pooled outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Certainty was rated considering study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. A Summary of Findings table is provided in the Supplementary Appendix No 3.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses was performed using Review Manager version 8.11.0. Similarity among participants, exposure, and research questions were considered. Heterogeneity was calculated using the I2 statistic, with 0%–29% indicating negligible heterogeneity; 30%–49%, moderate heterogeneity; 50%–74%, substantial heterogeneity; and 75%–100%, considerable heterogeneity. Based on these results, random-effects models were applied for high heterogeneity, whereas fixed-effects models were used otherwise. Results were presented as forest plots, with significance established at p ≤0.05.

Protocol amendments

The original PROSPERO protocol (CRD42024600628) planned to include adult neurocritical patients with IS, ICH, and aSAH, and evaluate both risk factors for VAP and their impact on in-hospital mortality. After study selection and data extraction, the planned analyses of in-hospital mortality was not performed because mortality outcomes were inconsistently reported across eligible studies and could not be pooled reliably. Finally, the literature search was conducted between 5 September 2024 and 10 September 2024, before PROSPERO registration was completed on 23 October 2024; therefore, the registration should be interpreted as nonprospective. These deviations from the registered protocol are disclosed in accordance with PRISMA 2020 Item 24c.

Results

In total, 584 references were identified through manual search and database queries; title and abstract screening were performed. Among these, 138 were the subject of full text review, and 16 studies were included in the final data extraction and risk of bias analysis. Excluded studies comprised case reports, pediatric populations, studies lacking a clear VAP definition, populations with traumatic brain injury (TBI), pneumonias due to other causes (non-VAP), thematic reviews, and book chapters. The selection process and exclusion reasons are illustrated in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Two authors independently reviewed the studies, with a third reviewer resolving any conflicts.

Description of the included studies

The analyzed studies included patients with diagnosis of IS (n = 6), aSAH (n = 5), ICH (n = 1), and mixed populations (IS, aSAH, and ICH) (n = 4). In terms of study designs, there were 10 cohort studies (62.5%) and six case–control studies (37.5%). All studies included in the analysis provided a clear definition of VAP based on the CPIS, CDC definition, or ATS criteria and reported the incidence of this condition.

Four studies (25%) were performed in the United States (US); 4 (25%) in China; 3 (18%) in India; 3 (18%) in France; and 1 each in Greece, Belgium, Malaysia, and Poland (6% each). Characteristics of the studies included are presented in Table 1.

Incidence of VAP in patients with ACVDs

Analysis of the selected articles revealed a wide range of VAP incidences. For example, Sachdeva et al. have reported an incidence of >60%. 11 Two studies have reported incidences >50% (Bogossian et al. and Zhu et al.).12,13 Six studies have documented incidences ranging between 30% and 50%, including those by Chang et al., Cinotti et al., Launey et al., Mhendiratta et al., Teng et al., and Xue et al.14–19 Incidences between 20% and 30% were reported by Catalino et al. and Kasuya et al.20,21 Conversely, five studies have reported incidences <20%, including those by Abulhasan et al., Lele et al., Pluta et al., Ren et al., and Villwock et al.22–26 These studies provide an evidence base for evaluating the incidence of VAP. The overall incidence was 31.62% (95% confidence interval (CI): 24.82%–38.42%, p < 0.001, I2 = 100%) (Figure 4). Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed for patients with IS (Figure 4(b)) and aSAH (Figure 4(c)), revealing pathology-specific differences in the incidence of VAP. The pooled incidence was 29.73% (95% CI: 18.89%–40.58%; p < 0.001; I2 = 98%) in patients with IS and 37.96% (95% CI: 22.25%–53.67%; p < 0.001; I2 = 100%) in those with aSAH, suggesting a higher burden of VAP among patients with aSAH. Subgroup analysis of patients with ICH was not feasible because the limited number of eligible studies and small pooled sample size precluded a robust estimation of VAP incidence. However, patients with ICH were retained in the overall pooled incidence analysis as part of the mixed ACVD population.

Meta-analysis of risk factors for VAP

A series of meta-analyses was conducted on the 16 included studies to explore VAP risk factors in ACVD patients (Figure 5).

Figure 5. Forest plot for relevant risk factors: (a) age ≥ 65 years, (b) COPD, (c) diabetes mellitus, (d) arterial hypertension, (e) tobacco use, and (f) tracheostomy.

COPD: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Age ≥65 years

Age ≥65 years emerged as a significant risk factor in two studies, encompassing a total of 277 patients (odds ratio (OR): 2.74, 95% CI: 1.68–4.48, p < 0.001, I2 = 14%), (Figure 5(a)).

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

COPD was identified as a risk factor in two studies involving 308 patients (OR: 2.03, 95% CI (1.05–3.93), p = 0.04); with a heterogeneity of I2 = 46% (Figure 5(b)).

Diabetes mellitus

Four studies comprising 606 patients evaluated the association between diabetes mellitus and VAP. Diabetes mellitus demonstrated a borderline association with the development of VAP (OR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.00–2.29; p = 0.05), with low between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 9%) (Figure 5(c)).

Hypertension

Three studies, including 409 patients, assessed the association between arterial hypertension and VAP. Arterial hypertension was not consistently associated with the development of VAP (OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.63–1.57; p = 0.98), with low between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 30%) (Figure 5(d)).

Tobacco use

Two studies, including 381 patients, assessed the association between tobacco use and VAP. Tobacco use was not consistently associated with the development of VAP (OR: 1.98, 95% CI: 0.25–15.49; p = 0.51), with considerable between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 95%) (Figure 5(e)).

Tracheostomy

Two studies, including 239 patients (95 with tracheostomy and 144 without tracheostomy), assessed the association between tracheostomy and VAP. Tracheostomy was not consistently associated with the development of VAP (OR: 2.19, 95% CI: 0.73–6.58; p = 0.16), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 65%) (Figure 5(f)).

Discussion

Multiple risk factors for VAP have been described across critically ill populations, with IMV representing the fundamental prerequisite for its development. However, evidence specifically addressing patients with ACVDs remains limited. Based on the present meta-analysis, which included patients with IS, ICH, and aSAH requiring IMV, age ≥65 years and COPD were the factors consistently associated with an increased risk of VAP.

The pooled incidence of VAP in patients with ACVDs was 31.62%, with a higher incidence observed among patients with aSAH (37.96%) than among those with IS (29.73%). However, the incidence of VAP varied substantially across studies.14,20,23 Pooled incidence analyses demonstrated considerable between-study heterogeneity, indicating that the reported incidence is influenced by multiple clinical and methodological factors rather than representing a universal estimate. This variability is likely explained by differences in patient characteristics, disease severity, VAP diagnostic criteria used, local prevention bundles, infection-control practices, duration of IMV, and institutional neurocritical care protocols. 27 Moreover, time-sensitive interventions specific to each cerebrovascular disease, including reperfusion therapies for IS, surgical or medical management of ICH, and early aneurysm occlusion for aSAH, may directly influence ventilator exposure and, consequently, the VAP risk. 28 Therefore, the pooled incidence should be interpreted as a summary estimate reflecting diverse neurocritical care settings rather than a universal incidence applicable to all institutions.

Age ≥65 years was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of VAP. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that age-related physiological changes, including reduced pulmonary elasticity, impaired cough reflex, diminished mucociliary clearance, and progressive immunosenescence, increase susceptibility to respiratory infections. 14 Previous studies have suggested that the risk of VAP increases by more than 1.01-fold with each additional year of age. 29 Our pooled analysis further supports these observations, demonstrating an approximately 2.7-fold increased risk of VAP among patients aged ≥65 years. These findings emphasize the importance of early identification and close respiratory monitoring in older patients with ACVDs requiring IMV.

Among the evaluated comorbidities, COPD was the only condition consistently associated with VAP. Although prolonged IMV may partially explain this association, several pathophysiological mechanisms provide additional biological plausibility. 30 Chronic airway inflammation, goblet-cell hyperplasia, mucus hypersecretion, and impaired ciliary function compromise mucociliary clearance, facilitating persistent colonization of the lower respiratory tract by potentially pathogenic microorganisms. 31 Stable COPD is also characterized by alterations in the airway microbiome that promote dysbiosis, chronic neutrophilic inflammation, and the selection of microorganisms capable of causing infection during critical illness. 32 Furthermore, expiratory flow limitation and loss of elastic recoil shift the equal-pressure point toward smaller, more collapsible airways, resulting in dynamic airway compression, impaired secretion clearance, and mucus retention. 33 In patients with ACVDs, these pre-existing abnormalities are further exacerbated by impaired consciousness, dysphagia, reduced cough and swallowing reflexes, immobility, endotracheal intubation, and prolonged IMV. Together, these mechanisms promote secretion retention, microaspiration, increased bacterial burden, and impaired pulmonary host defenses, providing a biologically plausible explanation for the increased risk of VAP observed in patients with COPD. 34

Increasing evidence has also suggested that the respiratory microbiome plays a central role in the pathogenesis of VAP among mechanically ventilated patients.15,35 Consequently, advances in precision medicine—including genomics, airway microbiome characterization, and host–microbiota interactions—may facilitate the identification of patients with increased susceptibility to respiratory infections, enabling the development of individualized preventive strategies, particularly among patients with underlying COPD. 36

Additional risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, tobacco use, dysphagia, and tracheostomy, were also evaluated. However, the available evidence was insufficient to establish consistent associations with VAP. The limited number of studies, conflicting findings, and clinical heterogeneity across ACVDs populations reduced the precision of the pooled estimates and precluded definitive conclusions regarding these variables. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of the absence of an association, but rather as reflecting the current limitations of the available literature. Larger prospective studies specifically designed to evaluate these risk factors in patients with ACVDs are warranted.

In particular, the role of tracheostomy deserves consideration. In patients with aSAH, early tracheostomy has been associated with shorter durations of mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and hospitalization. 37 Conversely, among patients undergoing decompressive craniectomy for IS, no significant differences in VAP incidence have been reported between early and late tracheostomy. 38 In contrast, studies involving patients with TBI have indicated that tracheostomy increases the risk of VAP. 39 These discrepancies likely reflect differences in patient populations, timing of tracheostomy, and clinical indications. Therefore, current evidence remains insufficient to determine whether tracheostomy independently modifies the risk of VAP.

Certain study limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the diagnostic criteria for VAP varied across the included studies, all studies included adopted internationally accepted definitions based on the CDC definition, CPIS, or ATS criteria. Nevertheless, differences in their application may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity, highlighting the need for greater standardization of VAP diagnosis in future studies.

Second, although several potential risk factors were identified during data extraction, only a few were reported consistently across studies, precluding quantitative synthesis for several clinically relevant variables.

Finally, substantial heterogeneity was observed for several pooled analyses, particularly those evaluating diabetes mellitus, hypertension, tobacco use, and the overall incidence of VAP. This variability likely reflects differences in study periods, evolving VAP prevention strategies, neurocritical care practices, patient case-mix, and institutional management protocols. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger, prospective, multicenter studies focusing specifically on patients with ACVDs.

Conclusion

VAP remains a common complication among patients with ACVDs. Older age and COPD were consistently associated with an increased risk of VAP. These findings may improve the understanding of VAP risk in this understudied neurocritical population and provide a foundation for future prospective studies evaluating targeted preventive intervention.
