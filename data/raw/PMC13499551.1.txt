Abstract

Chronic lung diseases, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), bronchiectasis and pulmonary fibrosis, remain major causes of morbidity and mortality despite advances in pharmacological treatment. Emerging evidence suggests that these conditions are influenced by a bidirectional gut–lung axis, through which intestinal microbiota, microbial metabolites, epithelial barrier integrity and mucosal immune programming shape pulmonary inflammation, tissue remodelling and host defence. In this narrative review, we synthesise current clinical and experimental evidence linking gut microbiota–immune interactions to these four chronic lung diseases. Rather than treating dysbiosis as a uniform process, we distinguish shared and disease-specific microbial signatures, including depletion of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing taxa, delayed microbial maturation, reduced faecal microbial diversity, expansion of pathobionts and altered bile-acid or tryptophan metabolism. We further summarise how SCFAs, tauroursodeoxycholic acid, tryptophan-derived indole metabolites and microbial fragments may regulate Th2 and Th17 inflammation, Th17/Treg balance, neutrophil function, epithelial barrier integrity and fibrotic remodelling. Microbiota-targeted strategies, including dietary fibre and prebiotic approaches, probiotics, synbiotic formulations, faecal microbiota transplantation and selected microbiota-modulating compounds, have shown protective effects in multiple preclinical models, whereas clinical evidence remains limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up, heterogeneous interventions and incomplete mechanistic endpoints. We also discuss unresolved issues, including animal-to-human translation and the difficulty of distinguishing gut-derived from airway-derived microbial signals. A more precise understanding of shared and disease-specific gut–lung circuits may support the rational development of mechanism-informed microbiota-targeted adjunctive strategies for chronic lung disease.

Introduction

Chronic lung diseases, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), bronchiectasis and pulmonary fibrosis, remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide despite advances in inhaled therapies, biologics and antifibrotic agents.1,2 Many patients continue to experience recurrent exacerbations, progressive loss of lung function and impaired quality of life, indicating that current treatments only partly address the systemic and mucosal drivers of disease.3,4 In recent years, the concept of a bidirectional gut–lung axis has reshaped this field. Accumulating evidence suggests that intestinal microbiota composition, microbial metabolites, gut barrier integrity and mucosal immune programming can influence airway inflammation, tissue remodelling and host defence across diverse chronic lung diseases.5 However, these effects should not be interpreted as a single generic form of dysbiosis. Different lung diseases appear to involve both shared microbial patterns and disease-specific microbial and metabolic signatures.

Mechanistic and interventional studies now support the gut–lung axis in several major respiratory phenotypes. In COPD, faecal microbiota transfer (FMT) and high-resistant-starch diets protected cigarette smoke-exposed mice from emphysema and systemic inflammation, and an inulin pilot trial in patients suggested possible symptomatic and inflammatory benefits, linking specific microbiome configurations to disease severity and treatment response.6 In asthma, inulin-type fructans improved airway inflammation, asthma control and gut microbiome composition in adults, complementing preclinical work on fibre-driven short-chain fatty acid signalling.7 In bronchiectasis, an Eggerthella lenta-derived bile acid, tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), was recently shown to impair neutrophil energetics and bactericidal function, thereby worsening Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung infection in experimental models.8 In pulmonary fibrosis, the Spirulina pigment C-phycocyanin attenuated bleomycin- and radiation-induced fibrosis while partially normalising gut and lung microbiota, suggesting that selected microbiota-modulating compounds may have antifibrotic effects in preclinical settings.9 These studies indicate that gut microbial signals can either protect against or exacerbate lung pathology depending on the disease context, metabolite profile and immune pathway involved.

The therapeutic implications of this axis also require clearer conceptual classification. Dietary fibre and prebiotic strategies primarily act by providing substrates for microbial fermentation and production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), whereas probiotics refer to live microorganisms administered for potential health benefit. Synbiotics should not be treated simply as another name for probiotics, because the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics defines synbiotics as formulations that combine live microorganisms with substrate(s) selectively utilized by host microorganisms to confer a health benefit.10 FMT represents an ecosystem-level replacement approach, while compounds such as C-phycocyanin are better regarded as microbiota-modulating agents rather than generalized microbiome interventions. This distinction is important because the strength of evidence, mechanism of action and translational readiness differ substantially across these categories.

Another unresolved issue is the relationship between intestinal and airway microbial communities. Several reviews have already summarised bidirectional gut–lung communication in COPD, and airway microbiome studies indicate that respiratory microbial communities also change with COPD severity, exacerbation risk and treatment exposure.11,12 Therefore, microbiota-derived metabolites such as SCFAs should not automatically be assumed to originate exclusively from the gut in all experimental settings. In many in vitro and in vivo studies, paired stool, sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage sampling is not available, making it difficult to distinguish gut-derived systemic effects from airway-local microbial signals. This uncertainty is particularly relevant in COPD, where gut and airway microbial alterations may coexist and potentially interact.

In this review, we focus on four major chronic lung diseases, namely asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis and pulmonary fibrosis, and summarise how gut microbiota and their metabolites influence disease onset, progression and exacerbation through the gut–lung axis. We emphasise a layered framework that links microbial composition, metabolite production, gut barrier integrity, gut-associated lymphoid tissue immune programming, systemic circulation, lymphatic trafficking and pulmonary pathological manifestations. We further compare microbiota-targeted interventions across these conditions, including dietary fibre and prebiotic strategies, probiotics, synbiotic formulations, FMT and selected microbiota-modulating compounds. By distinguishing shared cross-disease mechanisms from disease-specific microbial signatures and by critically evaluating the limitations of current evidence, this review aims to support more rational design of microbiota-based studies and future adjunctive therapeutic strategies for chronic lung diseases.

Gut Microbiota Alterations and Immune Correlates in Chronic Lung Diseases

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

Emerging evidence suggests that COPD is associated with a distinct gut microbial and metabolic profile rather than a non-specific dysbiosis.13 Primary human studies have reported altered fecal community structure, reduced levels of SCFAs and depletion of SCFA-producing taxa such as members of Lachnospiraceae, with these changes correlating with poorer lung function and systemic inflammation.14,15 One study identified a Prevotella-enriched fecal enterotype in COPD together with significantly reduced SCFA levels, whereas multi-omics profiling further showed disease-associated shifts in microbial metabolites and carbohydrate-utilisation pathways.14,15 Experimental transfer studies support a causal contribution of these intestinal communities. FMT from COPD patients into healthy mice induced heightened pulmonary inflammation, and when combined with chronic cigarette-smoke exposure, dysbiotic COPD microbiota accelerated lung-function decline, emphysematous destruction and neutrophilic airway inflammation.14 Conversely, restoration of a healthier gut ecosystem by normal fecal flora or fermentable-fiber supplementation mitigated airway inflammation, emphysema and lung-function impairment in cigarette smoke models.6 However, COPD should also be interpreted as a disease involving both gut and airway microbial ecology. Gut-derived SCFAs and microbial fragments may shape systemic immunity, but airway-local microbial changes may also contribute to inflammatory tone and exacerbation susceptibility. Thus, the COPD gut-lung axis is best viewed as a cross-compartment network involving loss of beneficial fermentative capacity, altered microbial metabolites, neutrophilic inflammation and Th17/Treg imbalance, rather than a uniform pattern of intestinal dysbiosis.

Asthma

Asthma is closely linked to gut microbial development and immunological education, particularly in early life; this relationship is supported by inflammatory-mechanism reviews and systematic syntheses of childhood microbiome data.16,17 Prospective birth-cohort data further show that delayed maturation of the infant gut microbiome, characterised by reduced diversity and depletion of fermentative taxa, is associated with increased asthma risk in childhood.18 In established asthma, several studies also report lower gut microbial diversity and altered bacterial composition in severe disease compared with mild asthma or healthy controls.19 The most consistent functional signal is a reduction in microbial fermentation capacity and SCFA-related immune regulation. High-fiber diets that enrich SCFA-producing bacteria protect mice from allergic airway inflammation, reducing eosinophilic infiltration, Th2 cytokine production and airway hyperresponsiveness.20 Mechanistically, propionate and butyrate can reprogram bone-marrow hematopoiesis, reduce the ability of dendritic cells to drive Th2 responses and promote Foxp3⁺ regulatory T-cell differentiation.20–22 In contrast, specific pro-inflammatory intestinal communities may promote lung inflammation. Segmented filamentous bacteria induce intestinal Th17 cells that can traffic to the lung and exacerbate experimental airway inflammation.23,24 Therefore, the asthma-related gut signature is not defined by one taxon but by the timing and function of microbial maturation, the availability of SCFAs and the balance between type 2, Th17 and regulatory immune pathways.

Bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis, a chronic suppurative lung disease characterised by recurrent infection, airway inflammation and impaired mucociliary clearance, has only recently been examined through the lens of the gut-lung axis.25 Compared with COPD and asthma, the clinical evidence base remains smaller, but available multi-omics data suggest that non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis is associated with reduced fecal microbial diversity, altered gut microbial composition and a distinct fecal metabolome.26 These changes were linked to clinical indices such as the Bronchiectasis Severity Index, supporting the possibility that intestinal microbial and metabolic features reflect disease severity or contribute to host-defence dysfunction.26 Importantly, these findings broaden the bronchiectasis gut-lung axis beyond a single organism or metabolite and indicate that diversity loss, altered microbial metabolism and systemic immune dysfunction should all be considered in this disease.

The most compelling mechanistic evidence currently centres on the Eggerthella lenta-TUDCA-neutrophil axis. Wang et al8 identified enrichment of E. lenta in patients with bronchiectasis and showed that this gut bacterium produces tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), which was elevated in feces and blood and correlated with more severe lung disease. In murine models, transfer of fecal microbiota from bronchiectasis patients or oral colonisation with E. lenta worsened Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung infection. Mechanistically, TUDCA disrupted LKB1-AMPK signalling, reduced neutrophil ATP generation and impaired bacterial killing, thereby weakening pulmonary host defence.8 This mechanism is particularly relevant because bronchiectasis progression is tightly linked to persistent bacterial infection and neutrophil-dominated airway inflammation.

The role of TUDCA should nevertheless be interpreted in a context-dependent manner. Outside bronchiectasis, TUDCA is often described as a hydrophilic bile acid with cytoprotective, anti-apoptotic and endoplasmic-reticulum stress-modulating properties.27 Thus, its detrimental effect in bronchiectasis does not imply that TUDCA is universally harmful; rather, it suggests that excessive or compartmentally inappropriate bile-acid signalling may impair neutrophil bioenergetics in the setting of bacterial lung infection. At present, direct clinical evidence for prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics or FMT in bronchiectasis is sparse. Future studies should determine whether restoration of fecal microbial diversity, modulation of E. lenta abundance or correction of bile-acid metabolism can improve neutrophil-mediated bacterial clearance without disrupting beneficial bile-acid functions.

Pulmonary Fibrosis

Chronic pulmonary fibroses, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), are increasingly recognised to involve gut microbiota-immune interactions despite the lung being the primary site of injury.28 Unlike asthma or COPD, the pulmonary fibrosis evidence base is still dominated by preclinical models, but these models suggest that gut microbial context can modify the severity of fibrotic remodelling. In the bleomycin model, Chioma et al29 showed that C57BL/6J mice housed under germ-free, ABSL-1 or ABSL-2 conditions developed almost no, mild or severe lung fibrosis, respectively; these gradients tracked with gut rather than lung microbial diversity. FMT from ABSL-2 donors reproduced more severe fibrosis in germ-free recipients, whereas Lactobacillus-conditioned supernatants dampened collagen-1A production in human lung fibroblasts.29 Gurczynski et al30 further demonstrated that C57BL/6NCrl mice, which harbour a distinct gut microbiome, exhibited lower mortality and attenuated collagen deposition after bleomycin than C57BL/6J mice; cohousing or horizontal transfer of microbiota from the protected substrain reduced mortality and shifted pulmonary immune transcriptional profiles toward more IL-10-rich and less IL-6-dominant responses. These findings highlight a key translational caveat: vendor, housing and substrain-dependent microbiota can substantially influence fibrotic outcomes in mice, and these effects may not directly mirror human IPF.

Microbial metabolites and non-bacterial members of the gut ecosystem add further mechanistic depth. Integrated 16S rRNA sequencing and metabolomics in bleomycin- and silica-induced fibrosis showed that gut dysbiosis was accompanied by increased serum and lung L-tryptophan. L-tryptophan supplementation aggravated fibrosis through mTOR/S6-dependent amplification of TGF-β1 signalling, whereas constraining this axis mitigated disease.31 Intestinal overgrowth of Candida albicans in dysbiotic mice similarly exacerbated bleomycin-induced fibrosis, increasing mortality, collagen deposition and Ashcroft scores through IL-17A-driven endothelial-mesenchymal transition; antifungal treatment or IL-17A blockade partially reversed these effects.32 Interventional studies with the Spirulina pigment C-phycocyanin suggest that microbiota modulation can accompany antifibrotic protection. In bleomycin-induced fibrosis, oral C-phycocyanin reduced lung collagen and inflammatory cytokines while partially normalising gut microbiota composition,9 and in radiation-induced lung injury it similarly attenuated fibrosis and corrected radiation-driven gut and lung dysbiosis.33 Overall, the pulmonary fibrosis gut signature appears to involve altered microbial ecology, tryptophan metabolism, fungal expansion and Th17/TGF-β-related remodelling pathways, but most evidence remains experimental and requires validation in human fibrotic lung disease.

Cross-Disease Synthesis of Gut Microbial and Metabolic Signatures

Taken together, the available evidence supports a layered model of gut-lung communication rather than a single generic dysbiosis model. Across asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis and pulmonary fibrosis, several recurring features can be identified, including reduced microbial diversity in selected cohorts, loss of fermentative or SCFA-producing capacity, expansion of pathobionts, altered microbial metabolites and impaired gut barrier integrity. These shared changes may promote systemic inflammatory tone, disturb Th17/Treg balance, alter neutrophil function and weaken epithelial barrier defence. However, each disease also has a distinct microbial and immunological emphasis. Asthma is strongly linked to early-life microbial maturation, SCFA availability and type 2/Th17 immune programming. COPD is characterised by reduced SCFAs, altered fermentative taxa and neutrophilic inflammation, with possible overlap between intestinal and airway microbial changes. Bronchiectasis currently shows the clearest disease-specific metabolite signal through E. lenta-derived TUDCA and neutrophil bioenergetic impairment. Pulmonary fibrosis is more closely associated in experimental models with tryptophan metabolism, fungal expansion, IL-17A/TGF-β signalling and fibrotic remodelling.

This cross-disease comparison also clarifies why microbiota-targeted interventions cannot be treated as interchangeable across chronic lung diseases. Strategies that enhance SCFA production may be most relevant to asthma and COPD phenotypes dominated by Th2, Th17 or neutrophilic inflammation, whereas bronchiectasis may require approaches that preserve bacterial clearance and avoid harmful bile-acid-mediated neutrophil suppression. In pulmonary fibrosis, modulation of tryptophan metabolism, fungal overgrowth or profibrotic immune circuits may be more relevant than simple restoration of bacterial diversity. Thus, the gut-lung axis should be framed as a set of overlapping but disease-specific microbial-metabolic-immune circuits. Key Immune Pathways at the Gut–Lung Axis

The gut-lung axis can be conceptualised as a multistep cascade rather than a single immune pathway. Disease-specific microbial changes first alter the intestinal metabolic environment and barrier function. These local changes then influence gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), circulating metabolites, microbial fragments and immune-cell trafficking through blood and lymphatic routes. In the lung, these signals converge on shared immune modules, including neutrophil activation or dysfunction, Th2 and Th17 inflammation, Th17/Treg balance, epithelial barrier repair and fibrotic remodelling. This layered model provides a clearer explanation for why similar microbial categories, such as SCFA-producing taxa, may be protective across several diseases, whereas specific metabolites such as TUDCA may aggravate bronchiectasis in a disease-specific context.

Gut Barrier Dysfunction and Systemic Microbial Translocation

Intestinal barrier integrity is an important intermediary between gut microbial ecology and distal lung inflammation. Reduced SCFA production, altered bile-acid metabolism, oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines may weaken tight-junction integrity and increase intestinal permeability. Once barrier function is impaired, microbial fragments, soluble metabolites and inflammatory mediators can enter the systemic circulation or lymphatic system and condition bone-marrow myelopoiesis, circulating leukocytes and lung-resident immune responses. In COPD, experimental and clinical studies linking gut microbial alteration with systemic inflammation, gut permeability markers and pulmonary injury support this barrier-centred interpretation.6,14,15,34 However, leaky gut should not be presented as the initiating lesion in every chronic lung disease. It is more appropriately viewed as an amplifier that connects intestinal microbial imbalance with systemic immune activation and pulmonary pathology.

Microbial Fragments, Bile Acids and Neutrophil Programming

Microbial fragments and bile acids illustrate how gut-derived signals can either support or impair pulmonary host defence. Commensal bacteria release peptidoglycan fragments that can translocate at low levels and prime systemic innate immunity. In a seminal mouse study, microbiota-derived peptidoglycan enhanced neutrophil killing of Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus through NOD1 signalling, indicating that tonic microbial sensing can strengthen antibacterial defence.35 In contrast, gut-derived bile-acid signals may have detrimental effects in selected disease contexts. In bronchiectasis, enrichment of Eggerthella lenta and increased TUDCA were linked to impaired neutrophil AMPK signalling, reduced ATP generation and defective Pseudomonas aeruginosa killing.8 These findings show that gut microbial products should not be classified as uniformly protective or harmful. Their effects depend on the producing taxa, host immune state, pulmonary pathogen burden and disease stage.

SCFAs and Th17/Treg Balance

SCFAs, particularly acetate, propionate and butyrate, are among the best-characterised mediators of gut-lung immune regulation. They can act through G-protein-coupled receptors and histone deacetylase inhibition to promote regulatory T-cell differentiation, restrain effector T-cell activation and modulate dendritic-cell and macrophage function. In allergic airway disease, high-fibre diets and propionate supplementation reduced eosinophilic inflammation and Th2 cytokine production by reprogramming bone-marrow haematopoiesis and lung dendritic-cell responses.20 The broader respiratory microbiome literature also supports a relationship between microbial communities and lung immune tone,36 while experimental studies show that butyrate and related SCFAs promote Foxp3+ Treg differentiation and suppress IL-17 and IFN-gamma release in immune-cell systems.21,22 In COPD models, SCFA-related interventions rebalanced Th17/Treg responses and reduced neutrophil-driven inflammation.37,38 In pulmonary fibrosis models, butyrate administration has been associated with lower inflammatory cytokines and reduced collagen deposition.39,40 Nevertheless, most direct evidence remains preclinical, and human studies still need paired metabolomic and immune phenotyping endpoints to determine whether SCFA changes are causal, compensatory or simply disease-associated.

Tryptophan-Derived Indoles, AhR Signalling and Epithelial Barrier Integrity

Microbial metabolism of tryptophan generates indole derivatives that activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a key regulator of mucosal immunity. AhR signalling in innate lymphoid cells and T cells promotes IL-22 production, epithelial repair, antimicrobial peptide expression and barrier maintenance. Lactobacilli and other commensals can produce AhR-active indole metabolites that support mucosal tolerance and colonisation resistance.41 In lung disease models, AhR activation has been linked to increased Treg responses, reduced Th17 or Th1 inflammation and attenuation of bleomycin-induced fibrosis.42 However, tryptophan metabolism is also context dependent. Integrated microbiome and metabolomic analyses in pulmonary fibrosis models showed that increased L-tryptophan may aggravate fibrosis through mTOR/S6-dependent amplification of TGF-beta1 signalling.31 Therefore, the tryptophan-AhR axis should be discussed as a regulatory network with both protective and disease-promoting branches rather than as a uniformly beneficial pathway.

Microbiota-Targeted Interventions

Microbiota-targeted interventions can be arranged by their level of action, from dietary substrates and prebiotics that reshape microbial metabolism, to live microbial products, synbiotic formulations, ecosystem-level replacement by FMT and selected microbiota-modulating compounds. This classification is important because each category has a different mechanism, safety profile and translational readiness. Across chronic lung diseases, preclinical studies are more abundant than clinical trials, and most human data remain limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up, heterogeneous endpoints and incomplete mechanistic readouts. Dietary Fibre, Prebiotics and SCFA-Related Strategies

Dietary fibre and prebiotic strategies aim to increase microbial fermentation and restore SCFA-related immune regulation. In allergic airway disease, fermentable fibre and propionate reduced airway eosinophilia, Th2 cytokines and IgE in mouse models, with effects linked to FFAR3-dependent changes in haematopoiesis and dendritic-cell function.20,51 In adults with asthma, soluble-fibre meal challenges and short-term inulin-type fructan supplementation increased circulating or fecal SCFAs and were associated with improved lung function, asthma control or airway inflammatory markers.7,43 These studies support biological plausibility, but their short duration and limited sample size mean that they cannot yet establish durable clinical efficacy.

In COPD models, high-fibre diets, resistant starch and SCFA supplementation attenuated cigarette-smoke-induced emphysema, airway inflammation, mucus hypersecretion and lung function decline.6,38,45,52 These effects were generally accompanied by increased SCFA production, restoration of microbial carbohydrate metabolism and reduced inflammatory signalling. Related airway-infection models further suggest that fibre- or acetate-driven SCFA signalling can modulate antiviral macrophage and epithelial responses.44,53 Human data remain much more limited. Primary smoking-related human and mouse data, together with review-level synthesis, indicate altered SCFA profiles in COPD-relevant contexts, while ongoing trials are testing whether inulin supplementation can modify systemic metabolism in COPD.52,54,55 Future clinical studies should combine dietary assessment, stool and airway metabolomics, immune phenotyping and exacerbation outcomes to determine whether SCFA-targeted strategies provide sustained benefit.

Probiotics

Probiotics offer a direct but highly strain-dependent approach to modulating the gut-lung axis. In cigarette-smoke-induced COPD models, oral Lactobacillus rhamnosus reduced pulmonary leukocyte influx, inflammatory cytokines and emphysematous remodelling while increasing anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10.46 In PM2.5-induced lung injury and chronic asthma models, probiotic treatment improved lung histology, reduced Th2 or Th17-associated inflammation and partly restored Th17/Treg balance.47,48 Early clinical evidence is encouraging but not definitive. A multistrain probiotic improved functional capacity and reduced gut permeability or inflammatory markers in older adults with COPD, and meta-analytic evidence suggests modest anti-inflammatory effects across COPD studies.34,50 However, existing trials vary in strain composition, dose, treatment duration, baseline diet, concomitant therapy and outcome selection. For this reason, probiotics should be described as candidate adjunctive interventions requiring strain-specific validation rather than as a broadly established therapy for chronic lung disease.

Synbiotics and Combined Biotic Formulations

Synbiotics require separate consideration because they combine live microorganisms with selectively utilised substrates and are not simply equivalent to probiotics. The ISAPP consensus defines synbiotics as formulations containing live microorganisms and substrate(s) selectively utilised by host microorganisms that confer a health benefit on the host.10 In respiratory models, combined prebiotic and probiotic regimens have reduced emphysema-like airspace enlargement or allergic airway inflammation in mice.48,49 Mechanistically, synbiotic formulations may provide both microbial strains and metabolic substrates, thereby increasing the likelihood of SCFA production or immunoregulatory metabolite generation. Nevertheless, the synbiotic evidence base in chronic lung diseases remains mainly preclinical, and future studies should define the microbial strain, substrate, intended metabolic product and immune endpoint before clinical translation.

FMT and Defined Microbial Consortia

FMT provides ecosystem-level replacement of a dysregulated intestinal community and has offered strong causal evidence in animal models. Healthy donor FMT or transfer of microbiota from protected animals attenuated cigarette-smoke-induced emphysema, airway inflammation, apoptosis and lung function decline in COPD models.6,56 Conversely, transfer of bronchiectasis patient microbiota or colonisation with E. lenta aggravated Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection in experimental models.8 In pulmonary fibrosis, horizontal transfer of gut microbiota from a protected mouse substrain reduced mortality and altered pulmonary immune transcriptional programmes.30 These findings support causality but also highlight safety concerns. Unscreened or poorly characterised FMT could transfer deleterious taxa, antimicrobial resistance genes or harmful metabolic functions. Limited clinical respiratory evidence is currently restricted to case reports in severe pneumonia rather than controlled trials in chronic lung diseases.57 Therefore, clinical application in chronic lung disease should remain experimental until donor screening, engraftment durability, disease-specific safety and long-term immune consequences are better defined. In the future, rationally designed microbial consortia may offer a more controlled alternative to whole-stool FMT.

Microbiota-Modulating Compounds

Selected non-living compounds may also modify the gut-lung axis by reshaping microbial communities or microbial metabolism. C-phycocyanin is best discussed in this category rather than alongside broad intervention strategies such as probiotics or FMT. In bleomycin-induced and radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, C-phycocyanin reduced lung inflammation, collagen deposition and fibrosis severity while partially normalising gut and lung microbiota composition.9,33 These findings suggest that microbiota-modulating compounds may influence pulmonary fibrosis through combined anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and microbiome-associated effects. However, current evidence remains preclinical, and it is unclear whether the microbiota changes are primary drivers of protection or secondary consequences of reduced tissue injury.

Disease-Specific Intervention Gaps

The translational readiness of microbiota-targeted interventions differs markedly across diseases. Asthma and COPD have the strongest early clinical evidence, particularly for fibre, prebiotics and selected probiotic regimens, although confirmatory trials with mechanistic endpoints are still needed. Bronchiectasis has compelling mechanistic evidence linking E. lenta-derived TUDCA to neutrophil dysfunction, but direct clinical trials targeting this pathway are lacking. Pulmonary fibrosis evidence remains dominated by animal models involving bleomycin, radiation, microbial transfer or microbiota-modulating compounds. Future intervention studies should therefore be disease-specific rather than assuming that one microbiota-targeted strategy will apply across all chronic lung diseases. Trial designs should include predefined microbial targets, metabolomic readouts, immune-cell phenotyping, pulmonary outcomes and long-term safety monitoring.

Discussion

Cross-Disease Interpretation and Disease-Specific Signatures

The revised synthesis supports a layered gut-lung axis model rather than a single, uniform dysbiosis model. Across asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis and pulmonary fibrosis, gut microbial alterations converge on several shared modules, including reduced fermentative capacity, barrier dysfunction, systemic inflammatory priming and altered T-cell or neutrophil responses. However, the microbial signatures and lung outcomes are not identical. In COPD, fecal microbiota from patients can induce lung inflammation in recipient mice and can aggravate smoke-induced airway pathology, whereas healthy fecal microbial transfer and complex carbohydrates can attenuate emphysema, airway inflammation and lung function decline in experimental COPD.6,14 In asthma, high-fibre feeding and propionate supplementation reshape hematopoiesis and dendritic-cell function to reduce allergic airway inflammation.20 In bronchiectasis, fecal microbiome and metabolome studies show reduced diversity and altered metabolic profiles,26 while concurrent stool-sputum profiling identified clinically relevant gut-lung microbial interaction clusters and validated a Pseudomonas-driven gut-lung relationship in mice.58 In pulmonary fibrosis, gut microbiota composition, fungal overgrowth and tryptophan-related metabolites have been experimentally linked to collagen deposition, IL-17A-related pathology and TGF-beta signalling.29–32 These primary studies support a cross-disease framework, but they also show why the term dysbiosis should be replaced, whenever possible, by specific descriptions of taxa, metabolites, immune pathways and pulmonary phenotypes.

Translational Limitations of Preclinical Gut-Lung Axis Models

The strongest causal evidence for gut-lung communication still comes from mouse experiments, but these findings require cautious translation. Laboratory mice differ from patients in microbial exposure history, diet, housing, respiratory anatomy, immune maturation and baseline microbiota. Even within mice, fecal microbial richness and diversity differ substantially by vendor and genetic background, indicating that experimental microbiota context can alter model behaviour.59 More naturalized mouse systems further show that laboratory mice carrying wild-derived microbiota develop immune and inflammatory responses that can differ from conventional specific-pathogen-free mice and may more closely model some human immune responses.60 Therefore, positive results from OVA- or HDM-induced asthma, cigarette smoke-induced COPD and bleomycin-induced fibrosis should be interpreted as mechanistic proof of concept rather than direct clinical efficacy evidence.

This limitation is particularly important for intervention studies. Fibre supplementation, SCFA administration, probiotics, FMT and microbiota-modulating compounds often produce coherent anti-inflammatory effects in controlled animal systems, but patient populations have more heterogeneous diets, medications, inhaled therapies, antibiotics, comorbidities and environmental exposures. Moreover, animal models reproduce selected components of chronic lung disease, such as type-2 inflammation, smoke-driven neutrophilia or acute fibrotic injury, rather than the full clinical trajectory of asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Future translational studies should therefore combine animal causality experiments with longitudinal human sampling and mechanism-informed clinical endpoints.

Quality of Clinical Evidence and Risk-of-Bias Considerations

The current clinical evidence is encouraging but not yet strong enough to support routine microbiota-targeted therapy in chronic lung disease. In asthma, the 7-day inulin-type fructan cross-over trial reported improvements in asthma control, airway inflammation and gut microbiome composition, but the intervention was short and not designed to test durable exacerbation reduction or long-term disease modification.7 In COPD, the randomized multistrain probiotic trial showed improvements in handgrip strength, functional performance and markers of gut permeability or systemic inflammation, but the study population was limited and the primary outcomes were not lung function decline or exacerbation prevention.34 These primary trials are useful because they include human intervention data, but they also illustrate the major gaps identified by the editor: small samples, short follow-up, heterogeneous formulations and incomplete mechanistic profiling.

For probiotics and synbiotics, strain selection is a major source of heterogeneity. Results obtained with one Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium or multistrain formulation cannot be generalized to all probiotics. Similarly, synbiotic effects depend on whether the paired substrate is actually utilized by the administered microorganism or by resident host microbes. For FMT, donor selection, pathogen screening, engraftment durability, antibiotic exposure and transfer of undesirable metabolic functions remain key safety and reproducibility issues. These concerns are not theoretical. The E. lenta-TUDCA study shows that a gut-derived metabolite can worsen bacterial lung infection by impairing neutrophil function,8 reinforcing the need to evaluate both beneficial and harmful microbial functions before clinical translation.

Gut versus Airway Microbiota and Uncertainty in Metabolite Origin

Another unresolved issue is the anatomical origin of microbial metabolites and immune signals. SCFAs are often interpreted as gut-derived products of anaerobic fermentation, but many studies do not include paired stool, sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage sampling, isotope tracing or compartment-specific microbial manipulation. Airway microbiota can also shift with chronic lung disease, exacerbation, antibiotic treatment and inhaled therapy, and may contribute local microbial metabolites or inflammatory ligands. The bronchiectasis study using concurrent stool and sputum sampling demonstrates the value of paired-compartment designs because it linked gut-lung microbial interaction patterns to exacerbation frequency and radiological severity and then validated a Pseudomonas-associated gut-lung interaction experimentally.58 Similar paired gut-airway multi-omics approaches are needed in COPD, asthma and pulmonary fibrosis to determine whether observed immune effects are driven primarily by gut-derived systemic metabolites, airway-local signals or coordinated changes across both compartments.

Future Directions for Mechanism-Informed Microbiota-Targeted Therapy

Future studies should move from broad microbiota modulation toward defined, disease-endotype-specific strategies. For asthma, this may involve identifying patients with low fermentative capacity or impaired SCFA responses who are most likely to benefit from prebiotic or synbiotic interventions. For COPD, trials should incorporate stool and airway microbiome profiling, metabolomics, systemic immune phenotyping and clinically meaningful endpoints such as exacerbations, lung function trajectories and physical performance. For bronchiectasis, the priority should be to test whether E. lenta abundance, TUDCA production, bile-acid profiles or Pseudomonas-driven gut-lung interaction clusters can predict neutrophil dysfunction and treatment response. For pulmonary fibrosis, microbial and fungal signals should be studied alongside established fibrotic pathways such as TGF-beta, IL-17A, epithelial injury and fibroblast activation. Across all diseases, defined microbial consortia, metabolite mimetics or targeted dietary substrates may ultimately be safer and more reproducible than non-specific FMT, but they require validation in adequately powered, mechanism-informed trials.

Conclusion

Gut microbiota-immune interactions provide a plausible and increasingly evidence-supported framework for understanding systemic contributions to chronic lung diseases. The available data indicate that asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis and pulmonary fibrosis share several gut-lung immune modules while retaining disease-specific microbial and metabolic signatures. Nevertheless, most mechanistic evidence remains preclinical, and current clinical trials are still limited in scale, duration and mechanistic depth. Microbiota-targeted strategies should therefore be viewed as promising adjunctive research directions rather than established treatments. Progress will depend on primary studies that integrate paired gut-airway sampling, metabolomics, immune phenotyping and clinically relevant outcomes, allowing microbial signals to be linked to defined disease endotypes and therapeutic responses.
