In a patient presenting with Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome and pectus carinatum, cardiac rotation rendered conventional RAO and LAO views unreliable during left‐sided accessory pathway ablation. Customized fluoroscopic views with caudal angulation allowed for successful ablation, supporting pre‐procedural CT imaging to plan for patient specific fluoroscopic angles.

1 Introduction

Catheter ablation is the treatment of choice for patients with symptomatic and recurrent atrioventricular re‐entrant tachycardia (AVRT) or Wolff‐Parkinson‐White (WPW) syndrome [1]. Ablation of left‐sided accessory pathways requires access to the mitral annulus, either antegrade via transseptal puncture or retrograde via the aorta. In patients with chest wall deformities, rotation and displacement of the cardiac anatomy can affect catheter navigation and mapping. Previous case reports have focused on catheter ablation in pectus excavatum [2]. To our knowledge, this is the first known case report of catheter ablation in a patient with WPW syndrome and pectus carinatum.

2 Case Details

A 70‐year‐old male with pectus carinatum (Figure 1A–C) and no significant medical history presented with recurrent palpitations and chest discomfort. He reported a heart rate of 200 beats per min. Baseline 12 lead electrocardiogram showed no manifest pre‐excitation (Figure 1D). During an electrophysiology study, a narrow complex supraventricular tachycardia with eccentric atrial activation earliest in CS 5, 6 was induced by atrial extra‐stimulus pacing (Figure 2A). Ventricular overdrive pacing showed a VAV response with PPI‐TCL of 18 ms and Delta VA of 50 ms (Figure 2B,C), favoring orthodromic AVRT utilizing a left accessory pathway. Cardiac anatomy was distorted on the conventional fluoroscopy views (Figure 3A), and a modified view of RAO 30° caudal 13° and LAO 60° caudal 16° was adopted. Initial attempt for retrograde approach was abandoned due to a tortuous aorta (Figure 3B). Right atrial angiography (Figure 3C,D) was performed for better delineation of cardiac anatomy. After successful transseptal puncture, mapping of the mitral annulus was done during RV burst pacing which identified VA fusion at the left posterolateral mitral annulus (Figure 4A). Radiofrequency ablation was performed with a non‐irrigated 4 mm ablation catheter (60 W, 60°C, 60 s) with VA dissociation seen (Figure 4B). Fluoroscopic localisation of the successful ablation site was corresponded to the activation map performed with three‐dimensional (3D) electro‐anatomical mapping (Figure 4C–E). Final inducibility test with atrial burst and extra‐stimulus pacing with isoproterenol infusion 4 mcg/min did not induce any further tachycardia. The patient tolerated the procedure well and remained symptom free at outpatient follow up.

3 Discussion

In pectus excavatum, depression of the anterior chest wall produces lateral displacement and clockwise rotation of the heart [3], leading to right atrial and ventricular compression against the sternum [2]. Pectus carinatum, in contrast, causes an outward protrusion of the sternum and costal cartilages, with minimal cardiac displacement and no impaired cardiopulmonary function [4]. However, it can be associated with aortic root dilation and mitral valve prolapse [5]. In our patient, standard fluoroscopic views and anatomic landmarks were misleading due to significant cardiac rotation. Retrograde approach was challenging due to a tortuous aorta and dilated aortic root. Antegrade approach for catheter ablation was still possible, with customisation of fluoroscopic views and aid of further imaging (right atrial angiography). Our patient's cardiac anatomy was superiorly and medially displaced, and a more caudally directed view was helpful for better visualization of catheter position and ablation site.

The key learning point from our case is the recognition that modified projections would be required, rather than the specific angles we used. Marked cardiac displacement was evident from physical examination and Chest X‐ray, and in retrospect, pre‐procedural computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax to define the degree and pattern of cardiac displacement should have been performed. Cardiac CT is commonly used to assess anatomical variation and left atrial, epicardial and extracardiac structures before atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia ablation [6]. It can also characterize aortic root diameter and its tortuosity, making antegrade approach preferable as first line in our patient.

CT images can be segmented and integrated into 3D electro‐anatomical systems to provide a geometry of the left atrium, mitral annulus and coronary sinus. This is feasible and reliable for guiding catheter navigation and can significantly reduce fluoroscopy times with improved procedural outcome [7, 8]. Where conventional fluoroscopic landmarks are unreliable, image integration replaces the assumed anatomy in standard fluoroscopic views with patient‐specific anatomy. Pre‐procedural cross‐sectional imaging should therefore be considered in patients whose chest wall deformity, marked scoliosis or abnormal cardiac silhouette suggest that cardiac orientation deviates from the norm.

The modified fluoroscopic views used in our case were empirically derived intra‐procedure, at the expense of additional contrast, radiation and procedural time. This need not necessarily be the case. CT coronary angiography is well established for planning of optimal fluoroscopic projections to visualize coronary ostia and proximal bifurcations [9], and multi‐slice CT is used to determine optimal viewing angles of the aortic and mitral annuli in structural intervention [10]. Within electrophysiology, use of CT optimized fluoroscopic angulation to facilitate pulsed field ablation for atrial fibrillation has been reported [11]. Applied to left‐sided accessory pathway ablation, multiplanar reconstruction of the mitral annular plane would allow the en‐face and orthogonal annular projections to be calculated in advance, yielding pre‐specified RAO and LAO angles.

Our patient is an extreme illustration of distorted cardiac anatomy. However, cardiac orientation can vary even between healthy individuals. CT studies have demonstrated mean anatomical cardiac axes can differ by approximately 13° between normal weight and obese individuals [12]; spinal deformities, diaphragmatic positions, lung disease and chamber enlargement can contribute further. In any patient whose cardiac anatomy is atypical, individualized fluoroscopic angulation is better determined during pre‐procedural planning instead of intra‐procedural improvisation.

When CT is not available or contraindicated, intracardiac echocardiogram provides real time visualization of catheters independent of cardiac orientation and can guide transseptal puncture and left‐sided ablation with reduced or no fluoroscopy [13]. Operators should anticipate that procedural times and radiation times may be longer due to the need for additional imaging, repositioning of catheters and difficulty during catheter navigation.

4 Conclusion

This case demonstrates that pectus carinatum can distort cardiac anatomy on fluoroscopy during catheter ablation of left‐sided accessory pathways. When retrograde access is limited by a tortuous aorta, careful fluoroscopic‐guided transseptal puncture using an individualized, more caudally angulated view provides an effective alternative. Pre‐procedural CT imaging, integrated with 3D mapping and customized fluoroscopic views can be considered. Successful ablation remains achievable with meticulous planning, multimodality imaging, and a flexible access strategy.
