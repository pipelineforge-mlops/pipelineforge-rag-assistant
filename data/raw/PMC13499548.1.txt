Purpose

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a global health problem with widespread prevalence, yet treatment rates remain persistently low worldwide. Although the impact of social influence on health behaviors has been well confirmed and proven to play a prominent role, a standardized scale to measure its specific influence on treatment behavior among chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients is still lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a social influence scale to assess its role in patients’ treatment decision-making.

Patients and Methods

The scale was developed through four steps. First, a preliminary scale was developed based on the literature review and internal discussion. Second, two rounds of Delphi expert consultation were conducted for item content validation. Third, a cross-sectional survey involving 611 participants was conducted from March to October 2023 in Fuqing, China, to test the reliability and validity of the preliminary scale. Finally, the scale was assessed by item analysis, internal consistency tests, and exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis.

Results

The final scale consisted of five dimensions and 16 items that explained 90.5% of the total variance. Confirmative factor analysis model showed that the 5-factor model fits the data well (χ2/df=4.266, CFI=0.979, GFI=0.925, and RMSEA=0.073). The Cronbach alpha coefficient of the total scale and the five factors was 0.818 and 0.896–0.970. For convergent validity, AVE was 0.795–0.895, and CR was 0.918–0.971. The dimensions comprised descriptive norm, injunctive norm, social support, discrimination, and prototype.

Conclusion

The scale is valid and reliable to evaluate the impact of social influence on treatment behaviors among CHB patients.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection has emerged as a global health challenge, contributing to approximately 887,000 deaths annually, primarily due to chronic hepatitis B (CHB)-related complications, 52% of which are attributed to cirrhosis and 38% to hepatocellular carcinoma.1–3 The asymptomatic nature of CHB causes many patients to miss the optimal treatment window, leading to severe complications and increased mortality. These factors, in turn, place a significant financial, physical, psychological, and social burden on CHB patients and their families.4 The World Health Organization (WHO) had set a target to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030.5 Despite considerable advances in antiviral therapy for CHB, the global diagnostic rate for hepatitis B remains low at 12.9%, with an even lower treatment rate of 8% in 2022.6 In China, the current diagnosis and treatment rates are slightly better at 22% and 15%, respectively.6

Several factors have been confirmed as barriers to seeking CHB treatment. Constraints in low- and middle-income countries are related to drug availability and cost affordability, whereas challenges in high- and middle-income countries stem from low screening rates, limited disease awareness, social stigma, and discrimination.7 Furthermore, regardless of socioeconomic status, public prejudice and stigma against CHB patients are on the rise, leading to social exclusion, emotional distress, and marginalization, which have a negative impact on the mental health of patients and their adherence to treatment.8,9 In this context, promoting CHB treatment and reducing morbidity and mortality from CHB-related diseases require greater attention to these factors, collectively referred to as social influence.

Social influence arises from subjective uncertainty when an individual experiences disagreement with in-group members.10 It plays a crucial role in individual decision-making behavior, as observed in studies on health care technologies such as telemedicine, electronic health records, and mHealth.11–13 However, some scholars have argued that social influence should not be restricted to one dimension and that its connotation and meaning need to be expanded. Victoria’s research on adolescent smoking intentions highlights multiple types of social influence, such as subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and direct stress.14 Similarly, Hammond et al noted that social capital and social pressure should be considered additional dimensions of social influence.15 Most current studies approach social influence as a subfactor in behavioral decision-making, focusing on components such as injunctive norms, descriptive norms, and social pressure.16–18 Only Harrison developed a social influence scale to examine the impact of multidimensional media on alterations in children’s body image.19 Thus, attention needs to be paid to broadening the multidimensionality of social influence to better assess and quantify its role in health behaviors.

Various theoretical frameworks have been applied to elucidate the mechanisms of behavior, including the attitude-social influence-efficacy (ASE) model, the prototype willingness model (PWM), with each placing emphasis on different determinants.20 The ASE model proposes that social factors, such as social norms, social support, and social discrimination, shape health behaviors by influencing individuals’ self-efficacy and attitudes. This theoretical framework underscores the powerful role of the social environment in driving CHB patients health-related cognitions and actions.21 The PWM model holds that an individual’s behavior is not only determined by rational planning but also influenced by the perception of a specific group’s “prototype”, which, beyond rational consideration, directly or indirectly affects behavioral decisions through the “willingness” path.22 On the one hand, the ASE model incorporates multidimensional social factors including descriptive norms, injunctive norms, social support, and social discrimination. On the other hand, the PWM introduces prototype perceptions to capture the non-rational socio-psychological processes in patients’ treatment decisions. The integration of these two models provides a more comprehensive framework for explaining antiviral therapy behavior among CHB patients.

In the context of CHB treatment, the primary goal of antiviral therapy is to suppress HBV replication and promote improvements in liver pathology. Although medications alone effectively inhibit viral replication, comprehensive management strategies are essential to optimize treatment outcomes, prevent complications, and avoid drug resistance. However, previous research has typically focused on individual components of health behaviors, such as disease screening, medication adherence, or self-care,23–25 rarely integrating these behaviors into holistic health care delivery, even though these ancillary health behaviors are also critical to disease healing. Thus, previous research has been limited to a single dimension and has not fully captured its multidimensional complexity. Additionally, the focus of previous research on CHB treatment has been on medication adherence alone, ignoring the overall synergy of behavior. Existing instruments assessing CHB-related health behaviors have largely focused on singular dimensions (eg, adherence levels or self-management skills), with limited attention given to the multidimensional social factors, such as social support, discrimination, norms, and prototype perceptions that profoundly shape patients’ treatment decisions.26,27 Therefore, developing a dedicated scale for CHB antiviral treatment behavior based on an integrated theoretical framework and covering multiple social influencing factors holds significant theoretical value and practical significance.

Since existing studies have not analyzed the impact of multidimensional social influence on CHB treatment or fully evaluated its effect on comprehensive treatment behavior, this study aimed to develop a scale that integrates various dimensions of social influence relevant to the comprehensive treatment behavior of CHB patients, thereby enhancing theoretical cognition and providing practical guidance to optimize CHB treatment strategies and improve patients’ treatment compliance.

Methods

Study Design

The development of the tool was divided into two phases: scale item development and evaluation.

During the development stage, a multidimensional framework and item scale were initially designed on the basis of a literature review and internal discussion. Two rounds of Delphi expert consultations followed, during which the experts provided detailed feedback and scoring. On the basis of this feedback, the items were refined, resulting in a preliminary questionnaire to be evaluated.

In the evaluation stage, the preliminary questionnaire was dispensed to the CHB patients to complete. For the collected data, an internal consistency assessment was used to ensure stability among items. Factor analysis, including exploratory and confirmatory methods, was performed to verify construct validity.

Scale Development Stage

Item Generation

A comprehensive literature review on social influence and CHB was conducted using the search terms “social influence or social impact”, “hepatitis B” and “healthcare” in Google Scholar, Web of Science, PubMed and so on. By referring to a variety of confirmed scales from previous studies, a 23-item social influence scale for antiviral therapy behavior in CHB patients was initially established with five dimensions: descriptive norms, injunctive norms, social support, stigma and discrimination, and prototypes.

Descriptive norms are defined as the standards or behavior patterns commonly exhibited by members of society in specific contexts. Injunctive norms reflect the expectations of society about an individual’s behavior. The roles of these two norms have been confirmed by research examining the impact of social influence on screening and testing behaviors in individuals with chronic conditions such as CHB or HIV.28,29 Social support encompasses emotional, material, and informational assistance from social networks and intimate partners. It is critical in shaping self-management and health behaviors among individuals with related diseases. For this dimension, we referred to validated scales such as the Mos Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS) for patients with chronic disease and the social support scale in infected populations.30,31 Furthermore, the stigma and discrimination related to HBV are also considered significant barriers to disease detection, treatment access, and the compliance of CHB patients. The items were developed on the basis of prior research on the stigma experienced by pregnant women with AIDS and the discrimination perceived by patients with hepatitis B in rural China.32,33 Finally, the prototype dimension, which assesses the characteristics attributed to groups of individuals with shared traits, was inspired by Gibbons’ framework for measuring the favorability and similarity of risk-related images.34,35 This dimension aims to capture how patients’ perceptions of others undergoing similar treatments can influence their health behaviors and decision-making processes.

Item Refinement

This study invited experts in different fields to participate; details about the basic characteristics of the experts can be found in Table S1. The degree of expert authority was expressed by the expert authority coefficient, which was calculated by the arithmetic mean of the familiarity coefficient and expert judgment coefficient. In the first round, the familiarity coefficient, expert judgment coefficient, and expert authority coefficient were 0.77, 0.95, and 0.86, respectively. The expert response rate was 100%, with a P value for coordination < 0.001 and a Kendall’s Ware of 0.32, indicating moderate agreement among the experts. If items with a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 0.25 were excluded, 7 items, such as “I think patients receiving antiviral therapy are positive”, were removed from the scale. More details about the removed items can be found in Table S2. Experts proposed modifications to some items, such as “Other hepatitis B patients regularly engage in health behavior management (eg, diet, work and rest, exercise),” “When I am in trouble, people around me will provide substantial help,” and the scale was refined accordingly. In the second round, 16 items were re-evaluated, yielding a 95% response rate. The familiarity coefficient, expert judgment coefficient, expert authority coefficient, and Kendall’s Ware were 0.73, 0.91, 0.82, and 0.41, respectively, indicating improved consensus. All the items had a CV less than 0.25, confirming their reliability (shown in Table S3).

Scale Evaluation Stage

Survey Questionnaire

After item refinement and modification, the surveyed questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part collected sociodemographic information, covering age, gender, education, marital status, and income. The second part comprised the social influence scale for CHB patients, which encompasses five dimensions with 16 items: descriptive norms, injunctive norms, social support, discrimination, and prototypes. All the items were evaluated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” (Supplementary Questionnaire).

Data Collection

The questionnaire survey was conducted among CHB patients from March to October 2023 in Fuqing city, Fujian Province, China. All participants were recruited from a local health screening program for HBV-infected populations conducted by medical institutions in Fuqing city. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) CHB diagnosed by a licensed physician or clinical examination; (ii) Patients with a history of hepatitis B or those who have been HBsAg positive for more than 6 months and/or HBV DNA positive, regardless of whether they have received antiviral treatment; (iii) no history of mental illness, with basic cognitive and comprehension abilities; and (iv) voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (i) history of neurological disorders or major psychiatric illnesses; (ii) severe cognitive impairment or hearing loss interfering with normal communication; (iii) concurrent other liver diseases (eg, autoimmune liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced liver injury). When screening the questionnaire items and evaluating the measurement performance, the sample size was estimated at 5 to 10 times the number of analyzed items.36 To meet the study’s requirements, at least 160 valid questionnaires were needed for analysis. Before the participants completed the questionnaire, the purpose and significance of this study were explained in detail to guarantee their full comprehension and voluntary participation. During the questionnaire filling process, the investigators promptly attended to the participants’ needs and provided standardized guidance to those experiencing difficulties. They also recorded abnormal situations such as interruption in filling, collected feedback from the participants on the questionnaire, and further reviewed the quality of questionnaire filling. All participants were informed about the study’s objectives, data usage, and confidentiality obligations, and written informed consent was obtained prior to completing the scales. Data Analysis

Descriptive analyses of quantitative variables were conducted using absolute frequencies and percentages. Cronbach’s α was used to test reliability and internal consistency, with values greater than 0.7 indicating good reliability.30,31 The Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test were used to assess whether the sample was suitable for factor analysis. The KMO index, which surpasses the recommended threshold of 0.7, was considered to reach an acceptable level of suitability.37,38 Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal component analysis was performed to extract factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. A cumulative explained variance of more than 50% indicated that the scale had good structural validity, and a commonality higher than 0.5 suggested that each item explained its respective variable well.39

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to verify convergent and discriminant validity. Indices such as χ2/df, RMSEA, CFI, and AGFI were used to test the model’s fit with the expected structure. Convergent validity was evaluated by factor loadings, average variance extracted (AVE), and composite reliability (CR), while the square root of the AVE was used to assess discriminant validity. The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and AMOS 24.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY), and a P value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Low participation rates severely challenge antiviral therapy for CHB patients. Although dimensions such as social support and discrimination affect individual treatment behavior, few studies have yet systematically investigated these dimensions in an integrated manner to reveal the complex mechanisms and interrelationships of their influence on treatment behavior. Therefore, this study developed and conducted the initial validation of a social influence scale for CHB patients.

In this research, Delphi expert consultations and literature research were conducted to establish an initial social influence scale for CHB patients. We combined the disease characteristics of CHB with the psychological features of patients and fully drew on the attitude–social influence–efficacy (ASE) model and the prototype willingness (PWM) model. The team, comprising 20 experts from a range of fields, demonstrates a high level of scientific research literacy and proficiency. The results of the expert consultation demonstrated that the degree of expert participation in this research was very high, and the opinions of the experts were concentrated and reliable. In accordance with the insights provided by the experts, the scale encompassed five distinct dimensions: descriptive norm, injunctive norm, social support, discrimination, and prototype, and 7 items were suggested to be deleted. In addition, a total of 16 items were utilized during onsite investigations to further verify the reliability and validity of the scale, with item analysis, internal consistency testing, EFA, and CFA. The results demonstrated that all 16 items were retained. Consequently, the development process of this scale was scientific and standardized.

The items measuring social support, discrimination, and other dimensions were validated using established scales, such as Feng’s Social Discrimination Scale, with Cronbach’s α greater than 0.7, indicating high internal consistency. The reliability of the five dimensions was greater than 0.8, indicating excellent internal consistency. For the validity test, all the items were loaded into the dimensions of social influence separately, and the convergent and discriminant validity were distinguishable, which indicated that the scale had good reliability and validity.

In terms of social influence, the scale accurately captures five core dimensions: descriptive norms, injunctive norms, social support, discrimination, and prototypes. These dimensions align with the scale’s initial design objectives and offer a nuanced understanding of the social factors shaping CHB patients’ treatment decisions.28,29

As key aspects of the scale, descriptive and injunctive norms elucidate the role of societal attitudes and normalized behaviors in influencing patients’ choices. In the context of Chinese culture, descriptive norms manifest in patients’ tendency to refer to the behaviors of those around them when deciding whether to adopt antiviral therapy. Particularly in rural China, the descriptive norm that “Western medicine harms the liver while Chinese medicine eradicates the root cause” carries such strong inertia that it can lead patients to disregard physicians’ injunctive norms recommending antiviral treatment, thereby influencing their therapeutic choices.28 This folk adage represents a deeply entrenched grassroots social consensus shaped by long-standing local experience, featuring prominent path dependence that renders such ingrained perceptions resistant to easy transformation. Meanwhile, injunctive norms are primarily embodied in family role obligations, where physicians’ advice takes a secondary position, and the responsibility of “being a parent” or “being a child” serves as the supreme behavioral guideline. Family responsibility is more binding on behavior than medical advice. When treatment decisions may compromise family economic security, even medically recommended regimens may be perceived by relatives as “unfilial” or “irresponsible,” and such social evaluative pressure further profoundly affects patients’ treatment decision-making and behavioral pathways. Rather than merely reflecting attitudes and behaviors, they actively shape the context in which patients make their decisions. An individual’s disease treatment behavior often involves not only personal decisions but also important social events involving the family and the entire intimate social circle. Thus, patients are often strongly influenced by the social networks to which they belong, and this influence largely determines their treatment choices.

Social support, which encompasses both material and emotional aid from one’s social network, reinforces patients’ social ties, provides access to treatment resources, and fosters a sense of belonging and community identity, which is crucial for improving treatment adherence.40

In addition, the modeling behavior of the prototype or the reference group of patients significantly contributes to the treatment behavior of CHB patients. Many patients need a model to help them make treatment decisions due to the severity of the disease they are suffering from or the uncertainty of treatment outcomes.41 If they are able to interact with others who have had a similar treatment experience with good results, they may make a treatment decision because of that group image.

Finally, discrimination, a salient feature of the scale, underscores the detrimental effects of stigma on the willingness of patients with CHB to seek treatment. In Asian and American immigrant populations, the stigma associated with CHB often leads patients to avoid HBV care, thereby limiting their access to testing and creating barriers to treatment and recovery. By incorporating this dimension, the scale offers a more holistic view of the social factors that influence the treatment behaviors of patients with CHB.42,43 Integrating the discrimination dimension with descriptive norms, injunctive norms, social support and prototype within a unified framework allows for a multi-perspective explanation of the mechanisms through which discrimination translates into treatment behavior deviations. Compared with the existing scales that predominantly focus on discrimination experiences, this scale directly anchors antiviral treatment behaviors, and the results are more targeted and have intervention value.44

Receiving emotional encouragement during low moods and tangible support amid hardships markedly boosts patients’ self-efficacy and strengthens their resolve to sustain long-term treatment. Perceiving support makes patients feel they are not alone, fostering positive attitudes toward therapy.20 Conversely, discrimination disrupts treatment via behavioral avoidance. Fearing disease disclosure at healthcare visits or loss of employment or educational opportunities, patients actively conceal their condition, discontinue follow-ups.45 In terms of prototypes influencing behavior, the rational path shapes behavior through behavioral intentions, while the social reaction path directly drives behavior via behavioral willingness. Positive prototype evaluations of antiviral therapy recipients (eg, brave, mature) and strong perceived self-similarity significantly elevate patients’ self-efficacy and treatment attitudes, ultimately boosting treatment motivation and uptake.46 Descriptive norms significantly enhance patients’ treatment attitudes by providing the social reference that “most other CHB patients adhere to regular follow-ups and health management,” while injunctive norms strengthen patients’ perception of role obligations and treatment attitudes through the explicit expectations of significant others (especially family members) regarding “taking medication on time and attending regular follow-ups.”

The value of the social impact scale for antiviral treatment behaviors of CHB patients developed in this research lies not only in providing a scientific and systematic measurement tool, but also in incorporating the neglected risk dimension of social factors into the policy perspective of chronic disease management. This study provides empirical evidence for the formulation of precision intervention strategies, facilitates the integration of social determinants into the hepatitis prevention and treatment policy framework, and, by considering the profound influence of diverse social factors within the Chinese cultural context throughout the scale development process, offers a methodological reference for cross-national and cross-cultural policy adaptation.

This study has some innovative points. Firstly, to understand the characteristics of the treatment behavior of CHB patients, as well as the role of social influence on individual decision-making or behavior, this study sorted out and integrated the possible dimensions of social influence with universal coverage, such as social support, prototypes, and discrimination, which will provide a valuable reference for subsequent studies related to social influence. Secondly, the scale is constructed based on the ASE model and the PWM model, which are widely recognized internationally. The core structure of the scale has the theoretical basis of cross-cultural transfer, and the items of the scale cover multi-dimensional structures. Thirdly, the scale was constructed on the basis of the treatment behaviors of CHB patients, which can fully reflect the treatment characteristics of this group and is measured in terms of medication-taking behaviors, follow-up visits, and self-management behaviors, which is more convincing than measuring medication adherence alone. However, this study has some limitations. First, participants in this study were recruited solely from Fuqing City, Fujian Province, China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should validate the scale in larger and more diverse populations. Second, differences in healthcare policies and medical accessibility, along with culturally specific items, may restrict the scale’s direct applicability in other cultural contexts. Therefore, rigorous translation and full psychometric validation, including cross-cultural measurement invariance testing, are recommended before use in other countries. Third, given the limitations of the Delphi method, the experts in this study were selected with a specific purpose rather than randomly, which may raise some concerns about the representativeness of the invited experts.

Conclusion

This study enriches the dimensions of social influence and validates a social influence scale for antiviral therapy behavior in CHB patients. This not only reveals the multilevel and multidimensional characteristics of social influence but also facilitates further exploration of the complex intertwined mechanisms and correlations between social influence and treatment behavior in CHB patients, providing a tool to support further study of the mechanism of how social influence affects behavior. Future research should focus more on the dimension of social influence, comprehensively and systematically exploring its role in affecting behavior.
