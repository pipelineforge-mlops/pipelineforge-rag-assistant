Background

The remarkable advances achieved in Parkinson's disease (PD) research across clinical science, genetics, molecular and cellular mechanisms, disease modeling, pathophysiology, biomarkers, and therapeutics reflect not only scientific innovation but also the evolution of collaborative research. The field has transitioned from single-investigator observations and isolated institutional efforts to regional networks and global consortia that now drive many of the most important PD discoveries.

Objective

To review the evolution of collaborative research approaches in PD, examine barriers and challenges to global research synergy, and discuss potential solutions to promote equitable and impactful international collaboration.

State of the Art and Future Directions

Key challenges to global PD research include funding inequities, regulatory and data-sharing complexities, difficulties in standardization and generalizability, language barriers, limited interdisciplinary communication, unequal resource allocation, insufficient training opportunities, regional mistrust, and the need to address diverse cultural contexts. Successful international initiatives have demonstrated the value of shared databases, harmonized protocols, cross-border funding mechanisms, and synergistic multidisciplinary, multicultural, and multinational partnerships. Future efforts should be grounded in trust, transparency, ethical reciprocity, equitable governance, and meaningful stakeholder engagement, while prioritizing capacity building, workforce development, and sustainable training opportunities.

Conclusions

Accelerating progress in PD research requires collaborative frameworks that integrate diverse populations, disciplines, institutions, and regions. Strengthening research infrastructure, community engagement, culturally grounded education, participant-centered research practices, and scientific leadership, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, will be essential to ensure that all populations can contribute to and benefit from scientific advances, fostering a more equitable and effective global PD research ecosystem.

Plain Language Summary Title

Working Together to Advance Parkinson's Disease Research Worldwide

Plain language summary

Many of the advances that have improved the lives of people living with Parkinson's disease (PD) did not happen because of the efforts of a single scientist or institution. Instead, progress has increasingly depended on collaboration between researchers, healthcare professionals, patients, families, advocacy organizations, industry partners, and governments worldwide.

This article explores how PD research has evolved from small, local efforts to large international collaborations. Early discoveries were often made by individual researchers or single research centers. Today, answering key questions about PD requires thousands of participants, specialized technologies, and expertise from multiple disciplines and countries. Global initiatives have helped researchers better understand the causes of PD, identify genetic risk factors, develop biomarkers, and accelerate the search for new treatments.

However, important challenges remain. Many regions still have limited access to funding, infrastructure, training opportunities, and advanced technologies. Differences in language, regulations, data-sharing policies, and cultural practices can also complicate international collaboration. Many communities remain underrepresented in research, limiting knowledge of PD across populations.

Drawing on lessons learned from successful international research networks, this article highlights practical approaches to strengthen global collaboration. These include sharing data and resources, harmonizing research methods, investing in training and capacity building, promoting transparent and equitable partnerships, and ensuring meaningful participation of patients and communities throughout the research process.

The future of PD research depends on building a global, inclusive research community. By working together across disciplines, cultures, and regions, we can accelerate scientific discovery, improve access to innovation, and ensure that the benefits reach the global PD community.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) represents a major neurodegenerative challenge, with a pooled crude prevalence of 516 cases per 100,000 inhabitants in low- and middle-income countries, characterized by significant regional variability and a marked age-dependent increase.1,2 Despite major milestones in PD research, ranging from early clinical observations and the discovery of dopaminergic dysfunction to advances in biomarkers, genetics, pharmacological therapies, and neuromodulation strategies such as deep brain stimulation, an effective disease-modifying therapy remains elusive. 3

This collaborative shift has become essential to meet the need for larger, diverse cohorts, complementary expertise, harmonized methodologies, and greater inclusion of underrepresented populations in PD research.3–5 Furthermore, transitioning toward a systems-level understanding requires a synergistic approach integrating diverse molecular, structural, and behavioral datasets to bridge the gap between benchside pathophysiology and clinical efficacy.3,6

Research synergy is defined as the collaborative integration of diverse expertise, resources, data, technologies, and stakeholders to generate scientific and translational impact exceeding what is achievable through isolated research efforts. This approach transcends standard multidisciplinary collaboration, encompassing global consortia and the active engagement of diverse stakeholders, including academia, the pharmaceutical industry, private foundations, and patient associations.7,8

Although genetics represents the primary area of expertise of our research group, this review aims to provide a concise overview of the major domains of fundamental PD research that have been substantially advanced through international collaboration. We examine the evolution of collaborative PD research, summarize key advances across fundamental and translational research enabled by global partnerships, analyze the challenges and opportunities of international research synergy, and propose strategies to foster equitable, sustainable, and impactful collaborations that accelerate scientific discovery while reducing global disparities in PD research.

The evolving landscape of global PD research collaboration

The history of PD research reflects the progressive evolution of scientific collaboration itself (Table 1). Early landmark discoveries were largely driven by individual physician-scientists or single academic centers, exemplified by James Parkinson's seminal 1817 clinical description of the “shaking palsy,” which established the clinical foundations of the disease.9,10 As biomedical science advanced during the twentieth century, discoveries increasingly emerged from small multidisciplinary teams integrating clinical neurology, pathology, pharmacology, and neuroscience. Friedrich Heinrich Lewy's identification of Lewy bodies in 1912 exemplifies an era in which major advances in Parkinson's disease research were predominantly driven by individual investigators and single-center observations. 11

Other notable examples include the identification of striatal dopamine deficiency and the subsequent development of levodopa therapy, achievements that transformed Parkinson's disease from a purely descriptive disorder into a treatable neurochemical condition.12–14 Subsequent decades witnessed the rise of institutional and regional collaborative networks, enabling the development and refinement of complex interventions such as deep brain stimulation which required coordinated expertise across neurology, neurosurgery, neurophysiology, and engineering.15,16 The evolution of neuroimaging biomarkers has closely paralleled advancements in neuroimaging technology, progressing from structural computed tomography and basic MRI protocols to sophisticated volumetric and functional imaging modalities, a transition that has necessitated both multidisciplinary teams and advanced technological infrastructure.5,17

The trajectory of genetic discoveries in PD closely mirrors the evolution of research methodology itself, from isolated, single-center observations to expansive, high-impact global consortia-driven science. Initial breakthroughs emerged from the study of informative families, leading to the identification of causal genes such as SNCA and LRRK2. Subsequently, large-scale case–control studies and genome-wide association studies uncovered numerous common risk loci, establishing Parkinson's as a complex genetic disorder. 18 More recently, the field of Parkinson's disease research has embraced an era of global consortia and large-scale international initiatives, driven by the need for massive sample sizes, multimodal datasets, and the inclusion of ancestrally diverse populations. 8 This expansion spans multiple levels of inquiry, from cell and animal models to organoids, and integrates genetics, disease mechanisms, biomarkers, and diverse therapeutic approaches, including clinical trials, through unprecedented international collaboration.5,19–21

Successful examples of translational research that have led to impactful clinical applications

Translational PD research has long been underpinned by two primary biological paradigms: α-synuclein aggregation and dopaminergic neurodegeneration. These concepts have fundamentally shaped contemporary disease models, informed the development of molecular biomarkers, and guided therapeutic strategies, including circuit-based interventions like deep brain stimulation.22,23 Over time, global collaborations have changed this translational pipeline by integrating multi-ancestry data, multi-omics, biomarker cohorts, and shared data platforms.24,25 International networks are actively driving a paradigm shift in the therapeutic development away from empirical protocols toward interventions matched to genetic ancestry and individual disease biology.26,27 Several collaborative initiatives drive progress in global PD research, bridging diverse resources across global and regional genomic projects, longitudinal tracking networks, biomarker discovery networks, and clinical trial infrastructure, and participant-centered practices such as meaningful return of results.

Decades of international collaborative research have transformed our understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying PD. Although the disease is biologically heterogeneous, converging evidence has identified several interconnected pathogenic processes, including α-synuclein misfolding and cell-to-cell propagation, mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic failure, impairment of the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy–lysosome pathways, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and neuroinflammation, all contributing to the progressive degeneration of selectively vulnerable dopaminergic neurons.28,29 Defining these biological pathways has represented a major milestone in PD research, shifting the field from descriptive neuropathology toward mechanism-based therapeutic development. This progress has directly informed the development of disease-modifying strategies, including α-synuclein-targeted immunotherapies, lysosomal therapies targeting the GBA1 pathway, and LRRK2 kinase inhibitors, illustrating how collaborative fundamental research continues to drive translational advances.30–33

The biological basis of this selective vulnerability has likewise been clarified through international collaboration.. Neuropathological studies established that degeneration preferentially affects dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta, the same tissue in which α-synuclein was first identified as the principal component of Lewy bodies, and where its progressive distribution through interconnected neural circuits was subsequently characterized.34,35 These studies revealed intrinsic features that confer these neurons particularly susceptible to degeneration, including their extensive axonal arborization, autonomous calcium-dependent pacemaking activity, and exceptionally high bioenergetic demands.36,37 More recently, collaborative efforts using patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells, midbrain organoids, and post-mortem brain tissue have enabled mechanistic investigation of these processes in human models while advancing our understanding of pathological spread across basal ganglia networks.34,36,37 However, many of these experimental resources remain disproportionately derived from individuals of European ancestry, underscoring the need for globally representative brain banks and cellular repositories. Emerging international initiatives in underrepresented populations are beginning to address this gap. 38 These collaborative efforts have not only advanced our understanding of PD pathogenesis and informed circuit-based therapies, such as deep brain stimulation related approaches. 39

The Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI), an international longitudinal cohort spanning 33 clinical sites across 12 countries, has provided a standardized framework for biomarker validation through longitudinal clinical assessment, neuroimaging, and biospecimen collection.40,41 Its most influential output to date has been the large-scale evaluation of the cerebrospinal fluid α-synuclein seed amplification assay (αSyn-SAA), which has demonstrated promising diagnostic performance and suggests the potential to identify molecular subtypes of disease as well as α-synuclein pathology in prodromal and at-risk individuals before the onset of motor symptoms. 42 These results have catalyzed efforts toward a biological definition of Parkinson's disease and contributed to the 2024 US Food and Drug Administration Letter of Support encouraging use of the assay in clinical trials.43,44 However, these findings remain an area of active investigation, and further validation is needed to establish the assay's clinical utility, reproducibility across populations, and role in routine diagnostic and prognostic workflows.

The Parkinson's Disease Biomarkers Program (PDBP) has been established by the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke at National Institutes of Health (NINDS-NIH), represents a landmark collaborative effort to accelerate biomarker discovery through the harmonized collection of longitudinal clinical, imaging, genetic, and biospecimen data across multiple centers. By providing an openly accessible research platform, the PDBP has facilitated the validation of candidate fluid biomarkers, the characterization of disease heterogeneity and progression trajectories, and the development of integrated approaches for patient stratification and clinical trial readiness. 45 In addition, the Swedish BioFINDER studies apply a similar multimodal strategy to neurodegenerative disease, advancing biomarker discovery and disease stratification. Within Parkinson's disease research, BioFINDER has been instrumental in validating cerebrospinal fluid and plasma biomarkers—including α-synuclein, neurofilament light chain, and more recently phosphorylated tau species—while simultaneously defining biological subtypes and predicting disease progression through a comprehensive multimodal approach. 46

The Accelerating Medicines Partnership for Parkinson's Disease Knowledge Platform is an outstanding example of cross-sector synergy: a public–private partnership uniting government, philanthropy, and industry that integrates whole genome sequencing, transcriptomics, proteomics, and harmonized clinical data from more than 10,000 participants across multiple cohorts into a single openly accessible resource. 47 By bringing together databases that were siloed datasets, this platform has become foundational infrastructure on which other consortia, including The Global Parkinson's Genetics Program (GP2) and many other regional consortia, build their analyses, expanding training and research opportunities.

GP2, funded by the Aligning Science Across Parkinson's initiative and implemented through Michael J. Fox Foundation has transformed PD genetics into a global, multi-ancestry research infrastructure. Launched in 2020 to scale variant discovery and correct demographic imbalances, the consortium has more than 400 cohorts across 84 international locations worldwide.24,25 To date, GP2 has identified several novel genetic risk factors, including ancestry-specific discoveries such as GBA1 rs3115534-G in African-ancestry populations, 48 novel loci from Latin American, Indian, and South African cohorts, and candidate causal variants such as RAB32 Ser71Arg. 49 Importantly, GP2's translational value extends beyond variant discovery: its cloud-based platforms, harmonized pipelines, strong global training network, and capacity-building framework provide a model for linking genetic discovery, reproducibility, and future precision medicine in Parkinson's disease. 25

At the regional level, several collaborative initiatives have driven significant advancements in Parkinson's disease research across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. In Africa, the International Parkinson Disease Genomics Consortium–Africa—a collaboration comprising institutions from 12 African nations alongside partners in the United Kingdom and the United States—led to the discovery of the GBA1 rs3115534-G variant, representing the first major PD risk factor identified specifically within African ancestry.48,50 Complementary efforts, such as TraPCAf 51 and AfrAbia+, 52 are further expanding genetic, epidemiological, and therapeutic research across the continent. In Asia, the East Asian PD Genomics Consortium and the Central Asian and Transcaucasian PD Consortium have established large-scale genomic and biobanking platforms, facilitating the study of ancestry-specific genetic risk factors and clinical phenotypes in historically underrepresented populations. 53 In Latin America, the Latin American Research Consortium on the Genetics of Parkinson's Disease (LARGE-PD), which unites more than 45 institutions across 15 countries, has demonstrated that polygenic risk scores derived from European populations yield reduced accuracy in admixed Latin American cohorts, while also identifying novel PD-associated loci, including variants in ITPKB.54,55 These initiatives underscore how regional collaboration and ancestral diversity are essential for uncovering biological insights that may be overlooked in predominantly European-centered studies.

PD GENEration is an outstanding example of translation from genomic discovery to patient-facing implementation. This international research initiative, supported by the Parkinson's Foundation, offers certified genetic testing and genetic counseling at no cost to people diagnosed with PD, with results returned during a genetic counseling visit; more than 8000 PD participants have received results. 56 The initiative demonstrates how global research synergy can integrate variant discovery with patient engagement and local capacity building.

The global Parkinson's disease clinical trial landscape has diversified across multiple therapeutic modalities. For symptomatic management, recent multi-country trials have validated the dopamine agonist tavapadon (in the TEMPO-3 Phase 3 trial) and continuous subcutaneous infusions of foslevodopa-foscarbidopa to improve management of motor fluctuations.57–60 The neuromodulation toolbox has also expanded, with deep brain stimulation, adaptive deep brain stimulation, remote programming, and MR-guided focused ultrasound offering progressively refined options for motor symptom control.61–63 Disease-modifying trials have transitioned to advanced clinical phases, targeting α-synuclein via monoclonal antibodies, inhibiting leucine-rich repeat kinase 2, and targeting GBA1-related pathways with venglustat or ambroxol.30,32,33,64–66 Meanwhile, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, such as liraglutide and exenatide, have shown mixed clinical efficacy.67,68 Regenerative approaches have advanced into clinical trials using human embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cell-derived dopaminergic progenitors.69,70 Other emerging modalities include gene therapies that deliver neurotrophic factors such as GDNF and BDNF via nanoparticle, microparticle, and hydrogel systems, alongside antioxidant and microbiome-directed strategies that address the gut–brain axis in PD pathogenesis.71,72 Current advances in PD genetics have catalyzed a shift toward precision medicine, exemplified by the development of gene-targeted clinical trials focused on pathways involving GBA1 and LRRK2; although these approaches hold considerable promise, significant challenges remain regarding their validation, accessibility, implementation, and integration into routine clinical practice. 73

Collectively, advances in molecular pathology, biomarker discovery, neuroimaging, and human genetics have transformed our understanding of PD, getting to the proposal of a biological rather than purely clinical definition of the disorder. A key enabler has been the maturation of α-synuclein seed amplification assays, which detect pathological α-synuclein in cerebrospinal fluid during life. 42 Building on these advances, two complementary research frameworks proposed in 2024, the Neuronal α-Synuclein Disease Integrated Staging System (NSD-ISS) 74 and the SynNeurGe classification 75 propose a new PD definition by integrating evidence of pathological α-synuclein, dopaminergic neurodegeneration, and genetic susceptibility rather than relying solely on clinical manifestations. Although these frameworks represent an important step toward precision medicine, the Movement Disorder Society has highlighted the risk of creating a two-tier diagnostic system determined by geography and resource availability, as current definitions depend on cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers and dopamine transporter imaging, technologies that remain inaccessible to many patients worldwide. 76

These collaborative initiatives exemplify a strategic shift in PD research toward leveraging international partnerships to accelerate genetic and biomarkers discovery, deepen the understanding of disease heterogeneity, and foster the inclusion of historically underrepresented populations. Collectively, they have generated discoveries, mechanistic insights, diagnostic tools, and biologically informed therapeutic strategies that would have been unattainable through isolated efforts.

Challenges when conducting synergistic international research

Funding disparities, data sharing, and complexity for standardization and broad generalizability

Despite the achievements described above, coordinating PD research across borders remains challenging (Figure 1). A fundamental obstacle is the stark disparity in research funding and resources. While low and middle-income countries account for approximately 85% of the world's population, sub-Saharan Africa represents only about 1% of global health spending, whereas the United States alone accounts for roughly 41.7%. 77 These asymmetries are mirrored in research output; recent analyses indicate that more than 85% of participants in genome-wide association studies have been conducted in populations of European ancestry.78,79 Although the cost of whole-genome sequencing has fallen significantly, it remains prohibitively expensive for the vast majority of patients in underfunded public healthcare systems, who are often forced to pay for molecular diagnostics entirely out of pocket. 80 Consequently, investigators in these settings are frequently relegated to the role of data contributors rather than scientific leaders, thereby perpetuating dependence on funders and institutions in high-income countries.

Overlapping data-sharing approaches and frequently misaligned regulatory frameworks, compounded by the burden of negotiating material and data transfer agreements, often delay collaborations and disadvantage institutions lacking specialized legal support. Furthermore, conflicting local data privacy mandates and strict regional barriers regarding the cross-border flow of sensitive genetic data create persistent operational friction when integrating local phenotypic registries into global, cloud-based platforms as occurs in genomics-related research.81,82

Achieving methodological standardization and broad generalizability remains complex. A recent systematic review of 78 α-synuclein seed amplification assay studies identified substantial inter-laboratory heterogeneity in reaction conditions and positivity thresholds, underscoring the lack of a universally validated standard. 44 Similarly, while recent PD biomarker initiatives have yielded significant advances, their reliance on predominantly European ancestry cohorts limits the generalizability of biomarker thresholds and calibration models to global populations.40,76,83 When harmonization remains incomplete even among well-resourced laboratories, the obstacles facing under-resourced settings are disproportionately greater. These disparities extend to clinical trials; fewer than 30% of recent PD drug trials have been conducted in the Western Pacific Region, while fewer than 3% and 5% occurred in the African and Eastern Mediterranean Regions, respectively, thereby limiting both the generalizability of results and equitable access to emerging therapies for the populations that increasingly bear the global disease burden.83,84

Language barriers and limited horizontal communication

Beyond material constraints, international PD research is shaped by linguistic and relational barriers that are often underrecognized in high-income settings. Global biomedical science operates predominantly in English, encompassing training materials, regulatory documentation, grant mechanisms, peer review, and professional networking. 85 This dominance can restrict the capacity of non-native English-speaking investigators to lead international projects, publish findings, secure funding, and participate fully in global governance. Furthermore, linguistic disparities influence research methodology; cognitive, functional, and quality-of-life assessments designed in high-income settings necessitate rigorous translation, cultural adaptation, and local validation to ensure validity across diverse linguistic, educational, and sociocultural contexts.83,86

Despite substantial progress, many international collaborations continue to face criticism for perpetuating inequitable research “colonialism type” paradigms, wherein institutions from high-income countries typically lead and coordinate projects, while partners from underrepresented regions are often relegated to peripheral roles in participant recruitment and data collection. These systemic imbalances are reinforced by disparities in resource allocation, language barriers, limited inclusion in decision-making, unequal access to capacity-building, and hierarchical governance structures.83,87,88 Direct scientific exchange among investigators in Latin America, Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia remains restricted, despite their shared challenges regarding infrastructure, ethics, recruitment, sample processing, and follow-up. Strengthening regional and interregional collaborations among developing countries, establishing regional scientific forums, creating multilingual training environments, and fostering peer-mentoring networks are essential steps to reduce dependency on high-income intermediaries and empower locally driven research agendas.89–91

Trust, cultural context, and research equity

Global health research has long contended with concerns about extractive or “helicopter” science, in which samples and data from underrepresented communities are used with little provision for sharing results, authorship equity, capacity building, or local benefit. 83 Mere inclusion of authors from these regions is insufficient if investigators from these regions are not empowered to lead analyses, author manuscripts, and drive long-term research agendas. 91 Cultural factors also significantly shape participation in clinical research, as occurs in genetic and biomarker PD-related research. Limited genetic literacy, social stigma, apprehension about discrimination or family disruption, and uncertainty about the utility of testing may reduce willingness to participate or to disclose results within family networks.80,91 However, underrepresentation should not be ascribed solely to mistrust. Simultaneously, the limited availability of clinical and cognitive instruments adapted to local languages and cultural contexts introduces measurement bias that disadvantages underrepresented populations. The validation of the Brazilian Indigenous Cognitive Assessment in Amazonian communities illustrates how culturally grounded, locally partnered tools can mitigate this disparity. 92 Community-based studies indicate that practical, modifiable barriers, such as limited awareness of research opportunities, prohibitive transportation costs, lengthy study visits, insufficient compensation, and invasive procedures can strongly influence participation. 93

Potential solutions for fostering global collaboration

Developing equitable and sustainable international collaborations is essential for advancing PD research in an increasingly globalized landscape. Large-scale consortia, supported by shared databases, harmonized protocols, and cross-border funding, have underscored the efficacy of team science in tackling complex scientific challenges that exceed the capabilities of individual centers. Current global PD research initiatives, such as GP2, 25 PPMI,40,41 and PDBP, 45 exemplify how a consortium can combine centralized infrastructure with distributed leadership, embedding contribution-based authorship norms, transparent governance, and an explicit mandate to empower investigators from underrepresented regions. The meaningful integration of low- and middle-income countries is crucial for enhancing population diversity, improving external validity, and mitigating global disparities in research participation. Beyond traditional North–South frameworks, regional and interregional collaborations among developing countries offer vital opportunities to leverage shared epidemiological, socioeconomic, and healthcare contexts, thereby fostering locally relevant research agendas and strengthening regional capacity.25,55,94,95 The LARGE-PD consortium demonstrates how such regional consortia can self-organize across multiple countries, relaying decisions and initiatives to group members rather than relying solely on a coordinating center, which generates discoveries of global relevance while building durable regional networks. 55

As summarized in Figure 1, successful global research networks rely on principles of equity, ethics, and transparency, supported by distributed leadership, inclusive governance, shared databases, harmonized protocols, cross-border funding mechanisms, and a strong commitment to training and capacity building. Equally important are participant-centered study designs, culturally grounded education, and meaningful community engagement. This collaborative ecosystem can accelerate discoveries and translate them into tangible clinical benefits for individuals living with PD worldwide.8,96–98 Addressing these challenges necessitates a concerted effort to foster local capacity building in less privileged regions. This requires investment that goes beyond data sharing and digital infrastructure, extending to the development of physical facilities, the delivery of sustained specialized training for local clinicians and researchers, and the provision of essential diagnostic and laboratory equipment. 4 By fostering sustainable local hubs of expertise, the scientific community can empower regional institutions to lead research agendas that are culturally competent and geographically relevant. Such empowerment is not merely a matter of equity but a scientific imperative, as it facilitates the integration of diverse environmental and clinical perspectives essential to addressing the multifactorial complexity of PD.4,8

Conclusion and future perspectives

Despite remarkable advances in PD research over the past decades, significant challenges remain, including funding disparities, limited training opportunities, structural and regulatory barriers, language differences, and persistent gaps in trust and equitable participation across regions. Addressing these challenges requires a truly global approach built on synergistic and transparent multidisciplinary, multicultural, multilingual, and multinational collaborations, encompassing North–South, South–South, and North–North partnerships. Future research initiatives should be grounded in trust, transparency, horizontal communication, ethical reciprocity, and equitable governance, while prioritizing capacity building, workforce development, and sustainable training opportunities. In the coming years, international collaboration is poised to narrow the asymmetries and evidence gaps that have long shaped the field. As PD is redefined, from multi-ancestry genomics to the emerging staging systems, these frameworks will serve all patients only if they are built from the outset with representative cohorts and with investigators from initially underrepresented regions who take on leading roles. Equally essential is genuine community participation, engaging patients and local populations not as sources of samples but as partners in setting research priorities. On these foundations, future advances can be both scientifically robust and representative of all populations living with the disease.
