Background

Postoperative pneumonia (PP) after esophagectomy arises from interacting pulmonary, aspiration, airway-protection, perioperative-care, and host-response pathways. Whether the preoperative fibrinogen-to-prealbumin ratio (FPR), a marker integrating inflammatory-coagulative activity and nutritional-inflammatory reserve, is associated with in-hospital PP after minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy remains uncertain.

Methods

Using routinely collected electronic medical-record data, consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) who underwent elective minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy from January 2023 to December 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. FPR was expressed on the ×1000 scale and analyzed continuously per 1-unit and per 1-standard-deviation (SD) increase. The primary endpoint was in-hospital PP. Sequential multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, infection-phenotype and microbiological description, multicollinearity diagnostics, and clinically defined sensitivity analyses were performed. A complete-case 30-day PP analysis was included as a sensitivity analysis. Selectively performed airway and swallowing assessments were analyzed descriptively only.

Results

Among 437 patients, 139 (31.8%) developed in-hospital PP. In the primary fully adjusted clinical model, each 1-unit increase in FPR ×1000 was associated with higher odds of PP (odds ratio [OR] 1.32, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.23–1.41; P<0.001); the OR per 1-SD increase was 4.40 (95% CI 3.09–6.29; P<0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis supported an overall graded association (P for overall <0.001; P for non-linearity=0.162). Results remained consistent in the complete-case 30-day analysis and other sensitivity analyses. Median PP onset was postoperative day 5; 25.2% of cases were classified as aspiration-likely, and 44.7% of cultured cases were culture positive.

Conclusion

Higher preoperative FPR was associated with in-hospital PP after minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy for ESCC. These single-center retrospective findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish causality or immediate clinical utility. Prospective multicenter and external validation is required before FPR is used for perioperative risk stratification.

Plain Language Summary

Pneumonia is a common complication after surgery for esophageal cancer and may arise from several overlapping causes. This study examined the fibrinogen-to-prealbumin ratio (FPR), which is calculated from two routine blood tests obtained before surgery.

Among 437 patients who underwent minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy, higher preoperative FPR was associated with pneumonia during the same hospital admission. The association remained after adjustment for clinical and operative characteristics and in several sensitivity analyses, including an analysis among patients with complete 30-day follow-up.

Because this was a retrospective study from one hospital, the findings do not show that FPR causes pneumonia and do not support using a specific FPR value to guide treatment. Prospective studies in other hospitals are required before FPR can be considered for clinical risk assessment.

Introduction

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains a major global health problem, particularly in East Asian populations, where squamous histology accounts for much of the disease burden.1,2 For resectable disease, esophagectomy remains an essential component of curative-intent treatment and is recommended in contemporary clinical guidelines.3,4 Minimally invasive approaches are increasingly used and have been associated with reduced postoperative morbidity compared with open surgery.5,6 Nevertheless, postoperative pulmonary complications, particularly postoperative pneumonia (PP), remain frequent after esophagectomy and are associated with worse short- and long-term outcomes.7,8

PP after esophagectomy is not a single-mechanism event. Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways and early mobilization can shape pulmonary recovery,9,10 while oral-intake timing and postoperative nutritional recovery interact with airway protection, aspiration risk, and host resilience.11 Airway dysfunction, recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis (RLNP), conduit emptying problems, and postoperative thoracic events may also contribute. A preoperative biomarker should therefore be interpreted within this broader clinical and temporal context rather than as an isolated numerical score.

Fibrinogen and prealbumin represent biologically opposing dimensions of perioperative resilience. Fibrinogen is a positive acute-phase reactant linked to systemic inflammation and coagulation activation. Prealbumin reflects protein reserve but is also suppressed by inflammation. Previous studies have evaluated FPR as an inflammatory-nutritional prognostic marker in pancreatic and gastric cancer,12,13 and a meta-analysis in digestive system malignancies suggested that FPR may capture clinically relevant inflammation-nutrition imbalance.14 A higher preoperative FPR may therefore indicate greater inflammatory-coagulative activity relative to nutritional-inflammatory reserve.

Nutrition-based and multimodal prehabilitation, oral and dental care, and airway-protection strategies are relevant perioperative domains in esophageal surgery.15–18 Recent studies have also examined the C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte index and the aggregate index of systemic inflammation in relation to pneumonia after esophagectomy.19,20 However, less attention has been paid to the adjusted association between a coagulation-linked inflammatory-nutritional marker and in-hospital PP while explicitly accounting for outcome timing, selective airway and swallowing assessment, aspiration phenotype, postoperative events, and microbiology. We therefore examined the association between continuous preoperative FPR and in-hospital PP in a homogeneous ESCC cohort undergoing minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy. Secondary objectives were to describe the dose-response pattern, infection phenotype, and robustness of the association. Exploratory internal performance analyses were retained only as supplementary, hypothesis-generating assessments and were not intended to establish a deployable prediction model.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Ethical Approval

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Affiliated Huai’an No. 1 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed ESCC who underwent elective curative-intent minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy between January 2023 and December 2025 were screened. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Reference No. KY-2026-072-01). Written informed consent was waived because only retrospective clinical data were analyzed. Study data were de-identified before analysis and handled confidentially. The study was reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement, an extension of STROBE.21

Study Population

Among 477 screened patients, eligibility required pathologically confirmed ESCC, elective curative-intent minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy, no neoadjuvant therapy, available preoperative fibrinogen and prealbumin values, and ascertainable in-hospital PP status during the index admission. Patients were excluded if they had pulmonary infection within 2 weeks before surgery (n=8), missing fibrinogen and/or prealbumin data (n=15), or insufficient clinical or imaging documentation for PP adjudication (n=17). The final cohort included 437 patients: 298 without PP and 139 with PP.

Surgical Procedure and Perioperative Management

All operations were conventional thoracoscopic-laparoscopic McKeown minimally invasive esophagectomies with circular stapled cervical anastomosis. Operative duration was measured from incision to closure, and intraoperative blood loss was abstracted from anesthesia records. Institutional perioperative care was based on ERAS principles. Recorded items included preoperative respiratory training, intraoperative lung-protective ventilation, multimodal analgesia, postoperative day (POD) 1 mobilization, pulmonary hygiene until POD2, and early enteral nutrition by POD2. Composite compliance percentages were calculated for preoperative/intraoperative and early postoperative ERAS items. Early postoperative items were counted before PP onset when applicable and were used descriptively rather than as baseline adjustment variables.

Outcome Definition and 30-Day Sensitivity Endpoint

The primary endpoint was in-hospital PP during the index hospitalization. PP was defined according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/National Healthcare Safety Network criteria: new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography or computed tomography plus compatible clinical evidence, including at least two of fever (>38°C) or hypothermia, leukocytosis or leukopenia, purulent sputum or increased respiratory secretions, new or worsening cough or dyspnea, and/or deteriorating oxygenation, with microbiological confirmation when available. Events were adjudicated from clinical notes, imaging reports, and microbiology records. PP onset day, aspiration-likely phenotype, radiologic pattern, culture performance, culture positivity, and predominant pathogen group were summarized among PP cases.

In-hospital PP remained the primary endpoint. To evaluate whether the observation window affected the association estimate, a complete-case 30-day sensitivity endpoint was defined as any source-confirmed PP through POD30. Thirty-day status was ascertained from same-hospital emergency or readmission records, outpatient electronic records, or telephone follow-up records. Potential post-discharge events identified during follow-up were reviewed against available clinical and imaging documentation before adjudication. Patients without reliable 30-day documentation remained missing and were excluded only from this sensitivity analysis; they were not coded as non-events.

Data Source, Extraction, Cleaning, and Covariates

Study investigators abstracted data directly from the hospital electronic medical record using a standardized form (Supplementary Table S1). Cohort identification used operative and pathology records together with the stated eligibility criteria rather than billing codes or an automated case-identification algorithm. No external database linkage was performed. Data cleaning included duplicate-identifier checks, range and unit checks, date and postoperative-day consistency checks, recalculation of FPR from its components, and source-record review of discordant or implausible values. The final analytic dataset was de-identified before analysis.

Baseline domains included demographics, lifestyle exposures, comorbidities, functional status, cardiopulmonary function, tumor features, laboratory values, and operative variables. Smoking status was coded as never versus current or former smoking; smoking index was recorded in pack-years and set to zero for never smokers. Pulmonary and cardiac measures included forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1, % predicted), FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC, %), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, %). Tumor variables included location, length, differentiation, and clinical TNM stage. Postoperative events and clinical-burden measures, including anastomotic leak, delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE), pneumothorax, treated pleural effusion, tube duration, timing of nutrition and oral intake, unplanned intensive care unit transfer/readmission, and length of stay, were handled as descriptive context or sensitivity-analysis variables rather than as primary adjustment variables because they could be causes, mediators, consequences, or co-occurring features of PP.

Laboratory Measurements and FPR Definition

Preoperative blood tests were obtained within 1 week before surgery as part of routine assessment. Plasma fibrinogen was recorded in g/L. Serum prealbumin was recorded in mg/L and converted to g/L when needed. FPR was calculated as fibrinogen (g/L) divided by prealbumin (g/L), equivalent to fibrinogen (g/L)/prealbumin (mg/L) ×1000 when displayed on the ×1000 scale. Continuous FPR ×1000 was the primary exposure for regression and spline analyses. FPR was analyzed continuously, and no dichotomous threshold was used.

Airway, RLNP, Swallowing, and Aspiration-Related Variables

Airway-protection and aspiration-related information was abstracted to contextualize PP mechanisms. Bedside hoarseness or suspected RLNP was recorded clinically. Laryngoscopy indications included routine early postoperative vocal-cord assessment, voice or cough abnormality, clinically suspected RLNP, choking or dysphagia, suspected aspiration, impaired cough or airway protection, and assessment after PP or aspiration concern. Swallow-screen indications included routine assessment before oral intake, choking, wet voice, dysphagia or suspected aspiration, delayed oral intake or conduit-emptying concerns, hoarseness or RLNP, and PP-related aspiration-risk assessment. Assessments were categorized as routine pathway, clinical-risk-triggered, symptom-triggered, or outcome-triggered. Because laryngoscopy and swallow screening were not universal and some assessments occurred after PP onset, their results were summarized descriptively only and were not entered into adjusted regression models. Aspiration-likely PP and documented aspiration evidence before or at PP onset were summarized among PP cases and used in sensitivity analyses.

Missingness, Structural Not-Applicable Values, and Selection Assessment

The primary outcome, fibrinogen, prealbumin, and FPR were complete for all included patients. Tumor length had one true missing value. Descriptive analyses used observed tumor length; Model 3 used median imputation, and a complete-case analysis was performed. Values that were not applicable by design, such as PP onset day in non-PP patients or leak timing in patients without a leak, were treated as structural not-applicable values rather than missing data. To examine potential selection bias, baseline characteristics of the 437 included patients were compared with the 32 patients excluded because of missing FPR components or insufficient PP documentation. Absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs) or Cramér’s V were used to describe these comparisons (Supplementary Table S5).

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized by PP status using means with standard deviations, medians with interquartile ranges, or counts with percentages. Group comparisons used Welch t tests, Mann–Whitney U-tests, Pearson chi-square tests, or Fisher exact tests as appropriate. Absolute SMDs were reported for continuous and binary variables, and Cramér’s V was reported for multi-level categorical variables.

Logistic regression was used to evaluate the FPR–PP association. FPR was assessed continuously per 1-unit increase on the ×1000 scale and per 1-SD increase. Model 0 was unadjusted. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index. Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking index, drinking history, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, ECOG performance status, ASA physical status, chronic pulmonary disease/COPD/emphysema, preoperative chest CT abnormality, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and LVEF. Model 3 additionally adjusted for tumor location, tumor length, tumor differentiation, clinical TNM stage, operative duration, and intraoperative blood loss and was the primary fully adjusted clinical model. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for FPR and all Model 3 predictors; VIF<5 was considered inconsistent with problematic multicollinearity.

Restricted cubic splines were fitted within Model 3 using four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of FPR. The median FPR (13.20) was the reference, and the curve was displayed from the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile. Likelihood-ratio tests assessed the overall and non-linear components. Sensitivity analyses included complete-case tumor length, the 30-day complete-case endpoint, exclusion of patients with anastomotic leak, exclusion of clinically relevant DGCE, exclusion of aspiration-likely PP, exclusion of PP with aspiration evidence before or at onset, and separate component models for fibrinogen and prealbumin.

Exploratory internal performance analyses were confined to the Supplementary Material and compared FPR alone, Clinical Model 3 without FPR, and Clinical Model 3 plus FPR. Apparent and repeated cross-validated areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs), Brier scores, calibration intercepts, and calibration slopes were reported descriptively. Repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 5 repeats used a fixed random seed of 636095. Median imputation and standardization were performed within each training fold. Logistic regression used an L2 penalty with C=1000, the lbfgs solver, and a maximum of 20,000 iterations. No hyperparameter tuning or decision-curve analysis was performed. These analyses were not intended to establish clinical utility or transportability. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Python 3.13.5 with statsmodels 0.14.6 and scikit-learn 1.8.0.

Results

Patient Selection and Postoperative Pneumonia Incidence

Of 477 screened patients with pathologically confirmed ESCC treated by minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy between January 2023 and December 2025, 40 were excluded because of recent pulmonary infection (n=8), missing laboratory values required to calculate FPR (n=15), or insufficient documentation for PP ascertainment (n=17). The final analytic cohort comprised 437 patients (Figure 1). Most measured characteristics were broadly comparable between the included cohort and the 32 analytic exclusions, although the excluded patients had a higher mean FEV1/FVC (78.7% versus 76.7%; absolute SMD=0.409), so selection bias cannot be excluded (Supplementary Table S5). Pneumonia Phenotype, Sensitivity Analyses, and Exploratory Supplementary Performance

Supplementary Table S2 describes the infection phenotype and microbiological profile of PP. Median onset was POD5. Aspiration-likely pneumonia accounted for 35 cases (25.2%), and aspiration evidence before or at PP onset was documented in 27 cases (19.4%). Sputum or bronchial culture was obtained in 94 PP cases (67.6%), and 42 cultures were positive (44.7%). Among PP patients, 21 of 29 laryngoscopy-confirmed RLNP cases were documented before or on the same day as PP and 8 were documented after PP.

The FPR association was consistent across sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S3). The complete-case tumor-length estimate was OR 1.33 (95% CI 1.24–1.42). Among 414 patients with complete 30-day ascertainment, 147 developed PP by POD30, including 8 post-discharge events; the corresponding adjusted OR was 1.34 (95% CI 1.25–1.43). Estimates remained similar after excluding patients with anastomotic leak, clinically relevant DGCE, aspiration-likely PP, or aspiration evidence before or at PP onset. Higher fibrinogen and lower prealbumin were each associated with PP in separate component models. Full Model 3 coefficients are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Exploratory internal performance results are provided in Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Figure S1. These analyses were descriptive and were not interpreted as evidence of clinical utility, transportability, or readiness for clinical deployment.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of patients with ESCC undergoing a uniform minimally invasive McKeown procedure, higher preoperative FPR was associated with higher odds of in-hospital PP after adjustment for demographic, pulmonary, tumor-related, and intraoperative variables. The association was observed on continuous and standardized scales, followed a graded dose-response pattern, and remained directionally stable across clinically defined sensitivity analyses. The principal finding is therefore an association between a continuous preoperative marker and PP, not validation of a treatment threshold or a clinical prediction tool.

FPR represents a biological axis that differs from blood-cell-derived indices. Recent studies have evaluated CALLY and AISI in relation to PP after esophageal cancer surgery.19,20 FPR instead combines fibrinogen, reflecting acute-phase and coagulation-linked activity, with prealbumin, reflecting protein reserve and inflammatory suppression of hepatic visceral proteins. Prior studies have mainly examined FPR in oncologic prognosis or cancer risk stratification.12–14,22–24 The present findings extend this inflammatory-nutritional framework to an early postoperative infectious outcome, while the observational design precludes mechanistic or causal conclusions.

The measured exposure should be understood as a static preoperative signal. FPR was obtained within 1 week before surgery; postoperative leak, DGCE, pleural events, and other complications therefore could not alter the measured preoperative value. Nevertheless, these events can lie on pathways between baseline vulnerability and PP, can share common causes with PP, or can occur as consequences or co-occurring features. Postoperative inflammatory and nutritional trajectories may also differ from the preoperative measurement, but serial FPR was not available. Excluding patients with leak or DGCE showed that the association was not confined to those subgroups; it did not estimate mediation or prove independence from postoperative pathways.

The infection-phenotype findings also argue against a single mechanism. Median PP onset was POD5, approximately one quarter of cases were classified as aspiration-likely, and culture positivity was moderate among cultured patients. The association remained after excluding aspiration-likely cases or cases with documented aspiration evidence before or at onset. These analyses do not diminish the importance of aspiration, airway-protection failure, impaired pulmonary reserve, or perioperative care. Rather, they are compatible with FPR reflecting a broader host-response vulnerability that coexists with mechanical, pulmonary, aspiration, and care-process determinants of PP.7–11,25–29

Laryngoscopy and swallow screening required particular caution. They were not performed according to a universal study protocol and were often prompted by clinical risk, symptoms, or PP-related concern; some assessments occurred after PP onset. This creates indication and ascertainment bias and can generate non-transportable regression coefficients. We therefore removed these variables from adjusted models and retained them only as descriptive clinical context. The observed clustering of RLNP and swallow-screen failure with PP should not be interpreted as an unbiased estimate of their causal effects.

The primary outcome was deliberately defined as in-hospital PP, but this window can miss post-discharge events and is not equivalent to standardized 30-day surgical outcome reporting. Complete 30-day ascertainment was available for 414 patients, and the sensitivity analysis—including eight documented post-discharge PP events—produced a similar FPR estimate. However, 23 patients lacked reliable 30-day documentation, and post-discharge ascertainment drew on several clinical sources rather than a prospectively uniform protocol. The 30-day analysis therefore supports robustness but does not eliminate potential outcome-ascertainment bias.

The supplementary performance analyses were retained only to quantify whether FPR carried statistical information beyond the selected clinical covariates. Recent studies and reviews demonstrate continuing interest in PP prediction after esophagectomy,30–35 while TRIPOD+AI, PROBAST+AI, and external-validation guidance emphasize that internal performance does not establish transportability or clinical usefulness.36–39 In the present data, calibration slopes below 1 indicated residual optimism or overfitting. No decision-curve analysis, clinical calculator, or externally validated threshold is presented. These results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

The findings do not currently justify changing perioperative treatment, surveillance, or nutrition based on FPR. At most, they support prospective evaluation of whether continuous FPR adds reproducible information within broader clinical assessment. Future studies should use multicenter cohorts, standardized 30-day outcome surveillance, serial perioperative biomarker measurements, prespecified analysis plans, and external validation before considering clinical implementation.

Several features strengthen the analysis. The cohort was restricted to pathologically confirmed ESCC treated with a uniform minimally invasive McKeown procedure and cervical anastomotic technique. FPR was analyzed primarily as a continuous exposure, the dose-response relation was examined without relying on a data-derived threshold, clinically relevant covariates were adjusted, multicollinearity was low, and robustness was tested across alternative outcome and subgroup definitions. The included-versus-excluded comparison, source-based timing audits, infection phenotype, microbiology, and explicit distinction between structural not-applicable values and true missingness improve transparency.

Limitations remain. First, the retrospective single-center design limits generalizability and cannot eliminate residual confounding. Second, the primary endpoint was limited to the index hospitalization; the 30-day analysis was complete-case and did not use a prospective uniform follow-up protocol. Third, laryngoscopy and swallow screening were selective, limiting inference regarding airway and swallowing factors. Fourth, the included and analytically excluded groups differed in FEV1/FVC, so selection bias cannot be excluded. Fifth, only one preoperative FPR measurement was analyzed, and postoperative biomarker trajectories were unavailable. Sixth, postoperative events may be causes, mediators, consequences, or co-occurring features of PP. Seventh, exploratory performance analyses were internally but not externally validated and remained susceptible to overfitting. Finally, the cohort excluded patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy and was limited to ESCC treated with minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy, so the findings may not apply to adenocarcinoma, other operative approaches, or centers with different perioperative pathways.

In conclusion, higher preoperative FPR was associated with in-hospital PP after minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy for ESCC. The association was graded and remained stable after multivariable adjustment and several sensitivity analyses, including a complete-case 30-day endpoint. These findings remain exploratory and require prospective multicenter and external validation before FPR is used for perioperative risk stratification or clinical decision-making.
