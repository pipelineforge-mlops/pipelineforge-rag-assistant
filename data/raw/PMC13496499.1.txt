Chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic respiratory disorders, and certain cancers are leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. While biological and behavioral factors contribute to their development, growing evidence indicates that social determinants of health (SDOH) play a fundamental role in influencing chronic disease outcomes. SDOH include socioeconomic status, education, employment, housing conditions, neighborhood environment, access to nutritious food, and availability of healthcare services. These factors not only shape individual health behaviors but also determine access to preventive care, disease management resources, and overall quality of life. Individuals and communities facing socioeconomic disadvantage are disproportionately affected by chronic diseases due to cumulative exposure to risk-enhancing environments, chronic stress, limited health literacy, and systemic barriers within healthcare systems.

This narrative review explores the multidimensional impact of SDOH on the prevalence, progression, and management of chronic diseases. It highlights the interconnected nature of social and environmental influences and examines how disparities in education, employment, housing, and racial or geographic equity contribute to poor health outcomes. The review also identifies key gaps in the existing literature, including the need for more intersectional and context-specific research, particularly in underserved and marginalized populations. Lastly, it proposes future directions that include policy integration, community engagement, and cross-sector collaboration as essential strategies for addressing the root causes of chronic disease and promoting health equity. Understanding and addressing SDOH is crucial for creating effective, sustainable, and inclusive approaches to chronic disease prevention and management.

1. Introduction

Chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, and various forms of cancer continue to rank among the leading causes of global morbidity and mortality, creating an immense burden on individuals, healthcare systems, and economies worldwide.1,2 While genetic predisposition and behavioral factors such as diet, physical inactivity, and smoking have traditionally been emphasized etiologies, there is now broad recognition that these alone cannot fully account for disparities in disease occurrence or outcomes. Instead, the social determinants of health (SDOH)—the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age—are increasingly understood as critical drivers of chronic disease patterns. 3,4 These determinants shape access to resources, exposure to risks, and opportunities for well-being, ultimately influencing both the onset and progression of non-communicable diseases (NCDs).

Socioeconomic status, education, employment stability, housing quality, and access to healthcare represent core components of SDOH that determine an individual’s vulnerability to chronic illness3–5. Populations experiencing poverty or social exclusion are disproportionately exposed to environmental hazards, limited healthcare availability, food insecurity, and chronic psychosocial stress—all of which contribute cumulatively to poor health outcomes. For instance, individuals from lower socioeconomic groups often have reduced health literacy, lower preventive healthcare utilization, and diminished access to nutritious food, factors that together increase their risk of developing hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. These disparities are further compounded by systemic inequalities that restrict opportunities for education and employment, perpetuating a cycle of disadvantage and ill health across generations.5

In addition to material deprivation, psychosocial factors play an equally significant role in linking social conditions to biological health outcomes. Experiences of chronic stress, discrimination, and social isolation can alter neuroendocrine and inflammatory responses, leading to metabolic dysfunction, immune dysregulation, and accelerated disease progression.6

This narrative review aims to explore the multifaceted impact of social determinants of health on chronic diseases, synthesizing current evidence to highlight how inequities in social conditions contribute to disease vulnerability and progression. By examining key determinants such as socioeconomic status, education, housing, and access to care, this review underscores the importance of integrated approaches that go beyond individual-level interventions to include systemic changes. Addressing social determinants is not only critical for effective chronic disease prevention and management but also for advancing health equity on a global scale.

2. Discussion

The intricate interplay between the social determinants of health (SDOH) and the burden of chronic diseases is increasingly recognized as a central factor shaping global health outcomes.7 These determinants encompass a broad spectrum of non-medical factors—such as income, education, employment, housing, neighbourhood environment, and access to healthcare—that collectively exert a stronger influence on population health than individual behaviour or clinical interventions.8 Chronic diseases, including diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular illnesses, and chronic respiratory diseases, are not uniformly distributed across populations; rather, they are disproportionately concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.9 Understanding and addressing these disparities requires evaluating the full spectrum of SDOH and their interconnected effects across the life course.10

2.1. Socioeconomic status and health disparities

Socioeconomic status (SES) is one of the most robust predictors of health outcomes, influencing nearly every aspect of life—from residential environment and education quality to employment opportunities and healthcare access.11 Fig. 1a demonstrates a clear gradient: chronic disease prevalence reaches 45 % among low-SES groups compared with only 18 % in high-SES populations. This disparity reflects not only material deprivation but also the biological toll of sustained stress, limited autonomy, and social marginalization. Individuals with lower SES often encounter cumulative disadvantages, such as restricted access to preventive care, inadequate nutrition, and unsafe physical environments, all of which increase their vulnerability to chronic illness.12

The relationship between poverty and chronic disease is cyclical. Limited financial resources constrain individuals’ ability to afford medications, attend follow-ups, or maintain healthy lifestyles. Chronic financial stress also activates neuroendocrine mechanisms, including dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, contributing to inflammation, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease.13 Employment status, a key component of SES, further shapes health outcomes. Unemployment or insecure work often leads to loss of insurance, food and housing instability, and reduced engagement in health-promoting activities. 14 Conversely, stable employment not only provides income but also offers psychological structure and social connection. Table 2 illustrates this gradient, showing markedly higher rates of hypertension, obesity, and depression among unemployed individuals compared with those in fulltime work. Strengthening labour protections, social safety nets, and equitable access to healthcare remains essential to mitigating SES-driven health disparities.15

2.2. Education, health literacy, and access to care

Education is one of the most powerful protective determinants of health, shaping outcomes both directly and indirectly.16 Higher educational attainment enhances employment prospects, income stability, problem-solving skills, and, crucially, health literacy—all of which contribute to improved health behaviours and access to care.17 Fig. 1b illustrates that healthcare utilization rises markedly with educational level: only 40 % of individuals without formal schooling access healthcare regularly, compared with over 90 % of those with tertiary education. This educational gradient influences early disease detection, preventive screening, and adherence to long-term treatments.

Health literacy—the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information—acts as a critical intermediary between education and health outcomes.18 Individuals with limited health literacy are less likely to recognize early warning signs, interpret medical instructions correctly, or maintain adherence to prescribed therapies. Conversely, those with higher literacy levels demonstrate better disease self-management, particularly in chronic illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension, and asthma.19,20 Studies consistently show that as education rises, health literacy improves significantly— from about 30 % in individuals with no formal education to nearly 90 % among university graduates.21

This literacy gap translates directly into disparities in treatment adherence and lifestyle modification. Patients with limited literacy exhibit roughly 45 % medication adherence and only 30 % uptake of healthy behaviours, compared to 85 % and 75 %, respectively, among high-literacy groups.22 Such differences underscore the need for clearer communication strategies, simplified treatment instructions, and enhanced provider–patient dialogue. 23 Educational and health system reforms that promote lifelong learning, plain-language health communication, and culturally adapted education programs can substantially improve chronic disease management and reduce preventable morbidity across populations.24

2.3. Environmental and nutritional determinants

Beyond education and income, the environments in which people live—shaped by housing policy, urban development, and economic inequality—-play a crucial role in determining overall health outcomes.25 Poor-quality housing exposes individuals to overcrowding, dampness, mold, and inadequate sanitation, which contribute significantly to the burden of respiratory diseases, allergies, and infectious illnesses.26 Substandard housing conditions, particularly those characterized by poor ventilation and exposure to indoor pollutants, have been shown to elevate the risk of chronic respiratory conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).27 Conversely, safe, adequately ventilated, and structurally sound housing provides protective benefits, reducing both acute and chronic illness rates.28 These environmental exposures are rarely distributed evenly across society; they are often concentrated in low-income or marginalized communities, reflecting broader patterns of economic segregation and inequitable resource allocation.29 Table 1 illustrates how substandard housing increases risk for respiratory diseases.

Nutritional environment is another central determinant of chronic disease.30 Communities with limited access to affordable, nutritious food—often termed “food deserts”—face disproportionately high rates of obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes.31 Individuals with lower incomes are more likely to experience food insecurity, forcing reliance on calorie-dense, nutrient-poor foods that are cheaper and more readily available.32 These unhealthy dietary patterns contribute directly to metabolic dysfunction, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular complications.33 The nutritional challenges faced by low-income populations are compounded by psychosocial stressors associated with economic hardship, which can further exacerbate poor dietary choices through emotional eating, disrupted sleep, and reduced motivation for physical activity.34 Fig. 1c highlights how food insecurity—defined as limited or uncertain access to adequate food—is most prevalent among lower-income populations. Over 55 % of individuals earning under $20,000 annually in high-income/developed settings, particularly the United States, experience food insecurity.

Environmental and nutritional inequities also have deep psychosocial and intergenerational effects. 35 Living in deteriorated or polluted neighbourhoods contributes to chronic stress, anxiety, and reduced social cohesion, all of which elevate cortisol levels and increase susceptibility to hypertension, obesity, and depression.36 These factors collectively contribute to what has been termed “cumulative disadvantage,” wherein the health impacts of poor environments and limited nutrition accumulate and persist across generations.37 Addressing these systemic inequities requires a coordinated public health response that extends beyond clinical care.38 Housing reform, equitable urban planning, improved access to affordable and nutritious foods, and the creation of safe community spaces are vital policy strategies to mitigate the long-term effects of environmental and nutritional deprivation.39

2.4. Community and racial disparities in disease management

The social environment continues to shape health outcomes long after a disease is diagnosed, influencing both disease trajectory and quality of life. Chronic disease management is not only a clinical issue but a reflection of community resources and social infrastructure. Access to primary care, proximity to clinics, availability of transport, and safe spaces for physical activity are crucial in determining how effectively individuals can adhere to treatment plans and maintain wellness. In resource-rich neighbourhoods, where these facilities are abundant, self-management behaviours— such as medication adherence and routine follow-up—are significantly higher. In contrast, under-resourced neighbourhoods face persistent barriers including long travel times, unaffordable care, low provider density, and unsafe surroundings that discourage outdoor activity. Fig. 1d demonstrates this disparity clearly: individuals living in well-developed areas report an 85 % success rate in chronic disease management, compared to only 30 % among those in poorer environments. These structural inequalities reflect broader patterns of economic segregation, policy neglect, and systemic disinvestment that perpetuate community-level vulnerability.29

Geographical disparities compound these inequities, particularly between urban and rural settings. Rural populations often experience fragmented healthcare systems, limited emergency services, and shortages of trained professionals. The difference in healthcare utilization—85 % among urban versus 55 % in rural residents— underscores a systemic gap that extends beyond infrastructure to include cultural and psychosocial dimensions.40,41 For instance, stigma surrounding illness, mistrust of medical institutions, and lower health literacy create additional layers of disadvantage for rural populations.42 Moreover, rural residents may delay seeking care due to transportation barriers or economic insecurity, resulting in late-stage disease presentation and higher rates of preventable complications. These issues are aggravated by the centralization of specialized care in urban hospitals, leaving rural communities reliant on general practitioners with limited resources. Addressing these inequities requires targeted interventions, such as rural telehealth expansion, incentive-based medical placements, and community outreach programs tailored to local needs.

Racial and ethnic disparities, deeply rooted in structural and historical inequities, further magnify disease management gaps. Structural racism manifests through historical redlining, segregation, discriminatory hiring, unequal educational access, and bias in clinical decision-making—all of which converge to reduce access to preventive and therapeutic care.43,44 Group B, representing historically marginalized racial populations, faces systemic disadvantages that translate into higher rates of uncontrolled diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases. Group C, typically composed of low-income immigrant or refugee populations, often struggles with linguistic barriers, limited insurance eligibility, and cultural alienation from the healthcare system. Meanwhile, Group D—rural and Indigenous populations—endure intergenerational trauma, forced displacement, and chronic under-investment in healthcare infrastructure.45

Mitigating these disparities requires structural reform across multiple sectors. Culturally sensitive care, community-led planning, and inclusive education for healthcare professionals are essential for building trust and improving engagement among underserved groups.46 The six foundational social determinants of health—economic stability, education, neighborhood and physical environment, food access, community and social context, and the healthcare system—interact in complex feedback loops that perpetuate inequity. For example, economic instability reduces access to education and healthcare, while poor education limits employment opportunities that could foster economic security. 47 Fig. 2 illustrates how these interrelated factors influence health throughout the life course. The physical environment determines access to nutritious food, quality housing, and safe schools, while social context—through networks, discrimination, and inclusion—affects mental health, behavior, and trust in institutions.48

Ultimately, achieving equity in chronic disease management requires shifting focus from individual-level interventions to systemic solutions. A socially responsive healthcare system must recognize the influence of structural factors—poverty, racism, and exclusion—on patient outcomes.49 This includes equitable resource distribution, anti-racist health policy, urban renewal, affordable housing, and food security initiatives. By integrating social determinants into health policy, healthcare systems can evolve from reactive treatment models to proactive, community-centered frameworks that reduce disparities and promote sustainable health equity.

2.5. Future perspectives and gaps in literature

Despite increasing recognition of the profound role that social determinants play in chronic disease outcomes, substantial gaps remain in current research and public health practice. Most existing studies continue to focus on individual determinants— such as income, education, or housing— without adequately exploring their cumulative and interactive effects across the life course. Consequently, the global applicability of these findings is limited, underscoring the need for more inclusive, longitudinal, and context-specific research designs that reflect diverse socioeconomic realities.3

Future investigations should adopt integrated, systems-based frameworks that connect healthcare with broader social policies, including education, labor, housing, and urban planning. Effective prevention of chronic diseases requires recognizing that these determinants are deeply interlinked— economic instability restricts access to quality food and healthcare, while limited education reduces health literacy and employment opportunities. Interdisciplinary collaboration between medical, social, and policy sciences is crucial to design interventions that address these factors simultaneously.50 Furthermore, community-based and participatory research models can empower affected populations to co-create locally relevant solutions, ensuring sustainability and cultural appropriateness in intervention design.51

Finally, one of the most persistent challenges lies in translating research on social determinants into actionable policies. Evidence on the health impact of social and economic reforms often remains fragmented or fails to influence decision-making. Strengthening data systems, developing equity-based performance indicators, and prioritizing intersectoral collaboration are essential steps toward bridging this gap.49,52 Ultimately, addressing the social roots of chronic disease requires reimagining healthcare as part of a wider social ecosystem— one that promotes equity, resilience, and prevention rather than merely treating illness.53

Understanding the past is essential for improving health outcomes because it allows us to recognize the historical, social, and structural factors that have shaped current patterns of health and disease.54 Many of today’s health disparities—such as unequal access to care, mistrust in medical systems, and disproportionate disease burdens among marginalized populations—are rooted in historical injustices, including colonization, slavery, segregation, and discriminatory health policies.55 By studying these past events, healthcare systems can identify where inequities originated and implement corrective measures that promote trust, inclusivity, and fairness.5

Moreover, understanding the successes and failures of past public health interventions helps refine future strategies.56 For example, lessons from historical vaccination campaigns, sanitation reforms, and community health initiatives provide valuable insights into what approaches foster lasting behavioral change and which ones inadvertently reinforce inequality.57 In essence, reflecting on the past allows policymakers, clinicians, and researchers to design health systems that are not only more effective but also more equitable and culturally responsive—ensuring that future health outcomes are improved for all populations.58

3. Conclusion

Chronic diseases are not solely the result of individual choices or biological factors but are profoundly influenced by the social environments in which people live. Social determinants such as poverty, limited education, unemployment, inadequate housing, and poor access to healthcare create conditions that foster the development and progression of chronic illnesses. These factors often interact and compound over time, leading to significant disparities in health outcomes across different communities. Understanding these root causes is essential for designing effective and equitable public health strategies.

Addressing the burden of chronic diseases requires a shift from purely clinical approaches to comprehensive social and policy interventions. Efforts must focus on improving living conditions, enhancing access to education and employment, and ensuring that healthcare systems are inclusive and accessible to all. By tackling these broader societal challenges, we can work toward healthier communities and more just health outcomes for current and future generations.
