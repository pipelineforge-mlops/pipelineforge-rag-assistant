Cytokine storm is a leading cause of mortality in severe infections and adverse responses during adoptive cell therapies. Leveraging multivalent sialic acids (SAs) and the specificity of the SA-Siglecs (SA-binding immunoglobulin-type lectins) axis to direct immune cell responses opens a compelling route to efficiently mitigate cytokine storms. However, the optimal configuration of multivalent SAs to maximize Siglec binding affinity and immune-directing activity has yet to be elucidated. Herein, we report Sialic Acid Polymer Molecular Patterns (SPMPs), a class of synthetic macromolecular immunomodulators engineered with precise control over polymer backbone conformation, pendant SA spacing, and branching topology. This molecular pattern recreates the hierarchical multivalency of natural glycans while exploiting the modularity of polymer chemistry and molecular controlled SA patterns. Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy and single-molecule spectroscopy confirm structure-specific sialic acid patterns promote Siglec-E clustering and high-affinity engagement, triggering potent inhibitory signaling and reshaping innate immune cell functions to abrogate cytokine storms. These pattern-encoded macromolecules establish a new paradigm for precise immune modulation and offer a framework for materials-based interventions in inflammatory disorders.

Sialic acid polymer molecular patterns (SPMPs) encode polymer scaffold conformation and sialic acid valency to regulate Siglec-E clustering and inhibitory signaling. Optimized SPMPs enable precise modulation of innate immune responses and mitigate cytokine storms.

Introduction

Cytokine storm syndrome, an uncontrolled and self-amplifying release of proinflammatory mediators by innate immune cells, underlies life-threatening conditions such as sepsis and immunotherapy-related toxicity.1–3 Sepsis alone accounts for over 11 million deaths annually (20% of global mortality), with children under five comprising half of the cases.4 Despite its devastating prevalence, effective and safe therapeutics for cytokine storm syndrome remain unavailable, underscoring the significant unmet medical need associated with cytokine-driven pathologies.5 Broad-spectrum agents like glucocorticoids act symptomatically and cause pleiotropic metabolic disruption,6 whereas cytokine-targeting biologics (e.g. IL-6 and TNF-α antibodies) provide limited benefits and fail to abrogate the cellular origins of cytokine production.7–9 Given that cytokine storms originate from dysregulated innate immune activation rather than a single inflammatory mediator, strategies capable of selectively restoring immune homeostasis at the cellular and signaling levels are urgently needed.

Harnessing endogenous inhibitory pathways that restrain excessive innate immune activation represents an attractive strategy to restore immune homeostasis. Among these pathways, sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins (Siglecs) constitute a family of pattern recognition receptors largely localized to immune cell surfaces, regulating self-recognition and homeostatic regulation of immune responses.10 Several family members, notably human Siglec-7/9 and murine Siglec-E, are elevated in the context of sustained inflammation or immune activation, and harbor intracellular ITIM motifs that recruit SHP-1/2 phosphatases to curtail cytokine production.11,12 These receptors recognize sialic acid (SA) patterns on glycoproteins, mediating key immune regulatory functions like maintaining self-tolerance, modulating cell–cell interactions, and limiting tissue damage during immune responses. However, to trigger Siglec-mediated inhibitory signaling, multivalent engagement is required, which is often lacking in current small-molecule and monoclonal antibody-based therapeutics that fail to induce receptor clustering.10,13

Synthetic macromolecules provide a unique opportunity to engineer multivalent ligand presentation and receptor clustering through control polymer architectures.14–17 Despite their promise, achieving precise control over ligand presentation, polymer conformation, and receptor engagement remains a major challenge. Harnessing Siglec immune checkpoints with sialic acid-based macromolecules is impeded by an incomplete mechanistic understanding of how polymer structure and sialic acid valency influence Siglec clustering and subsequent immune signaling.18 This structural ambiguity restricts their therapeutic potential, particularly in achieving selective immune modulation while minimizing the risk of prolonged immunosuppression.

Here, we propose Sialic Acid Polymer Molecular Patterns (SPMPs), a novel class of synthetic macromolecular immunomodulators designed to precisely control the spatial organization of SA epitopes (Scheme 1). We systematically modulated polymer conformation, flexibility, SA spacing and branching topology to investigate how ligand spatial organization dictates Siglecs binding affinity, selectivity and clustering. Using super-resolution fluorescence microscopy, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and single molecule spectroscopy, we demonstrated that SPMPs with α-helical polymer backbones and optimized SA presentation enhance Siglec clustering, binding strength, and immune modulation effeciency. The optimized SPMPs, PPLG-2V-SA, exhibited potent therapeutic efficacy in sepsis driven by acute lung injury (ALI) and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) models, significantly prolonging survival. This study provides a mechanistic framework for engineering macromolecular therapeutics that engage pattern recognition receptors to precisely reprogram immune cells, thereby establishing a novel strategy for treating inflammatory disorders.

Results and discussion

Preparation of sialic acid polymers with different patterned structures

Natural sialylated glycoconjugates are structurally heterogeneous, which complicates mechanistic analysis and therapeutic translation.19 To address the central question of how molecular patterning controls Siglec engagement, we designed SPMPs not merely to mimic natural glycoconjugates but as programmable probes that decouple backbone conformation, chain length and epitope topology. We therefore selected α-helical poly(γ-propargyl-l-glutamate) (PPLG) with a degree of polymerization (DP) of 60 as a primary scaffold (Fig. 1a and S1–S3), its predictable α-helical fold provides a membrane-proximal, rigid display ideal for testing cis-ligand-like presentations, while its peptide chemistry affords biocompatibility and orthogonal click-chemistry handles.20 Pendant sialic acids (N3-SA) were installed by Cu(i)-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) at 50% grafting to balance epitope density and solubility (Fig. 1a and S4–S11).21,22 To contrast rigidity with flexibility, we synthesized a flexible poly(2-oxazoline)s (POx) of matched DP via ring opening polymerization of 2-(hexa-4-ynyl)-2-oxazoline (Fig. 1a and S12–S15).23

Backbone-dependent presentation of these SPMPs was confirmed by circular dichroism, PPLG-SA exhibited canonical α-helical signatures (positive Cotton band at 192 nm and negative minima at 208 and 222 nm), whereas POx-SA lacked helical features (Fig. 1b).24 These spectroscopic differences predicted distinct molecular assemble morphologies, which were validated by cryo-EM. PPLG-SA appeared as rod-like elements of ∼10 nm, consistent with a rigid, solvent-exposed SA display, while POx-SA formed ∼60 nm spherical assemblies that limit surface-exposed epitopes (Fig. 1c–g). To capture the dynamic basis of these static images, molecular-dynamics simulations revealed rapid helix formation for PPLG-SA (≈1 ns) with SAs arranged on the solvent-exposed face and equilibration by 100 ns (Fig. 1e, S16–S19 and Movie 1). While, POx-SA instead formed looser spherical aggregates with fewer surface-exposed SAs within the same timeframe (Fig. 1h, S20–S22 and Movie 2). This combined structural and dynamic evidence established that backbone geometry directly sculpts epitope presentation, rigid helices favor continuous, membrane-proximal SA arrays, while flexible polymers produce heterogeneous, partially buried displays. Recognizing that predictable multivalent display requires sufficient chain length, we therefore synthesized PPLG variants (DP = 30, 60, 120) and found that PPLG30-SA failed to adopt a stable helix (irregular CD in 180-200 nm and absent 208 nm band), whereas PPLG60-SA and PPLG120-SA maintained regular helical signatures (SI Fig. S23).25 This length-dependence defines a practical threshold for reliable cis-like SA presentation and thus for designing constructs with predictable multivalency.26–31

Collectively, this scaffold library, rigid vs. flexible backbones and varied DP, provides the controlled structural diversity required to map how backbone conformation, chain length and epitope topology jointly determine Siglec binding, clustering and immunomodulatory output.

To dissect how spatial patterning governs Siglec recognition, we next introduced SA building blocks with a controlled number of SA units (different valency of SAs) to precisely modulate epitope spacing along the polymer backbone. While decorating SAs on more repeating units could enhance apparent multivalency, the randomness of grafting potentially leads undefined spatial intervals between adjacent epitopes.26–28 To address this challenge, we synthesized divalent and tetravalent SA moieties (SI Fig. S24–S33). Using the same CuAAC coupling, the PPLG-2V-SA and PPLG-4V-SA were obtained with grafting ratios of 25% and 12.5%, respectively, ensuring consistent ligand presentation across polymer chains (Fig. 1i and S34–S35).

We then employed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to quantify how these spatially encoded SPMPs interact with distinct Siglec subtypes in dynamic solution.29 Strikingly, spatial organization alone endowed pronounced receptor selectivity (Fig. 1j and S36). All SPMPs bound preferentially to Siglec-E, an inhibitory receptor abundant on macrophages that signals through ITIM motifs,30 while showing negligible affinity for Siglec-15, which bears an immunoreceptor tyrosine based activation motif (ITAM) domain that promotes activation.31 Quantitatively, valency proved decisive, monovalent SPMPs exhibited dissociation constants (KD) approximately two orders of magnitude higher than divalent analogs, and one order higher than tetravalent constructs, highlighting how increasing ligand multiplicity exponentially amplifies Siglec-E affinity. These findings reveal that epitope geometry, more than chemical composition, determines Siglec selectivity and signaling bias, laying the groundwork for elucidating how SPMP-induced Siglec-E clustering mediates macrophage reprogramming and cytokine suppression.

Structural encoding of SPMPs governs macrophage inflammatory responses via SA-Siglec axis

To probe how molecular patterning translates into immunomodulation, we first compared the role of polymer conformation by contrasting rigid α-helical PPLG-SA with flexible POx-SA. Despite comparable Siglec-E affinities in SPR assays, PPLG-SA demonstrated substantially greater cytokine inhibition in both bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and RAW264.7 cells (EC50 = 270 µg mL−1vs. 589 µg mL−1 for POx-SA), nearly restoring cytokine levels to non-activated state at 500 µg mL−1 (Fig. 2a and S37). Free SA showed negligible activity, confirming that multivalent presentation was essential. We hypothesized that this enhanced efficacy arises from the cis-ligand mimicry of α-helical PPLG-SA,32 which can partially insert into macrophage membranes and emulate endogenous Siglec ligands, in contrast to POx-SA that remains as a trans-binding polymer in solution.33,34 Molecular dynamics simulations supported this model, showing spontaneous membrane insertion of PPLG-SA, yielding a stable interfacial configuration within nanoseconds (SI Fig. S38–S42 and Movie 3).

We next examined the dependence of inflammation-inhibiting efficacy on polymer chain length. PPLG-SA with varying degrees of polymerization (DP = 30, 60, 120) were incubated with LPS-stimulated BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells at equal SA amount. Consistent with TLR4-mediated cytokine activation, LPS induced sharp increases in IL-6 and TNF-α secretion.35 Notably, cytokine inhibition by SPMPs was highly chain-length dependent: PPLG30-SA, which lacks uniform α-helical structure, showed the weakest suppression, whereas PPLG60-SA and PPLG120-SA, both retaining stable helices, exerted markedly stronger inhibition with comparable efficacy (Fig. 2b and S37). Accordingly, PPLG60-SA was chosen for subsequent mechanistic studies. To further evaluate the effect of SA epitope topology, we compared PPLG-SA, PPLG-2V-SA, and PPLG-4V-SA bearing increasing sialic valency. Multivalent SPMPs exhibited substantially enhanced cytokine suppression, with EC50 values reduced by ∼30% relative to PPLG-SA and no significant difference between divalent and tetravalent forms (Fig. 2c and S37). The optimized PPLG-2V-SA also exhibited potent immunomodulatory activity in LPS-stimulated human THP-1 cells, which express inhibitory human Siglecs including Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 (Fig. S43), supporting the activity of SPMPs in a human immune cell context. The differences in the immunomodulating effency of the SPMPs reflect the chelate-driven multivalency effect, where localized epitope clustering lowers the entropic cost of receptor engagement and increases Siglec occupancy, thereby amplifying inhibitory signaling.36,37 However, we also observed a balance between sialic acid density, polymer architecture, and epitope accessibility. While increasing the number of sialic acid units improved Siglec engagement, excessive crowding introduced steric constraints, limiting further enhancement. This explains why the tetramer did not outperform the divalent forms. By normalizing to the total number of sialic acids in the polymer, we found that optimal binding efficiency is achieved by carefully balancing ligand density with polymer flexibility, ensuring sufficient spacing between epitopes for effective receptor clustering without overwhelming steric limitations. To verify that the immunomodulatory activity of SPMPs arises from specific SA-Siglec recognition, mannose was introduced onto the PPLG-2V scaffold as a glycan control. Replacement of sialic acid with mannose abolished Siglec-E binding and eliminated the immunomodulatory activity of PPLG-2V-SA, demonstrating that SA presentation is the critical determinant for Siglec-E-mediated immune regulation (Fig. S44).

Given the co-expression of multiple Siglecs on macrophages,13 we next identified the predominant receptor mediating SPMP activity. Upon antibody blockade of Siglec-E, the suppressive effect of PPLG-2V-SA on IL-6 and TNF-α secretion was progressively reversed in a concentration-dependent manner, with full recovery at 10 µg mL−1 antibody (Fig. 2d–e and S45).

To further validate that the immunomodulatory activity of SPMPs is dependent on Siglec-E,three sequence-specific siRNAs was designed for targeted knockdown of Siglec-E expression in RAW264.7 cells. LPS stimulation markedly increased Siglec-E expression, whereas co-treatment with PPLG-2V-SA partially reduced its expression, consistent with the involvement of Siglec-E signaling during inflammatory regulation (Fig. S46a and b). In contrast, transfection with Siglec-E siRNA sequences 1 and 2 efficiently suppressed Siglec-E expression, even after subsequent LPS stimulation, confirming effective receptor knockdown.

The anti-inflammatory activity of PPLG-2V-SA in Siglec-E-knockdown RAW264.7 cell was further evaluated in Siglec-E-knockdown RAW264.7 cells. Compared with control Raw264.7 cells, Siglec-E-knockdown cells exhibited slightly increased IL-6 and TNF-α production under both basal and LPS-stimulated conditions (Fig. S46c and d). Importantly, the inhibitory effects of PPLG-2V-SA on LPS-induced IL-6 and TNF-α production were substantially reduced following Siglec-E knockdown, demonstrating that Siglec-E is required for efficient immunomodulation by PPLG-2V-SA. These results confirm that Siglec-E is the principal checkpoint receptor engaged by SPMPs to dampen macrophage hyperactivation, establishing the SA-Siglec axis as a molecular conduit for polymer-pattern–encoded immunoregulation.

Structural encoding of SPMPs governs Siglec clustering and restrains macrophage hyperactivation

After establishing that SPMPs with distinct molecular architectures displayed markedly different capacities to restrain macrophage hyperactivation and attenuate pro-inflammatory signaling, we next sought to elucidate the molecular dynamic mechanism underlying these structural effects. We hypothesized that both the binding strength and mechanical stability of SPMPs-Siglec-E interactions determined their ability to trigger receptor clustering and downstream inhibitory signaling.38,39 To quantify these interactions at single-molecule level, we employed atomic force microscopy-base single-molecule force spectroscopy (SMFS) (Fig. 3a). SPMPs were covalently immobilized on silicon nitride probes and brought into contact with RAW264.7 macrophages. Each rupture event was recorded as a characteristic deflection on the retraction curve based on the position change of Z-sensor and the receiver of deflected light (Fig. 3b). The changes were corresponding to the dissociation of a single SPMP-Siglec-E complex, and the unbinding forces were calculated according to Hooke's Law.40 Three repeated sets of 2000 curves were collected per SPMP and statistically analyzed for binding probability and rupture force. SPMPs bearing α-helical backbones (PPLG-SA, PPLG-2V-SA, and PPLG-4V-SA) showed significantly higher binding probabilities and rupture forces than flexible POx-SA (Fig. 3c, d and S47). Moreover, increasing ligand valency further amplified the unbinding force, consistent with multivalent cooperative interactions between sialic acid epitopes and multiple Siglec-E receptors on the macrophage membrane. These results suggest that structural rigidity and controlled multivalency synergistically enhance receptor binding stability, laying the physical foundation for efficient Siglec clustering.

However, ligand-receptor binding alone is insufficient to trigger Siglec-E-mediated immune inhibition, which, similar to the Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING),41,42 the T cell Receptor (TCR),43 and the B cell Receptor (BCR),44 requires receptor cluster formation to activate downstream inhibitory signaling. To visualize how different SPMP architectures regulate receptor clustering, we employed super-resolution fluorescence microscopy (STORM) to directly image Siglec-E organization on LPS-activated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 3e).45 After incubation with 100 ng mL−1 LPS and SPMPs for 24 h, Siglec-E was labeled by fluorescent antibodies and reconstructed from 3000 image slices. POx-SA treatment resulted in sparse, small puncta, whereas α-helical SPMPs induced prominent Siglec-E aggregation into larger clusters (Fig. 3e and S48). Quantitative analysis revealed that PPLG-2V-SA and PPLG-4V-SA increased the frequency of clusters exceeding 0.04 µm2 by nearly threefold compared with POx-SA or PPLG-SA, aligning with their stronger cytokine-suppressive effects (more than 30 fluorescence spots in 0.1 µm2 were identified as one cluster). To further confirm the stability of these interactions at the cellular level, we examined the surface binding and retention kinetics of FITC-labeled SPMPs on macrophages using flow cytometry and confocal microscopy (Fig. 3f–h). All SPMPs rapidly associated with macrophage membranes within minutes, but the dissociation rates varied substantially across different structures. PPLG-2V-SA and PPLG-4V-SA exhibited significantly slower fluorescence decay and maintained higher signal intensity over 24 h than PPLG-SA or POx-SA (Fig. S49 and S50). Confocal imaging verified that α-helical multivalent SPMPs remained localized on the membrane for over 6 h, consistent with their stronger Siglec-E binding and clustering capacity.

These results delineate a coherent mechanism in which the molecular architecture of SPMPs encodes their immune-modulatory capacity: α-helical conformation dictates ligand presentation, multivalent epitope spacing reinforces cooperative Siglec-E binding and clustering, and sustained membrane retention ensures persistent inhibitory signaling. In contrast, the greater flexibility of POx-SA systems introduces an entropic penalty upon binding due to conformational restriction of the polymer backbone, which diminishes the effective binding affinity.46–48 In conclusion, by stabilizing Siglec-E nanoclusters and dampening excessive macrophage activation, SPMPs effectively reprogram innate immune responses and restore homeostatic balance. This pattern-encoded molecular mechanism illustrates how polymer chemistry can achieve precision control over inflammatory signaling through nanoscale spatial regulation of immune checkpoints.

Transcriptomic signatures reveal intracellular mechanisms by which SPMPs reprogram macrophage inflammatory responses

To elucidate how molecular SA patterns encoded in SPMPs modulates inflammatory responses, we performed RNA sequencing of LPS-activated macrophages treated with SPMPs of distinct backbone conformations and valencies. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed significant difference among samples (Fig. 4a). LPS-activated and POx-SA-treated samples clustered together, indicating minimal transcriptional deviation from the inflammatory state. Whereas α-helical PPLG-SA, PPLG-2V-SA, and PPLG-4V-SA formed a distinct cluster, consistent with their superior efficacy in suppressing cytokine hypersecretion.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis identified 630 shared DEGs across all SPMP-treated groups relative to LPS stimulation, while the largest overlap (2350 DEGs) occurred between PPLG-2V-SA and PPLG-4V-SA (Fig. 4b and S51), underscoring a common anti-inflammatory transcriptional signature driven by multivalent α-helical backbone. To elucidate the biological processes underlying the transcriptional modulation induced by SPMPs, we performed pathway over-representation analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the Hallmark gene set database (Fig. 4c).49 The Hallmark collection consolidates redundant gene sets into coherent biological modules that capture major cellular states and signaling events, thereby enhancing interpretability. Across all SPMP-treated macrophage groups, the enriched pathways displayed a consistent downregulation of canonical pro-inflammatory signaling, including NF-κB, MAPK, and p53 cascades, together with suppression of IL-6, TNF-α, and chemokine biosynthetic programs. Concomitantly, transcriptional adjustments were observed in phosphatase and kinase regulatory modules, macrophage activation and differentiation circuits, and metabolic remodeling pathways such as hypoxia and cell-cycle control. These transcriptomic signatures collectively delineate how the molecular structure of sialic acid polymer conjugates reprograms macrophage inflammatory signaling, shifting the transcriptional network from a hyperactivated, cytokine-producing state toward a restrained and homeostatic phenotype. To corroborate the link between upstream signaling and transcriptional activity, we probed the TLR4-NF-κB pathway via immunoblotting. LPS triggered IκB-α phosphorylation, enabling nuclear translocation of p50/p65 heterodimers and initiation of inflammatory gene expression. Conversely, co-administration of SPMPs, notably PPLG-2V-SA, abrogated this cascade, as shown by diminished nuclear p50/p65 and preservation of unphosphorylated IκB-α in the cytoplasm (SI Fig. S52). These results confirm that engagement of Siglec-E by SPMPs effectively blocks TLR4-mediated NF-κB signaling.

To identify the structural determinants most critical for immunomodulation, we next focused on PPLG-2V-SA, which had emerged from biophysical and cellular analysis as the most potent construct in suppressing cytokine secretion and promoting Siglec-E clustering. Building on these findings, we conducted transcriptomic profiling of macrophages treated with PPLG-2V-SA relative to LPS-activated controls to delineate the molecular programs underlying its optimal anti-inflammatory response. We identified 1639 upregulated and 1827 downregulated DEGs relative to the LPS-activated group (Fig. 4d). Pathway enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were associated with E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and peroxisome pathways, which are indicative of cell-cycle re-entry, metabolic reactivation, and reparative remodelling, consistent with macrophages transitioning toward a resolution-phase phenotype after inflammatory stress (Fig. 4e). Representative genes within these networks further supported this transition. The expression of Il6, Cxcl3, and Tnfrsf1b, which act as key amplifiers of cytokine release and leukocyte recruitment, was markedly reduced, whereas Mki67 and Csf1r, markers of proliferative and restorative macrophage programs, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4f).

Comparative transcriptomic analysis across PPLG-SA, PPLG-2V-SA, PPLG-4V-SA, and POx-SA revealed distinct molecular signatures corresponding to their structural and biophysical properties (Fig. S51). PPLG-2V-SA exhibited the most balanced transcriptional profile, characterized by robust suppression of pro-inflammatory genes together with moderate activation of proliferative and metabolic programs. In contrast, PPLG-4V-SA induced broader downregulation of immune pathways but also repressed genes associated with macrophage metabolic recovery, suggesting that excessive multivalency may over constrain receptor mobility and attenuate downstream homeostatic signalling. Meanwhile, the flexible POx-SA scaffold triggered weaker suppression of inflammatory transcription, consistent with its limited ability to promote stable Siglec-E clustering. These findings highlight that the α-helical conformation of PPLG provides structural rigidity, which reduces the loss of conformational entropy favouring membrane insertion and cis-ligand mimicry, while divalent spacing of sialic epitopes ensures optimal receptor engagement without steric hindrance or kinetic trapping. Collectively, the synergy between backbone conformation and moderate valency enables PPLG-2V-SA to achieve a precisely balanced Siglec activation state that suppresses hyperinflammatory signalling while preserving macrophage reparative function.

Therapeutic efficacy of SPMPs in cytokine storm-induced acute lung injury (ALI)

Guided by the in vitro potency of SPMPs, we selected the lead construct PPLG-2V-SA and benchmarked it against PPLG-SA in an early-phase ALI model of cytokine storm. We first assessed the biosafety and pharmacokinetic behavior of the lead construct PPLG-2V-SA, given its superior anti-inflammatory performance in vitro. Stable serum biomarker level (CRE, UA, BUN, GOT, GPT) for up to 7 days after 2 mg per mouse dosing indicates good systemic tolerability and no apparent hepatic or renal toxicity (SI Fig. S53). Pharmacokinetic analysis of Cy5-labeled PPLG-2V-SA demonstrated a prolonged circulation half-life of 12.3 h (SI Fig. S54), while ex vivo fluorescence imaging indicated predominant clearance through the liver and kidney within 24 h, which may originate from degradation of the peptide backbone. Notably, in ALI mice, PPLG-2V-SA exhibited enhanced pulmonary accumulation, which could be attributed to rapid binding to monocytes and neutrophils in circulation and subsequent enrichment at inflamed sites characterized by immune cell infiltration (SI Fig. S55). These findings confirmed that PPLG-2V-SA possessed both favorable in vivo stability and safety, making it suitable for further therapeutic evaluation in the ALI cytokine storm model.

We next investigated the therapeutic efficacy of PPLG-2V-SA in the LPS-induced ALI model. BALB/c mice received tail vein injections of PPLG-SA or PPLG-2V-SA at doses of 1 and 2 mg/mouse immediately following LPS administration (Fig. 5a). After 24 h, cytokine levels in serum and lung tissue, as well as immune cell infiltration, were analyzed. IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations were significantly reduced in both treatment groups, with PPLG-2V-SA showing the most pronounced suppression of cytokine storm responses (Fig. 5b). Flow cytometry and immunostaining revealed markedly lower recruitment of monocytes (CD11b+), macrophages (F4/80+), and neutrophils (CD11b+Ly6G+) in lungs of PPLG-2V-SA treated mice compared with controls (Fig. 5c–e and S56). This reduction correlated with decreased expression of CCL2 and CXCL8, key chemokines responsible for leukocyte infiltration (Fig. 5f).

Histological analysis further confirmed that PPLG-2V-SA significantly mitigated pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage. H&E staining revealed attenuated alveolar thickening and inflammatory cell infiltration, while MDA assays indicated lower oxidative stress levels in lung tissue compared with the untreated group (Fig. 5g–h). These findings suggest that after systemic administration, sialic polymer molecular patterns rapidly engage Siglec-E on circulating macrophages and monocytes, initiating inhibitory signaling that prevents their overactivation at inflamed sites. This SA-Siglec-mediated immunosuppression effectively interrupts the inflammatory cascade and protects lung tissue from further oxidative and structural damage. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PPLG-2V-SA is effective in mitigating cytokine storm-associated acute lung injury in this prophylactic/intervention model, with favorable systemic safety and biocompatibility profiles that support further evaluation.

To further validate the role of Siglec-E signaling in vivo, we performed Siglec-E-blocking experiments in the ALI model. Mice were pre-treated with a Siglec-E blocking antibody prior to ALI induction, followed by PPLG-2V-SA administration (Fig. S57). Siglec-E blockade aggravated inflammatory responses and substantially reduced the protective effects of PPLG-2V-SA, as evidenced by increased IL-6 and TNF-α levels in serum and lung tissues. These results further demonstrate that Siglec-E signaling is required for efficient PPLG-2V-SA-mediated regulation of inflammatory responses in vivo. Our data supports a mechanistic sequence whereby prolonged systemic exposure and inflammation-guided lung accumulation enable PPLG-2V-SA to engage with Siglec-expressing innate cells, stabilize inhibitory signaling, lower cytokine/chemokine output, curtail leukocyte influx, and protect lung tissue. The α-helical backbone with divalent epitope topology thus constitutes a most suitable molecular architecture for resolving hyperinflammation while preserving pulmonary integrity.

Antisepsis therapeutic activity of SPMPs in a cecal ligation and puncture-induced cytokine storm model

To determine whether the anti-inflammatory activity of PPLG-2V-SA extended beyond local ALI to systemic cytokine storm, we employed a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) sepsis model, which closely recapitulates the pathological features of human sepsis, including uncontrolled inflammation, cytokine surge, and multi-organ failure. In this model, C57BL/6 mice underwent ligation and puncture of the cecum to induce polymicrobial sepsis (Fig. 6a). Following surgery, mice received intravenous injections of PPLG-2V-SA (2 mg per mouse) or dexamethasone (DEX, 100 µg per mouse), while sham-operated and untreated CLP groups served as controls. At 24 h post-treatment, serum and peritoneal lavage fluids were collected to quantify IL-6, TNF-α, and infiltrating immune cells. Both PPLG-2V-SA and DEX markedly reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels, with PPLG-2V-SA showing superior suppression (Fig. 6b). In consistence with the ALI results, PPLG-2V-SA significantly decreased the amount of monocytes, macrophages and neutrophils in peritoneal fluid (Fig. 6c–e and S58). Moreover, macrophage polarization shifted toward an anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotype, characterized by upregulation of CD206 and downregulation of CD86, while pro-inflammatory M1-like populations decreased. This indicates reprogramming of the local immune environment toward an anti-inflammatory state (Fig. 6f–g).

To evaluate long-term protection, DEX or PPLG-2V-SA was administered daily for eight consecutive days after CLP induction (Fig. 6h). Body weight, temperature, clinical scores, and survival were monitored continuously for 12 h post-surgery and daily thereafter (Fig. 6i). Untreated mice exhibited rapid declines in body weight and temperature, leading to mortality within six days. While DEX treatment only partially delayed death, PPLG-2V-SA effectively preserved body temperature, mitigated early weight loss, and reinstated normal physiological parameters by day 4. Clinical symptoms, including piloerection and lethargy, gradually resolved (Fig. 6j–l). Mechanistically, PPLG-2V-SA engages Siglec-E on macrophages and monocytes, initiating inhibitory signaling that tempers overactivation while maintaining phagocytic function. This immunoregulatory balance permits bacterial clearance and tissue repair without triggering a lethal cytokine storm. Collectively, our results demonstrate that PPLG-2V-SA functions as a potent and selective polymeric immunomodulator capable of suppressing sepsis-induced cytokine storm and averting multi-organ failure.

Conclusions

Precise modulation of innate immune signaling through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) is fundamental to restoring immune homeostasis in inflammation-associated diseases. Despite extensive studies on PRR ligands, natural glycoproteins and glycoconjugates exhibit limited therapeutic utility due to their weak affinity, structural ambiguity, and poor in vivo stability. Achieving selective and durable immunoregulation therefore remains an unmet therapeutic need and may require synthetic molecular systems that can mimic, decode, and reprogram the molecular “patterns” governing PRR signaling with high structural precision and tunability.

We establish the concept of polymer molecular pattern (PMP) as a new class of macromolecular immunomodulators that can rationally engage PRRs through encoded structural cues. Inducing different sialic acid polymer molecular patterns (SPMPs) on helical and random coil polymers, we reveal how polymer backbone conformation and ligand valency cooperate to regulate Siglec clustering and downstream anti-inflammatory signaling. The α-helical, divalently branched PPLG-2V-SA emerged as the most effective structure, selectively activating Siglec-E, reprogramming macrophages toward a tolerogenic phenotype, and conferring effective protection in both cytokine storm and sepsis models, establishing a proof-of-concept for pattern-encoded macromolecular immunomodulators. This work demonstrates that synthetic polymer architectures can indeed function as tunable immune checkpoints, enabling precise control of innate immune responses. The PMP framework provides a structure-guided approach for investigating and designing synthetic macromolecules that regulate immune receptor signaling. Future expansion of this concept to other immune recognition systems will require further optimization of molecular architectures, receptor specificity, and disease-relevant validation.In summary, the PMP concept establishes a molecular framework for encoding immune-regulatory information within polymer structures. Bridging polymer architecture with receptor-level signaling outcomes, this strategy provides a foundation for the rational design of pattern-encoded macromolecular immunomodulators.
