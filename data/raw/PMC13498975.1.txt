Introduction

Persistent air leak (PAL) secondary to bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is a serious complication of secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) resulting from ruptured emphysematous bullae in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Management is particularly challenging in elderly high-risk individuals who are unsuitable for surgical intervention. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP), containing fibrinogen and coagulation factors, may act as a biological sealant and facilitate pleural closure.

Case report

A 76-year-old man, a former smoker with COPD and multiple comorbidities presented with severe acute dyspnea and persistent cough due to right-sided pneumothorax with lung collapse. A water-sealed intercostal chest drain was inserted; although respiratory distress improved, a persistent air leak continued. High-resolution computed tomography confirmed right-sided pneumothorax with associated lung atelectasis and multiple emphysematous bullae. Given the high surgical risk, a total of three units of ABO-compatible fresh frozen plasma (FFP) were administered intrapleurally through the existing chest tube on alternate days. A gradual reduction in air leak was observed, with complete cessation by day 17. The chest tube was removed on day 20 following radiographic confirmation of full lung re-expansion. At 6-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable without recurrence of pneumothorax or the need for further pleural intervention.

Conclusion

Intrapleural FFP pleurodesis may represent a safe, feasible, and cost-effective alternative for managing persistent air leak in high-risk elderly COPD patients when surgical options are contraindicated. Complete resolution may require a prolonged course; however, the favorable outcome without complications reinforces the value of a carefully selected management strategy in this challenging clinical context.

Highlights

• Persistent air leak due to bronchopleural fistula in high-risk COPD patients remains challenging to manage.

• Intrapleural fresh frozen plasma may act as a biological pleural sealant.

• FFP pleurodesis achieved successful air leak resolution without procedure-related complications.

• A structured conservative approach may be effective in carefully selected patients.

• Intrapleural FFP represents a minimally invasive option warranting further evaluation.

1 Introduction

Bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is defined as an abnormal communication between the bronchial tree and pleural space and remains a potentially life-threatening complication in patients with advanced pulmonary disease [1]. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), rupture of emphysematous bullae is a frequent cause of secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) [2]. When pleural drainage fails to seal the visceral pleural defect, the condition may progress to persistent air leak (PAL), generally defined as air leakage lasting more than 5–7 days [2,3]. PAL is associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased infection risk, impaired rehabilitation, and higher mortality, particularly in elderly patients with limited cardiopulmonary reserve [[3], [4], [5]].

Recent pleural disease guidelines emphasize individualized management strategies for SSP and PAL, especially in frail patients who are poor surgical candidates [2]. Although surgical repair remains definitive therapy, perioperative morbidity and mortality may be substantial in elderly patients with severe COPD and multiple comorbidities [6,7]. Chemical pleurodesis using talc or other sclerosants is widely practiced for selected patients with persistent air leaks; however these may induce significant pleural inflammation, pain, fever, and rare systemic complications including acute lung injury [5,7].

Autologous blood patch pleurodesis has gained renewed attention as a minimally invasive approach to PAL management, with encouraging results reported in recent systematic reviews and cohort studies [8,9]. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP), which contains fibrinogen and coagulation factors, may function as a biological sealant by promoting fibrin deposition over pleural defects [[10], [11], [12]]. However, contemporary evidence regarding intrapleural FFP remains limited. We report a case of successful intrapleural FFP pleurodesis in a high-risk elderly patient with PAL secondary to presumed BPF after ruptured emphysematous bullae.

2 Case report

A 76-year-old man, a former smoker with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic renal impairment, and a recent ischemic stroke one month earlier, presented with severe acute dyspnea and persistent cough. He reported no history of fever. On admission, his oxygen saturation was 84% on room air, improving to 96% with supplemental oxygen. Physical examination revealed markedly reduced breath sounds over the right hemithorax.

Initial chest radiography demonstrated a right-sided pneumothorax with partial lung collapse (Fig. 1 a). On admission to the intensive care unit, a 28-Fr intercostal chest tube was inserted and connected to underwater seal drainage. Respiratory distress improved, and oxygen saturation increased to 93–94% on room air without supplemental oxygen. A portable chest radiograph obtained 12 hours later demonstrated partial lung re-expansion; however, a continuous air leak persisted (Fig. 1 b). High-resolution computed tomography of the chest was performed 48 hours to determine the underlying cause of the pneumothorax. Imaging confirmed a right-sided pneumothorax with associated right lung atelectasis and basal pleural thickening. Bilateral emphysematous changes were noted, with areas of fibrosis, septal thickening, and ground-glass opacities. Multiple bullae of variable sizes were observed in both upper and the right middle lobes, contributing to a mosaic attenuation pattern. Despite optimal conservative management including bronchodilator therapy, initial oxygen supplementation, chest physiotherapy, and confirmation of appropriate tube position and function, the air leak persisted beyond 72 hours. Given the patient's advanced age, severe airflow limitation, recent cerebrovascular event and renal impairment, surgical repair was considered prohibitively high risk after multidisciplinary evaluation. Intrapleural biological pleurodesis using fresh frozen plasma (FFP) was therefore performed.

Three units of ABO-compatible fresh frozen plasma (250 mL per unit) were administered intrapleurally through the existing chest tube under strict aseptic precautions on alternate days (hospital days 4, 6, and 8). In contrast to conventional pleurodesis techniques, the chest tube was not clamped because of the ongoing air leak and risk of tension pneumothorax. Instead, the external drainage tubing was looped and positioned above the level of the patient's chest for approximately 6–8 hours. This allowed continued release of air while minimizing outflow of the instilled plasma. The patient was repositioned intermittently to ensure uniform distribution within the pleural cavity. He was closely monitored for potential complications, including fever, hypersensitivity reactions, bronchospasm, and hemodynamic instability.

Intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered for suspected infective exacerbation or pneumonitis, along with bronchodilator therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, and strict glycemic control. Serial chest radiographs demonstrated progressive lung re-expansion without development of subcutaneous emphysema (Fig. 1 c). As a digital chest drainage system capable of objective air-leak quantification (mL/min) was not available at our institution, air leak was assessed clinically using the Cerfolio air leak classification based on bubbling observed in the underwater seal drainage system, [3,7]. Initially, the patient had a continuous air leak (Cerfolio Grade 4, continuous bubbling during both inspiration and expiration). Following serial intrapleural FFP instillations, the air leak gradually decreased and, by hospital day 10, was present only during coughing (Cerfolio Grade 1), consistent with persistent air leak (PAL) and suggestive of bronchopleural fistula [7]. As the patient remained clinically stable and ambulatory, he was discharged on hospital day 10 with the intercostal chest tube connected to water-sealed drainage system in situ.

During outpatient follow-up, a gradual reduction in air leak was subsequently observed, with complete cessation by day 17. The chest tube was removed on day 20 after radiographic confirmation of complete lung re-expansion, following intermittent clamping to ensure stability and absence of recurrent air leak. At 2-week follow-up after chest tube removal, the patient remained clinically stable with sustained lung expansion on chest X-ray and no recurrence of pneumothorax (Fig. 1 d). At 6-month follow-up, he remained free of recurrent pneumothorax and required no further pleural intervention. 3 Discussion

Persistent air leak following SSP in COPD patients presents significant therapeutic challenges. SSP typically affects older individuals and is associated with higher morbidity and recurrence rates compared with primary spontaneous pneumothorax. Prolonged air leakage increases the risk of pleural infection, delayed recovery, and extended hospitalization [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

Although surgical repair remains the gold standard for bronchopleural fistula (BPF) management, perioperative risk is often substantial in elderly patients with severe airflow limitation and multiple comorbidities, including renal impairment and recent cerebrovascular events [6,7]. Contemporary guidelines recommend consideration of minimally invasive approaches in high-risk patients who are poor surgical candidates [2].

Autologous blood patch pleurodesis has demonstrated promising efficacy in recent systematic reviews and multicenter cohort studies, with acceptable safety profiles [8,9]. The mechanism likely involves immediate mechanical sealing produced by coagulated blood followed by fibrin-mediated pleural adhesion [8,9]. Fresh frozen plasma may exert a similar biological effect by supplying fibrinogen and coagulation factors that promote fibrin deposition over visceral pleural defects. Current evidence regarding intrapleural FFP remains limited, with published experience consisting primarily of postoperative case series demonstrating successful closure of persistent air leaks without major procedure-related complications [[10], [11], [12]]. Therefore, FFP may represent a feasible biological alternative when conventional treatment options are unsuitable or unavailable.

In the present case, fresh frozen plasma was selected based on individualized clinical considerations. Although autologous blood patch pleurodesis represents a more established biological approach for persistent air leak, the patient's advanced age, severe airflow limitation, recent ischemic stroke one month earlier and ongoing anticoagulant therapy until the day of chest drain insertion, and multiple comorbidities prompted us to avoid the additional procedural burden and potential discomfort associated with autologous blood collection in a highly frail state. FFP was selected as a readily available, cost effective and practical alternative, providing fibrinogen and coagulation factors that could support localized fibrin formation at the bronchopleural fistula site [11].

Following intrapleural FFP instillation, the progressive reduction and eventual cessation of air leak may reflect fibrin-mediated sealing of the pleural defect. No procedure-related adverse events were observed. Given its biological sealing potential and availability, intrapleural FFP may be considered as an alternative option for managing persistent air leak in selected patients.

Despite the favorable outcome, this report is limited by its single-case design and lack of objective air-leak quantification. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case describing successful intrapleural FFP pleurodesis for persistent air leak associated with secondary spontaneous pneumothorax. Further studies are needed to establish the safety, efficacy and broader applicability of this approach.

4 Conclusion

Intrapleural fresh frozen plasma pleurodesis may serve as a safe, feasible, and cost-effective therapeutic option for persistent air leak due to bronchopleural fistula in high-risk elderly COPD patients who are unsuitable for surgical intervention. Complete resolution may require a prolonged course; however, the favorable outcome without complications reinforces the value of a carefully selected management strategy in this challenging clinical context.
