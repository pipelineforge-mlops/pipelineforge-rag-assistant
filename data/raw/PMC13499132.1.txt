Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global public health concern, affecting more than 9% of adults worldwide and contributing to rising morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. The burden is disproportionately higher in low-resource settings such as Sub-Saharan Africa, where limited diagnostic capacity leads to delayed detection and accelerated progression to kidney failure. In Ethiopia, existing evidence is largely confined to diabetic populations, with minimal data on other high-risk groups. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of CKD and its associated risk factors among individuals with cardio-metabolic risk factors.

Methods

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 March to 30 September 2025. A total of 510 adults with at least one of the cardiometabolic risk factors were enroled to the study. Data were collected through structured interviews, clinical examination, laboratory testing, and abdominal ultrasonography. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD–EPI 2021). Logistic regression was used to identify predictors of CKD.

Results

The mean age of study participants was 61.7 years and 22.35% of them were found to have chronic kidney disease. CKD Stages 3, 4, and 5 were present in 13.92%, 5.69%, and 2.75% of the study participants, respectively. Chronic kidney disease was associated with abnormal waist circumference (P = 0.040), hepatitis B infection (P = 0.021), type 2 diabetes (p = 0.001), hypertension (p = 0.021), and previous acute kidney injury (p = 0.0001).

Conclusion and recommendations

The study demonstrated a substantial burden of CKD with more than one-fifth of adults found to have moderate to severe kidney function impairment. Early screening of high-risk individuals and targeted management should be strengthened. Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) testing, which is relatively simple method of CKD screening, should be made available to improve the early screening and detection of kidney disease.

Highlights

• CKD prevalence was 22.35% among adults with cardio-metabolic risk factors in Northeast Ethiopia.

• Most CKD cases were moderate to severe (Stages 3–5), indicating a substantial disease burden.

• Only 5.5% of CKD cases were aware of their condition, highlighting delayed diagnosis.

• Diabetes, hypertension, obesity, HBV, and previous AKI were independent predictors of CKD.

• Findings support routine CKD screening in high-risk adults attending chronic disease clinics.

Background

According to KDIGO-2024, chronic kidney diseases (CKD) is defined by the presence of structural kidney damage or decreased kidney function for three or more months, irrespective of the causes. Functionally, CKD is diagnosed when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is lower than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or albumin creatinine ratio (ACR) is greater than 30 mg/g for three or more months. The level of kidney injury is graded based on GFR (Grade 1 to Grade 5) and ACR (A1 to A3).1

Globally, the age-standardised prevalence of chronic kidney disease increased by 3.5% between 1990 and 2023, reaching 14.2% in 2023. In Ethiopia, the age-standardised prevalence of chronic kidney disease increased by 8.2% (95% UI: 7.0–9.3%) during the same period. Between 1990 and 2023, global deaths attributable to chronic kidney disease increased from approximately 0.6 million to 1.48 million.2

Approximately 0.1% of the world population has kidney failure (end-stage kidney disease), and this proportion will increase over time. Early stages of CKD, as well as kidney failure, are both associated with high morbidity and increased healthcare utilisation.3 There are striking regional differences in CKD burden. The overall CKD prevalence in Africa is 15.8%, with CKD G3 to G5 accounting for 4.6%. Sub-Saharan Africa including Ethiopia has significantly higher CKD prevalence (17.7%). In Ethiopia, most studies on CKD have been conducted among diabetic patients which rely primarily on chart reviews and showed a CKD prevalence of 14.3–35%.4, 5, 6

Cardio-metabolic risk factors including diabetes Mellitus, Hypertension, obesity and dyslipidaemia are the most common risk factors of CKD worldwide.7 CKD is asymptomatic in its early stages, but it can be identified with simple tests, and its progression can be influenced by public health measures, lifestyle interventions, and inexpensive drug treatments. Many of the drivers of kidney disease can be assessed in the context of addressing the fundamental issues of obesity, hypertension, and diabetes.8

Early detection of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is crucial because early treatment can slow or prevent progression to end-stage kidney disease, its complications and saves millions of dollars due to health cost for treatment of end stage renal disease (ESRD) and its complications. Screening is recommended for individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity and dyslipidaemia. Evaluation should include a urine albumin test and serum creatinine to estimate glomerular filtration rate (GFR).9, 10, 11

In low-resource settings like Ethiopia, CKD prevalence are also influenced not only by late diagnosis, but also by real differences in exposure to CKD risk factors like hypertension, diabetes, infections and other nephrotoxins.12 Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and predictive factors of chronic kidney disease among individuals with cardio-metabolic risk factors in a low-resource settings, where CKD is often underdiagnosed due to limited diagnostic and screening services. The findings will provide evidence to support early detection, improve risk stratification, strengthen CKD screening within non-communicable disease programs, and inform health policy and resource allocation.

Methods

Study design and setting

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 March to 30 September 2025 among adults attending the medical referral clinics of Debre Berhan University Hospital, Debre Berhan Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. These hospitals are located approximately 120 km and 380 km, respectively, from the capital city, Addis Ababa, in North East Ethiopia. Together, they serve a total catchment population of about 13 million people.

Study subjects

Adults aged 18 years and above with cardiometabolic risk factors who attended follow-up care at medical referral clinics during the study period were considered eligible for inclusion in the study. Accordingly, adults with one of the following risk factors for CKD were included in this study: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, impaired fasting blood glucose or HgA1C, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, waist circumference ≥94 cm for male and ≥80 cm for female, BP ≥ 130/85 mmHg or on antihypertensive medication, plasma triglyceride ≥150 mg/dL or on lipid lowering therapy, plasma HDL ≤ 40 mg/dL for male and ≤50 mg/dL for female.

Study variables

Dependent Variable: Chronic kidney disease (CKD).

Independent variables

• Sociodemographic Factors: Age, sex, educational status, marital status, occupation, residence

• Behavioural and lifestyle factors: Alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity level, dietary habits

• Clinical factors: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, body mass index, waist circumference, duration of illness, medication history, previous renal injury

• Laboratory and imaging variables: Creatinine, urine dipstick protein, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, triglycerides, ultrasound findings, HBV surface antigen, HCV antibody, HIV antibody tests.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula by considering a 35% prevalence of CKD among patients with chronic illness in Ethiopia, with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error. A consecutive sampling technique was employed to recruit a total of 510 study participants. When adults attended routine medical follow-up visits at the respective referral clinics, they were screened for cardio-metabolic risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and obesity. Patients identified with at least one of the cardio-metabolic risk factors were invited to participate in the study. All study participants were fully informed about the objectives, procedures, potential benefits, and risks of the study, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment. Adults who declined participation were excluded from the study.

Serum creatinine and other relevant laboratory investigations were performed for all enroled participants, and serum creatinine measurement was repeated after three months. Participants with a persistent estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 were considered to have CKD according to KDIGO criteria. Participants with findings suggestive of CKD were counselled and referred for appropriate clinical evaluation and management. No special events affecting the study participants or clinical care setting were observed during the study period.4

Data collection instrument and procedures

Data were collected through an investigator administered pre-designed questionnaire. Patients were interviewed to obtain socio-demographic data. Focused clinical examination was done to each of study subjects. Relevant medical history and laboratory parameters were obtained from patients’ electronic medical records. Laboratory investigations from blood and urine sample were also done after recruitment. Creatinine and urine dipstick tests were done two times at least three months apart to check the chronicity of the findings so that CKD can be diagnosed accordingly. Ultrasound was done by senior radiologist using sonoscapP15 to check for structural findings.

Blood pressure measurement using a digital machine was done after the patient was allowed to rest quietly for 5 min, while seated comfortably with the arm supported at heart level. A correctly sized cuff was applied on the upper arm over the brachial artery. The device was then started, and it automatically inflated, detected arterial pulsations during deflation, and displayed systolic and diastolic blood pressure on the screen. The cuff was removed after the reading was completed, and the result was recorded. The measurement was repeated to ensure accuracy.

A digital weighing scale with 250.00 kg capacity (Seca787) was used to obtain the weights of the participants. A non-distensible plastic tape with 200.00 cm capacity was used to measure the heights of the study participants with participants leaning against a wall and looking straight ahead, ensuring that the legs are straight and touching together, arms are at the sides, and shoulders are level, to ensure uniformity.

Venous blood samples were collected from study participants after an overnight fast of 8 h. Approximately 3 mL of venous blood was drawn under aseptic conditions into plain serum separator tubes and another 3 mL in to EDTA citrated tube by professional laboratory technicians. The one in serum separator tube were allowed to clot at room temperature for about 30 min, and then centrifuged at 2,500 revolutions per minute for 15 min to obtain clear serum and separated serum was aliquoted and analysed.

Biochemical analysis was performed using an automated clinical chemistry analyzer Siemens EXL 200 in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols and internal quality control standards. Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG) determined by the enzymatic glucose oxidase method. Serum creatinine was measured using the kinetic alkaline picrate method. Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were determined enzymatically to assess the participants’ metabolic profiles. Estimated GFR was calculated using Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD –EPI 2021) based on creatinine alone.

Data quality assurance

To ensure the reliability, validity, and integrity of the study findings, several quality assurance measures were implemented throughout the research process. Before the commencement of data collection, all data collectors were given intensive training including objectives of the study, ethical considerations, data collection procedures, anthropometric measurements, laboratory requisitions, and proper ultrasound examination and reporting. The structured questionnaire and clinical data collection tools were pretested on 20 patients at other hospital and necessary modifications were made based on the pre-test feedback.

Clinical and laboratory measurements were conducted according to standardised procedures. Laboratory testing were performed in a certified laboratory which uses internal and external quality control procedures and standardisation of methods. Reagents were checked for expiry and proper storage. Anthropometric measurements were taken using calibrated equipment and recorded with appropriate precision. Abdominal ultrasound examinations were performed by an experienced radiologist using a consistent technique. All questionnaires were checked daily by data collectors and supervisors to ensure completeness and consistency.

Data analysis

Data were entered into EPI Info version 4.4.1 and transported to STATA version 14 for analysis. Patient characteristics were reported as counts and percentages for categorical variables, and mean with standard deviation for continuous variables. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using CKD–EPI 2021 based on creatinine alone, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using graphical assessment and the Shapiro–Wilk test. All continuous variables were found to be approximately normally distributed. Chi-square test and the t-test were used to determine whether there are significant differences or associations between the dependent and independent variables. Both bi-variate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify associated factors with CKD. Those variables with a P-value <0.2 in the bi-variate analysis were exported to multivariable analysis to control the possible effect of confounders. Variables were selected for the model based on both the observed data and established clinical relevance from previous literatures. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) and P-value <0.05 were used to select independently associated variables with CKD.

Prevalence of CKD among adults with cardiometabolic risk factors

Based on the CKD-EPI 2021 equation, a total of 510 study participants were classified into GFR categories. The majority, 77.65%, had estimated GFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (no CKD). The rest, 22.35% of study participants were found to have persistently low eGFR (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2) on two measurements taken three months apart, which is considered diagnostic of CKD. On further analysis, 13.92%, 5.69%, and 2.75% of the study participants were found to have stages 3, 4, and 5 CKD, respectively. Discussion

This study was conducted to assess the prevalence and predictive factors of CKD among adults with cardiometabolic risk factors based on eGFR. Accordingly, the study found a 22.35% prevalence of CKD. Only 5.5% of the participants reported symptoms and were aware of their disease condition, indicating that the majority of CKD cases were asymptomatic and previously undiagnosed at the time of screening. The figure is much higher than the global prevalence of CKD (10%), North America (13.9%), Germany (18.1%), and most countries in Asia (10.8% to 13.9%).20, 21, 22, 23 The higher prevalence of CKD observed in this study compared with reports from Europe, North America and Asia likely reflects differences in study population, healthcare access, cardiometabolic disease control, and high prevalence of infectious diseases. In Ethiopia, high prevalence of cardiometabolic risks, delayed detection and inadequate management of hypertension, diabetes and other metabolic diseases may contribute to cumulative renal injury. In addition, high prevalence of infectious diseases such as HBV, TB, HIV and other tropical infectious diseases may increase susceptibility to CKD.

Several population and institution-based studies conducted in Africa have also reported lower prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), ranging from 10.7% to 15.8%, compared to the prevalence observed in this study. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study populations, disease risk profiles, and methodological approaches. Many African studies have been conducted in general adult populations, whereas our study focused on individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors, who are inherently at higher risk for CKD. The clustering of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidaemia in our study population likely contributed to the higher observed prevalence.24, 25, 26 A systematic review conducted in Ethiopia reported 18%–17.6% prevalence of CKD among patients with diabetes, which is considerably lower than the prevalence observed in this study. This difference is likely due to variations in the study populations and diagnostic criteria (eGFR formula, albuminuria), and study design.5, 27

In this study, Type 2 DM showed significant association with chronic kidney disease (AOR: 2.47, 95 CI: 1.51–4.04, P = 0.001). This is due to the fact that chronic hyperglycaemia damages the kidney’s microvasculature, leading to progressive loss of function. Similar associations have been reported in numerous studies worldwide.25, 28 Hypertension was also found to be associated with high prevalence of CKD, as sustained high blood pressure causes glomerular injury and accelerates renal decline (AOR: 1.83, 95 CI:1.11–3.02, P = 0.017). These associations are in line with findings from several prior worldwide studies.29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 This highlights the importance of routine renal function monitoring and strict control of blood glucose and blood pressure among high-risk populations, particularly in resource-limited settings where the burden of non-communicable diseases is rapidly increasing. In addition, more sensitive screening methods, such as urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR), should be introduced for the early detection of nephropathy.

Abnormal waist circumference was also identified as significant predictor associated with CKD. This association is explained by the harmful metabolic effects of visceral adiposity. Central obesity promotes insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, leading to glomerular injury and progressive nephron damage. It is also closely linked to hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidaemia, which further accelerate renal decline. Since waist circumference reflects metabolically active visceral fat better than BMI, it is a practical marker of renal risk. Many other studies have also demonstrated an association between abnormal waist circumference and CKD.35 Therefore, routine measurement, periodic renal monitoring, and lifestyle interventions targeting weight reduction are essential to reduce the burden of CKD.

Adults with a previous history of AKI were also about four times more likely to have CKD compared to those without previous history of acute renal injury (AOR = 4.2; 95% CI: 2.12–8.31; p = 0.001). Several other studies also demonstrated similar association between previous acute renal injury and CKD.36, 37, 38, 39 This strong association can be explained by the fact that acute kidney often causes incomplete structural and functional recovery of nephrons. Individuals with a history of AKI should be considered a high-risk group for CKD and enroled in structured post-discharge follow up programs. Regular monitoring of eGFR and urine albumin is essential for early detection of renal decline in adults with previous history of acute kidney injury.

Adults with hepatitis B infection were about three times more likely to have chronic kidney disease compared to those without HBV infection (AOR = 3.06; 95% CI: 1.18–7.89; p = 0.021). Many other studies and systematic analysis also revealed similar association between CKD and hepatitis B infection.40, 41, 42, 43 This association may be explained by immune complex deposition in the glomeruli leading to HBV-associated glomerulonephritis, as well as persistent inflammation and endothelial dysfunction that contribute to progressive renal injury. Therefore, individuals with HBV infection should be considered at high risk for CKD and undergo regular monitoring of renal function.

Despite its limitations, this study demonstrated that a substantial proportion of individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors had asymptomatic CKD with poorly controlled risk factors. These findings underscored the importance of implementing a targeted case-finding approach to early CKD detection within primary healthcare settings. Routine CKD evaluation using serum creatinine–based estimated glomerular filtration rate and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio should be integrated into the care of high-risk groups.

Given existing resource constraints especially in low income countries like Ethiopia, CKD detection efforts should be directly linked to actionable interventions, such as optimisation of blood pressure and glycaemic control and timely referral to appropriate levels of care. Strengthening CKD awareness among healthcare providers and patients and embedding CKD testing within existing non-communicable disease programs may represent a cost-effective strategy to reduce progression to end-stage kidney disease and associated cardiovascular morbidity in Ethiopia. Public health policies should also focus on integrated strategies for screening, prevention, and risk factor management in the broader non-communicable and infectious diseases framework.

Strength and imitations

This study attempted to include all adults with cardiometabolic risk factors, unlike previous studies that focused only on diabetic or hypertensive patients. As a result, it offers valuable insight into the prevalence of CKD among adults with cardiometabolic risk factors in the country and supports the need for targeted screening and prevention strategies to reduce CKD progression in these groups. In this study, eGFR was used as the primary screening tool for CKD; however, eGFR alone has limited sensitivity for detecting early-stage disease. Ideally, both eGFR and the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) should have been measured to identify early CKD. This was not possible due to resource limitations.

The relatively small sample size may also limit the generalisability of the results and reduce the statistical power to detect certain associations. The use of a facility-based cross-sectional study design might limit the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between the identified factors and CKD. In addition, since the study was conducted in health facilities, the findings may not fully represent the general population. Finally, the use of a consecutive sampling technique may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited based on their order of attendance during the study period.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study demonstrated that more than one-fifth of study participants had moderate to severe kidney impairment. Diabetes, hypertension, central obesity, previous history of CKD and hepatitis B virus infection were associated with CKD. Early and routine screening of high-risk individuals using both eGFR and albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) is recommended to detect CKD at an early stage. Preventive interventions targeting diabetes, hypertension, obesity and hepatitis B infection management should be strengthened, and health facilities should prioritise resources to support comprehensive CKD detection and management.
