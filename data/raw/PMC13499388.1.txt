==============================
Nanomedicines have been constructed to enhance the efficacy and safety of traditional anti-cancer drugs. However, the clinical translation of these nanomedicines was hindered by the complexity of carrier materials and functional integration. In this study, a carrier-free nanomedicine composed of doxorubicin (DOX), Cu2+ and siRNA of GPX4 was constructed for combination therapy of tumors. DOX, Cu2+ and siRNA were assembled through multiple forces and decorated with hyaluronic acid (HA) to prepare tumor targeted carrier-free nanomedicines (HDCS). The HA coating on the surface of HDCS ensured specific tumor targeting, and the released drugs triggered by acidity could eliminate tumor cells. The nanomedicine showed significant anti-cancer efficiency and quite reduced side effects in the breast cancer model when compared with DOX solution. HDCS treatment led to significant accelerated production of ROS, reduced GPX4 expression, and sustained decrease in GSH at tumor sites, ultimately forming a powerful chemodynamic effect enhancing circuit. The HDCS provides an effective strategy for tumor combination therapy with good clinical translation and application potential.

1 Introduction

Malignant tumor is a disease that seriously threatens human health and causes huge social burden due to its forefront global morbidity and mortality [[1], [2], [3]]. Chemotherapy, the core means of tumor treatment, can exert a broad-spectrum killing effect against tumors due to its systemic drug delivery characteristics [[4], [5], [6]]. However, drug resistance and non-specific biodistribution also limit the drug efficacy [7,8]. Combination therapy has been proposed with the innovation and development of tumor treatment, including photodynamic therapy (PDT), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), and chemodynamic therapy (CDT) to combine chemotherapy for anti-tumor [[9], [10], [11]]. Compared with PDT and SDT, CDT can be activated by endogenous substances (such as H2O2), avoiding the limitation of tumor hypoxic microenvironment, promoting Fenton/Fenton-like reaction in acidic environment, breaking through the shortcomings of light penetration depth, and saving treatment costs [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. To date, a wide range of nanocarriers including natural biological carriers have been developed and made great progresses in CDT [16].

Although CDT has shown satisfactory application prospects in tumor therapy, studies have found that the effect of CDT is still restricted by the tumor microenvironment (TME) [[17], [18], [19]]. Firstly, the endogenous H2O2 of tumor is insufficient to participate in the Fenton reaction to continuously produce hydroxyl radicals [20,21]. Next, endogenous glutathione (GSH), a vital substance in the antioxidant defense system, is highly expressed in tumors, which can reduce the level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and decrease Fenton/Fenton-like efficiency [[22], [23], [24]]. Thirdly, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) can convert toxic lipid peroxide (LPO) produced by CDT into non-toxic lipid alcohols, thereby reducing chemodynamic efficacy [25,26]. To solve the above problems, it is necessary to design and develop new therapies that can synchronously increase H2O2, reduce GSH contents, and inhibit GPX4 protein expression in tumors to improve the efficiency of CDT.

Doxorubicin (DOX), as a classic chemotherapy drug, can increase intracellular H2O2 levels and disrupt H2O2 homeostasis while exerting chemotherapy effects [27]. Copper possessed a wider pH reaction range and exhibit significantly stronger reactivity in neutral and weakly acidic environments compared to iron-based materials [28,29]. Moreover, Cu2+ can also consume GSH through redox reactions, which would further enhance ·OH generation [30]. DOX can work together with Cu2+ to promote Fenton like reactions, increase ROS levels, and increase lipid peroxidation levels [31]. GPX4 small interfering RNA (siRNA) can effectively silence the GPX gene, further enhancing the efficacy of CDT. However, synchronous delivery of DOX, Cu2+ and siRNA is a challenge faced by pharmaceutics studies. Designed delivery systems should ensure drug stability, targeting efficiency, and timely release. More importantly, it is necessary to ensure the safety of the carrier material.

To achieve ideal anti-tumor therapeutic effects, this study designed a carrier-free nanomedicine that integrated chemotherapy and CDT. Firstly, positively charged DOX-Cu nanoparticles (DC) were prepared by utilizing π-π stacking, hydrophobicity interaction, and metal coordination between DOX and Cu2+. Then, DOX-Cu-siRNA nanoparticles (DCS) were formed by electrostatic binding between positively charged DC and negatively charged GPX4 siRNA. Finally, the carrier-free nanomedicine (HDCS) was constructed by decorating DCS with hyaluronic acid (HA) (Scheme 1A). HDCS can actively target tumor cells by specifically binding with CD44 receptors [32,33], enter the cells through endocytosis, and release DCS by degrading HA with hyaluronidase. The in vivo delivery of siRNA faces typical obstacles of degradation and difficulty in escaping the endosome. HA can protect siRNA from degradation by nucleases in the blood. Cu2+ could help nanoparticles achieve lysosomal escape, enter the cytoplasm in response to GSH and acidic environment disintegration, and release Cu2+, DOX and siRNA to promote cell apoptosis, increase ROS levels, enhance Fenton-like reaction, and inhibit GPX4 expression, finally achieving the combined anti-tumor effect of chemotherapy-CDT (Scheme 1B). Both in vitro and in vivo experiments have confirmed that compared with DOX, HDCS possessed good biocompatibility and could integrate chemotherapy and CDT to enhance anti-tumor efficacy. 2 Materials and methods

2.1 Materials

Doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX·HCl) was purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HA and CuCl2 were bought from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). GSH was supplied by Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was supplied by Gibco Life Sciences (Grand Island, USA). Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8), ROS detection kit, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), rhodamine 123 (RH123), lysosome fluorescent probe, JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential fluorescent probe, and Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit were acquired from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). H2O2 assay kit was bought from Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Monoclonal antibody to GPX4 was provided by Immunoway Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). CD44 Polyclonal antibody was bought from Proteintech Group, Inc (Rosemont, USA). FITC anti-Mouse CD44 antibody was purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). GPX4 siRNA (sense strand: 5′-UGGUCUGCCUGGAUAAGUATT-3′, antisense strand: 5′-UACUUAUCCAGGCAGACCATT-3′), FAM or Cy5-labeled siRNA were customized from Suzhou Jima Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China).

2.2 Cells and animals

BALB/c mice (female, 18–22 g) were supplied by the Laboratory Animal Center of (due to double-blind peer review, the affiliation was temporarily hidden). All animal care and experimental protocols were performed in accordance with the guidelines of Experimental Animal Administrative Committee of (due to double-blind peer review, the affiliation was temporarily hidden).

The mouse breast cancer cell line 4T1 was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2).

2.3 Preparation of HDCS

DOX·HCl and CuCl2 were blended and stirred for 20 min. Saturated NaHCO3 was added to adjust pH and stirred for 30 min. After washed by centrifugation, DOX-Cu nanoparticles (DC) were obtained by low-temperature ultrasound.

DC and siRNA were mixed at various N/P ratios and whirled for 10 s to form different nanoparticles. After incubation for 1 h, DC was added with loading buffer and loaded onto agarose gel (2%, ethidium bromide pre-stained). Electrophoresis (100 V) was conducted for 15 min in 1× TAE buffer and stopped when the bromophenol blue band moved to 2 cm from the lower gel. The DOX-Cu-siRNA nanoparticles (DCS) prepared with the best mass ratio were selected by band strength.

HA (10 mg/ml) was dropped to the prepared DCS dispersion, with HA to DOX mass ratios of 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25 and 1.50. After incubation for 1 h, HA@DOX-Cu-siRNA nanoparticles (HDCS) were prepared.

2.4 Characterization of HDCS

UV visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometry, fluorescence scanning, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) were used to explore the coordination forces between DOX and Cu2+. Full scan spectrum (400–700 nm wavelength range) of DOX, CuCl2, DC and DC + HCl (1 mol/l) was determined by UV–Vis. The λem spectra of DOX, DC and DC + HCl were measured using a microplate reader from 550 nm to 700 nm at λex = 480 nm. Furthermore, 2 mg DC lyophilized powder was mixed with KBr and compressed into tablets to measure the infrared absorption spectrum using FTIR (400–4000 cm−1). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was chosen to identify elements and the valence states of copper in DC.

The particle sizes, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potentials of DC, DCS and HDCS were estimated by Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, Worcestershire, U.K.). Morphologies of DC, DCS and HDCS were observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi, Japan). HDCS was dispersed in PBS with pH 7.4, pH 5 and pH 5 + GSH (10 mM) for 4 h (37 °C, away from light and shaking) to explore the responsiveness. HDCS was loaded onto copper grids for 30 s. After drying the grids, morphological changes were examined by TEM.

Quantitative freeze-dried HDCS were added to 1 mol/ml HCl to disrupt the nanoparticle structure. Ultrasound was used to promote nanoparticle disintegration, and centrifugation was performed to confirm no precipitation. Subsequently, the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and drug loading (DL%) of DOX, siRNA, and Cu2+ were investigated by UV–Vis, fluorescence detection, and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS), respectively.

2.5 Stability and hemolysis of HDCS

HDCS was incubated in water, PBS, and 10% FBS (PBS containing 10% FBS) at 4 °C for 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h. The changes in particle size were explored to investigate the stability of HDCS.

The heparin sodium chelation assay was used to verify whether siRNA could be displaced from HDCS. HDCS (N/P ratio: 1/10) was incubated with heparin sodium (0.1–1.0 mg/ml) for 30 min. Agarose gel electrophoresis and imaging were performed to observe the brightness of free bands to investigate the effect of heparin sodium of different concentrations on the dissociation of siRNA. Free siRNA and HDCS were mixed with an equal volume of FBS (50 µl), incubated in a 37 °C constant temperature oscillator, and the samples (10 µl) were taken at each time point. Then, the serum stability of siRNA in HDCS and free siRNA in serum was investigated by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Biocompatibility of HDCS was investigated through hemolysis test. Erythrocytes were freshly isolated from BALB/c mice. Equal volumes of deionized water (positive control), PBS (negative control, pH 7.4), DOX, DCS and HDCS were blended with the erythrocyte suspension and incubated for 2 h. After centrifugation (3000 rpm/10 min), the absorbance was detected at 545 nm. The hemolysis percentage of HDCS was calculated by following the formula:Hemolysis(%)=(Asample−Anegative)(Apositive−Anegative)×100%

2.6 Chemodynamic performance of HDCS

·OH generation ability: Methylene blue (MB) could be reduced to colorless by reacting with hydroxyl radicals. Therefore, MB was chosen to evaluate the ·OH generation ability of HDCS. HDCS (100 µg/ml), GSH (1 mM), MB (10 µg/ml), and H2O2 (10 mM) were blended at different pH (7.4, 6.3, and 5.0) and shaken for 2 h. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation and determined using UV–Vis to evaluate ·OH generation ability of HDCS under different conditions. In addition, different HDCS concentrations (10, 25, 50, 100, 150 and 200 µg/ml) and various incubation times (0, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120 and 150 min) were also performed to investigate the ·OH production ability of HDCS.

GSH consumption ability: DTNB would turn yellow when thiol compounds were present. PBS solution containing DTNB (2.5 mg/ml), HDCS (100 µg/ml), and GSH (10 mM) was incubated at different times (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 h), and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm using UV–Vis to evaluate the GSH consumption ability of HDCS.

2.7 Drug release behavior of HDCS

Release behaviors of DOX and siRNA from HDCS were determined at different pH with or without GSH (10 mM). Briefly, nanoparticles suspension was sealed in a dialysis bag (Mw, 3500 Da), which was immersed in the release medium (PBS with pH5, PBS with pH 7.4, PBS with pH 5 + GSH, and PBS with pH 7.4 + GSH). Dialysate was sampled at different time points and replaced with fresh medium of equal volume. Cumulative release of DOX and siRNA from HDCS was quantified using UV–Vis and fluorescence detection, respectively.

2.8 Targeting capacity of HDCS on tumor cells

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry (FCM) were employed to evaluate the uptake ability of 4T1 cells to HDCS. In brief, 4T1 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were inoculated in 12-well plates and incubated with DOX, FAM dye labeled siRNA (FAM-siRNA), DC, DCS and HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml). After 4 h, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI to label cell nuclei. Cellular images were obtained using CLSM. 4T1 cells were incubated with DOX, FAM-siRNA, DC, DCS and HDCS. After 4 h, cells were collected and analyzed using FCM.

To verify the CD44 receptor-mediated uptake of HDCS by 4T1 cells, HA was used as a competitive antagonist. 4T1 cells were pre-incubated in HA (1 mg/ml) and then treated with HDCS medium. CLSM and FCM were used for analysis according to the above steps.

2.9 Lysosomal escape ability of HDCS

4T1 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were inoculated into 12-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After incubation with HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 1, 2 and 4 h. Lysotracker red was added to each well to stain lysosomes for 45 min. The intracellular distribution of FAM-siRNA and lysosomes at different time points was examined by CLSM.

2.10 Intracellular chemodynamic behaviors of HDCS

·OH detection: 4T1 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS, and HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml). After 4 h, DCFH-DA (10 µM) was added and stained for 20 min. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI to label cell nuclei. Cellular images were visualized using CLSM. 4T1 cells were cultured with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS. After 4 h, samples were collected and analyzed using FCM.

GSH detection: 4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 4, 8 and 12 h. Cell lysis buffer (100 µl) was added and lysed for 30 min. The supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min) and incubated with DTNB (20 mg/ml) for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader.

MDA detection: Malondialdehyde (MDA) was selected as an indicator for detecting lipid peroxidation levels. 4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS (DOX 5 µg/mL, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 24 h. The cell lysis buffer was added and incubated for 30 min. The supernatant was blended with 200 µl MDA detection working solution according to the instructions of the MDA kit. The absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader.

H2O2 detection: 4T1 cells were cultured in the medium containing HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 4, 8, 12 and 24 h. Cells (5 million) were mixed with 1 mL acetone and disrupted by ultrasound. After centrifugation, the intracellular H2O2 content was determined using H2O2 assay kit.

2.11 Cytotoxicity and apoptosis ability

4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS at various concentrations of DOX (0, 2, 5, 10, 15 and 20 µg/ml) for 24 h. Medium containing 10% CCK-8 (100 µl) was added and incubated for 1.5 h. The optical density (OD) value was measured at 450 nm to calculate the cell viability.

4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 24 h. After centrifugation, samples were collected and stained with 4 µl Annexin V-FITC and 8 µl PI. Cells were collected for apoptosis analysis using FCM.

2.12 Mitochondrial potential detection

4T1 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were treated with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS (DOX 5 µg/mL, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 6 h. JC-1 working solution (20 µM) was supplied and incubated for 30 min. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI to label cell nuclei. The fluorescence distribution of JC-1 was examined using CLSM.

4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS for 6 h. RH123 (2 µM) was used to stain for 20 min. After centrifuging and resuspending, the fluorescence intensity of each sample was detected by FCM.

2.13 GPX4 protein expression

4T1 cells were treated with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS (DOX 5 µg/ml, siRNA 0.27 µg/ml) for 24 h. RIPA lysis buffer was added, the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation. The expression of GPX4 protein was determined by Western Blotting and ELISA.

2.14 Biodistribution of HDCS in vivo

4T1 cells (2 × 106) in logarithmic growth phase were inoculated into the left axilla of BALB/c mice (6 weeks, 15–18 g, female). After the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, the breast cancer mouse model was successfully established.

To investigate in vivo distribution of HDCS, Cy5-labeled DCS and HDCS were intravenously injected into tumor bearing mice. Cy5 signals were analyzed via FX Pro imaging system (Bruker, Inc., USA) at different time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h). Tumors and the major organs were dissected and imaged at 24 h.

2.15 Anti-tumor effect of HDCS

Tumor bearing mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (with 6 mice in each group) and intravenously injected with PBS, DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS (DOX 5 mg/kg, siRNA 0.27 mg/kg). The treatment was conducted every 3 d for 5 times. During the treatment, tumor volume and body weight were continuously recorded. The tumor volume (V) could be calculated according to the formula: V = ab2/2, where a is the longest diameter and b is the shortest diameter of the tumor. After the treatment, the mice were euthanized to dissect tumors. The tumor weight was measured, and the tumor inhibition rate (TIR) was calculated according to the following formula:TIR(%)=m1−m2m1×100%

where m1 represented the average mass of tumors in control group, and m2 represented the mass of tumors in treatment group.

The tumors from different groups were obtained to observe histopathological changes by hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E). After dehydration treatment, tumors were embedded in wax, sliced with a thickness of 5 µm, and stained in H&E staining solutions for 10 and 5 min in sequence. After sealing, the slices were observed under a microscope. To further investigate the therapeutic effect, tumor sections were stained with TUNEL, and the distribution of apoptotic cells was examined using an optical microscope. Moreover, proteins were extracted from tumor tissues to evaluate the ability of HDCS to inhibit GPX4 expression using western blotting and ELISA analysis.

2.16 In vivo safety of HDCS

The serum of tumor bearing mice treated by each group was collected for blood biochemistry testing. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CR) were measured to detect liver and kidney function. Furthermore, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) were obtained to investigate the damage to organs after treatment by H&E staining.

2.17 Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out at least three times. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Significances between the groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences were considered significant at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. No significant differences were marked as ns.

3 Results and discussion

3.1 Construction and characterization of HDCS

The π-π stacking, metal coordination and hydrophobic interaction between DOX and copper ions (Cu2+) were used to prepare DC, which then electrostatically bound with negatively charged siRNA and was coated with HA to form carrier-free nanoparticles HDCS (Fig. 1A). The optimal molar ratio of DOX to Cu2+ was evaluated based on particle size, encapsulation efficiency, combination index (CI) and cell survival rate, and ultimately 1:2 was chosen for subsequent research (Table S1 and Fig. S1). The elemental composition of DC could be qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed using XPS. The characteristic peaks contained Cu, O, N and C elements could be seen from the full spectrum, which proved the successful preparation of DC (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, high-resolution scanning of Cu2+was performed, the results illustrated that Cu 2p peaks of the Cu element in DC were located at 934.57 eV and 954.78 eV, indicated a + 2-valence state for Cu (Fig. 1C). The UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy results revealed that the maximum absorption peak of DOX was located at 480 nm, whereas that of DC exhibited a red shift (Fig. 1D). After the addition of HCl, the characteristic absorption of DOX resumed, indicated that the coordination interaction between DOX and Cu²⁺ was dissociated under acidic conditions (Fig. 1D). Fluorescence spectra of DOX, DC and DC + HCl were detected by fluorescence full scan. The results discovered that DOX exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity at λem = 588 nm, and the fluorescence was quenched in DC, demonstrated the coordination binding of DOX with Cu2+. After HCl was added to DC, fluorescence of DOX was restored due to the dissociation between DOX and Cu2+ (Fig. 1E). In the FTIR spectrum of DC, the bands associated with the hydroxyl group (970 cm⁻¹) and carbonyl group (912 cm⁻¹) in the DOX spectrum have disappeared. DCS was formulated through the electrostatic binding between DC and GPX4 siRNA. Agarose gel electrophoresis experiments revealed that the bands vanished at an N/P ratio of 10, indicated electrostatic binding between siRNA and DC (Fig. 1G). Particle sizes of DCS decreased from 208.9 ± 16.0 nm to 124.3 ± 4.5 nm with the N/P ratio increased (Fig. S2A). When the electrostatic interaction was weak, the rigid structure and low charge density of siRNA were inadequate to form compact complexes [36,37]. Subsequently, the particle size changed insignificantly with the increase of the N/P ratio. Moreover, when the N/P ratio was 10:1, the CI value was about 0.18, indicating a strong synergistic effect (Fig. S2B). Therefore, an N/P ratio of 10 was used for the subsequent studies. HDCS was prepared by utilizing the electrostatic and metal coordination interactions between DCS and HA. Agarose gel electrophoresis results demonstrated that no distinct bands were observed after adding HA, indicated that the decoration of HA did not result in siRNA leakage (Fig. 1H). As HA mass ratio increased, the HDCS particle size reached a minimum value of 181.8 ± 7.8 nm at the mass ratio of 1:1 (Fig. S3). This alteration might be due to the interaction between HA and DCS became stronger when HA increased, resulted in a reduction in nanoparticle size. However, when HA increased to a certain proportion, its large molecular weight would cause the particle size to increase again [38].

The particle sizes of DC, DCS and HDCS were 109.7 ± 1.0 nm, 124.3 ± 4.5 nm and 181.8 ± 7.8 nm, respectively, and TEM results showed that the nanoparticles were uniformly spherical (Fig. 1I–1K and Table S2). The reduced zeta potential in DCS indicated successful binding of siRNA and DC through electrostatic interaction, while the negative zeta potential of HDCS indicated successful modification of DCS with HA (Fig. 1L and Table S2). The EE% of DOX and siRNA in HDCS were 97.03% ± 2.54% and 98.34% ± 2.07%, respectively (Table S2). The DL% of Cu2+, DOX and siRNA in HDCS were 17.71% ± 0.48%, 38.28% ± 1.44% and 2.07% ± 0.34%, respectively (Table S2). The heparin sodium chelation experiment observed that siRNA could be completely replaced from HDCS when the concentration of heparin sodium was 0.8 mg/mL. Subsequently, the stability of siRNA in serum was determined using this concentration (Fig. 1M). Free siRNA and HDCS were incubated with serum in a shaker at 37 °C for different periods of time. The siRNA in the preparation was replaced with heparin sodium, and the stability of siRNA was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Fig. 1N, free siRNA bands almost disappeared at 6 h, whereas the siRNA in HDCS could remain stable for 24 h. HDCS successfully improved the serum stability of siRNA. The particle size distribution of HDCS dispersed in various media was determined to evaluate the stability of HDCS. As shown in Fig. S4, no significant change was observed in the particle size after 72 h, indicated that HDCS exhibited good stability under storage conditions and could be used for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experimental studies.

3.2 Release behaviors and chemodynamic properties of HDCS

HDCS could dissociate in response to the acidic environment and high GSH content at the tumor site (Fig. 2A). The degradation ability of HDCS was investigated by simulating the TME in vitro. As the pH changed from 7.4 to 5, the morphology of HDCS changed from regular circular to irregular polygonal, indicated that the nanoparticles were degraded in an acidic environment. When HDCS was dispersed in PBS with pH 5 + GSH (10 mM), the nanoparticle morphology appeared fragmented, indicated the TME responsiveness of HDCS (Fig. 2B). The cumulative release of DOX was 10.91% ± 0.11% within 72 h at pH 7.4, but increased to 54.05% ± 3.17% after the addition of GSH (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the release of DOX reached 82.52% ± 1.42% at pH 5 + GSH (10 mM). The increase in drug release might be due to the dissociation of DC in an environment rich in H+ and GSH. The in vitro release characteristic of siRNA was consistent with DOX. As illustrated in Fig. 2D, the cumulative release of siRNA reached 85.52% ± 6.13% in PBS with pH 5 + GSH (10 mM). These results indicated that HDCS possessed the highest efficiency in releasing DOX and siRNA when exposed to acidic and GSH environments. The chemodynamic properties of HDCS were further investigated. The absorbance significantly decreased at 665 nm when the pH changed from 7.4 to 5, the decrease in absorbance indicated MB degradation by ·OH, thus confirming ·OH generation. The result might be due to that HDCS could convert H2O2 into ·OH under acidic conditions (Fig. 2E). In addition, the absorbance under different incubation times and HDCS concentrations was also detected. The production of ·OH by HDCS was time and concentration dependent (Fig. 2F and 2G). Moreover, the ability of HDCS to deplete GSH was determined. The GSH concentration gradually reduced with increasing incubation time, which was beneficial for reducing ROS consumption and amplifying CDT effects (Fig. 2H). In vitro hemolysis test is a vital tool to investigate the blood compatibility of intravenous nanomedicine. The hemolysis rate of HDCS group decorated with HA was less than 5% at different DOX concentrations, indicated the highest biocompatibility compared to the DOX or DCS (Fig. S5).

3.4 Intracellular distribution and chemodynamic analysis of HDCS

Successfully escaping from lysosomes after being taken up by cells is the key for siRNA to exert the gene silencing effect [41,42]. Therefore, the distribution of HDCS within 4T1 cells after internalization was investigated using CLSM. After incubated with 4T1 cells for 1 h, the green signals of HDCS almost completely overlapped with the red signals of lysosomal dyes, which indicated that HDCS were mainly captured in lysosomes (Figs. 3C and S8). The green fluorescence gradually separated from the red fluorescence after 2 and 4 h, indicated that HDCS could achieve lysosomal escape and effectively transport drugs to tumor cells (Figs. 3C and S8).

Intracellular ROS levels were determined using DCFH-DA probe. The green fluorescence was almost not observed in control group, but was observed in DOX group, which might be due to the activation cascade reaction of NADPH—NOXs-SOD by DOX to increase H2O2 concentration in tumors (Fig. 3D). The fluorescence intensity in DC group was stronger than DOX group, indicated that Cu2+ and DOX could synergistically enhance ·OH generation (Fig. 3D). HDCS group displayed the strongest fluorescence, which certified that HDCS possessed the strongest ability to generate ROS (Figs. 3D and S9A). FCM experiment also confirmed the above results by detecting DCF fluorescence (Figs. 3E and S9B). HDCS could significantly increase intracellular ROS levels in 4T1 cells compared with other preparations, indicated that the combination of DOX, Cu2+, siRNA and increased targeting ability of HDCS synergistically enhanced ROS levels.

The intracellular chemodynamic behavior of HDCS was detected. H2O2 is an important substrate for the Fenton reaction and has significant implications for promoting CDT. The intracellular H2O2 concentration was detected using a commercial kit. H2O2 concentration in DOX group increased approximately 1.5 times than that of PBS group (Fig. S10A), which was due to that DOX could effectively increase H2O2 levels through the NADPH—NOX-SOD pathway. After treated with HDCS, the H2O2 content showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing over time, and the level of ·OH obviously increased at 24 h (Fig. S10B and S10C). This phenomenon might be because HDCS could effectively increase intracellular H2O2 levels within 12 h, and then Cu+ converted H2O2 to ·OH in Fenton reaction, finally resulting in a reduction in H2O2 levels in cells.

GSH has important antioxidant functions in cells and is important for maintaining intracellular redox balance [43]. Therefore, consuming GSH is generally considered an effective method to increase intracellular ROS levels. HDCS could degrade in response to GSH and reduce intracellular GSH levels. DTNB was used to detect GSH content, as shown in Fig. S10, where the GSH content decreased from 100% to 43.5% ± 3.3% over time (Fig. S10D). This illustrated that HDCS possessed the potential to enhance CDT effects by reducing GSH. MDA is usually selected to explore lipid oxidation [44]. Therefore, MDA assay kits were used to detect intracellular lipid oxidation levels after treatment with different formulations. HDCS group exhibited the highest MDA levels, which were about 2.5 times higher than the PBS group (Fig. S10E). In addition, antioxidant supplementation was conducted and the results showed that MDA levels significantly decreased in HDCS group after treated with antioxidant (Fig. S10F). This result suggested the capacity of HDCS to catalyze the production and accumulation of peroxidized lipids.

3.5 Cytotoxicity and apoptosis of HDCS on 4T1 cells

Cytotoxicity of different formulations was analyzed using CCK8 method and FCM (Figs. 4A and S11). With the increase of DOX concentration in the formulation, all groups of formulations showed dose-dependent toxicity. The viability of 4T1 cells was less than 10% in HDCS group at 20 µg DOX/ml, indicated the strongest tumor cell killing effect (Fig. S11A). The IC50 values of DOX, DC, DCS, and HDCS groups for 4T1 cells were 8.38 ± 0.58, 8.07 ± 0.38, 6.61 ± 0.26, and 5.32 ± 0.08 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. S11B). The HDCS group showed the smallest IC50 value, indicated that HDCS had the strongest toxicity to 4T1 cells. The apoptosis rates were determined using FCM. The early/late apoptosis in 4T1 cells induced by HDCS (16.2% and 34.8%) was higher than that of DOX (7.29% and 28.1%), DC (8.26% and 27.3%), and DCS (12.7% and 29.1%), demonstrated that HDCS exhibited the strongest killing effect on 4T1 cells (Fig. 4A). The mitochondrial membrane potential increases in normal cells, where JC-1 aggregates and produces red fluorescence [45]. On the contrary, the mitochondrial membrane potential decreases in early apoptotic cells, where JC-1 disperses and produces green fluorescence (Fig. 4B). The CLSM results illustrated that the red fluorescence decreased and the green fluorescence increased after incubation with different formulations (Fig. 4C). The DC group exhibited stronger green fluorescence than the DOX group, indicated that DC could enhance oxidative stress mediated by Cu2+ and combine with DOX to induce ·OH accumulation in 4T1 cells. The green fluorescence intensity of DCS group is stronger than DC group, because the silencing effect of siRNA on GPX4 protein could lead to increased lipid oxidation and mitochondrial damage in the cells. The HDCS group exhibited the highest green fluorescence, indicated that the combined administration effectively activated the mitochondrial dependent apoptotic pathway, causing severe mitochondrial damage to 4T1 cells (Figs. 4C and S12A). RH123 is a live cell mitochondrial fluorescent dye, which fluorescence intensity can reflect the level of mitochondrial membrane potential. The fluorescence quenching degree of RH123 in HDCS group was the strongest detected by FCM (Figs. 4D and S12B), demonstrated that HDCS could cause cell death by damaging mitochondria through ROS.

Western blotting and ELISA were used to detect the ability of siRNA to inhibit GPX4 expression. The GPX4 protein expression decreased in the DOX, DC, DCS and HDCS groups (Fig. 4E–4G). This might be due to that DOX could enhance the immunogenicity of tumors and suspend downstream GPX4 related pathways [46,47]. The HDCS group had the shallowest imprint because Cu2+ and siRNA inhibited GPX4 expression by consuming GSH and silencing GSH genes, respectively (Figs. 4E–4G and S13). The mRNA expression of GPX4 in 4T1 cells was detected by RT-qPCR. Compared to the DCNC and HDCNC (nanoparticles containing siRNA negative control) groups, the expression of GPX4 mRNA was significantly reduced in the DCS and HDCS groups (Fig. S14). In addition, HA modification endowed HDCS with targeting ability toward tumor cells, enabling HDCS to have the strongest inhibitory effect on GPX4 protein expression.

The release behavior of HDCS in 4T1 cells was investigated, and the results showed that the cumulative release of DOX and siRNA from HDCS gradually increased since 4 h and reached about 85% at 24 h (Fig. S15). This might be due to the escape of HDCS from lysosomes to the cytoplasm after 4 h (Fig. 3C), and then disintegrated under high intracellular GSH levels to release DOX and siRNA.

4 Conclusion

This research developed a carrier-free nanoparticle (HDCS), which was composed of DOX-Cu-siRNA and coated with HA. HDCS with good stability and environmental responsiveness had been successfully prepared through various forces such as π-π stacking, metal coordination, and electrostatic bonding. In vitro cell experiments proved that HDCS could effectively promote tumor cell apoptosis, reduce intracellular GSH content, increase ROS levels, and inhibit GPX4 expression. Moreover, HDCS could specifically target tumor sites and release DOX, Cu and siRNA in response to acidic TME and high GSH concentration, thereby achieving the effects of integrated chemotherapy and chemodynamic therapy in vivo. The construction of HDCS provided a novel nanomedicine for the combination therapy of tumors.
