Abstract

Introduction

COVID-19 vaccination campaigns have successfully limited the spread of the disease and reduced its severity. Neurological side effects of COVID-19 vaccines are rare. To the best of our knowledge, only a few case reports have shown an association between the COVID-19 vaccine and the development of probable acute fulminant demyelination.

Case Presentation

In this case report, we investigated and documented a temporal association suggesting a possible causal link between COVID-19 vaccination and acute fulminant demyelination, supported by a review of the existing literature. We describe the case of a 30-year-old female who experienced severe neurological symptoms, including memory loss, disorientation, paranoia, hallucinations, and urinary incontinence, just 3 days after receiving the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine booster. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multiple T2 lesions with contrast enhancement consistent with demyelination. The condition rapidly progressed to somnolence, disorientation, aphasia, and quadriparesis. Treatment was initiated with pulse intravenous methylprednisolone, plasma exchange, prednisolone, natalizumab, and high-dose cyclophosphamide, resulting in disease stabilization, which was complicated by febrile neutropenia and meningitis. Fortunately, gradual improvement was observed, with MRI showing no new lesions on the follow-up imaging.

Conclusion

The literature review revealed limited case reports on COVID-19 vaccine-induced central nervous system demyelination, with no consensus on its link to the Marburg variant of multiple sclerosis. This case highlights the heterogeneity of MS pathogenesis, risk factors, and challenges in managing severe clinical deterioration. Given the rare nature of Marburg MS, early detection and management of the disease lead to a better outcome and prognosis.

Introduction

Vaccines are effective in preventing a wide range of infectious diseases and in mitigating their negative effects. Despite the proven benefits of vaccines, they have also been linked to the development of autoimmune diseases. The COVID-19 vaccination campaigns have played a significant role in limiting the spread and severity of the disease. Notably, the neurological side effects of the vaccine are infrequent [1]. To the best of our knowledge, only a handful of case reports and case series have demonstrated a connection between the COVID-19 vaccine and the occurrence of central nervous demyelination. When preparing this manuscript, there were no reports of patients developing the Marburg variant of multiple sclerosis after the COVID-19 vaccination. In this report, we describe the patient course and outcome and discuss a temporal relationship suggesting a possible causal link between COVID-19 vaccination and the subsequent development of fulminant demyelination.

Case Presentation

A 30-year-old female was hospitalized for evaluation of acute-onset memory impairment, disorientation, paranoia, visual and auditory hallucinations, in addition to urinary incontinence. The symptoms started 3 days after the 3rd dose of the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19. Her medical history was remarkable for an uncomplicated sleeve gastrectomy a few years prior and no prior COVID-19 infection. She had received the first and second doses of the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine in 2024 without incident.

She was initially evaluated at another hospital, where MRI showed multiple T2 lesions with contrast enhancement (see Fig. 1). She received pulse intravenous methylprednisolone for 5 days before being transferred to our hospital for further management. Upon arrival, neurological examination revealed somnolence, disorientation, expressive aphasia, and asymmetric spastic quadriparesis (worse on the left side). The pupils were equal and reactive. There were no meningeal signs. Repeated brain MRI showed significant progression and an increased size and number of white matter lesions in the subcortical white matter, periventricular region, and brainstem. Some lesions were large and surrounded by vasogenic edema and showed peripheral restriction on diffusion-weighted images. Postcontrast imaging showed incomplete ring enhancement in many of those lesions. Spinal cord MRI showed multiple short-segment intramedullary lesions affecting the cervical and thoracic parts with patchy post-contrast enhancement. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was acellular, with negative herpes simplex PCR results and absence of oligoclonal bands. Investigations for autoimmune encephalitis, paraneoplastic syndromes, and testing for infections, including tuberculosis, syphilis, Brucella, HSV, HIV, and CMV, were negative. Serum and cerebrospinal fluid tests for anti- myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) and anti-aquaporin-4 antibodies were negative. Anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody testing was not performed due to resource limitations at the time of admission. At that point, the differential diagnoses included acute fulminant demyelination, such as Marburg’s variant of multiple sclerosis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders. The absence of anti-MOG and aquaporin-4 antibodies excludes MOGAD and NMO. Susac syndrome was suspected; however, the absence of hearing impairment and visual presentation along with the presence of cord lesions diminished the likelihood of this diagnosis. According to the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality assessment system, this adverse event was categorized as “possible.” This classification is supported by a reasonable temporal relationship between vaccination and symptom onset (3 days), while extensive investigations excluded infectious, paraneoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies. Furthermore, the absence of a more plausible alternative explanation fulfills the WHO-UMC definition of a “possible” causal association. The diagnosis was probable acute fulminant demyelination. A brain biopsy was considered but not performed due to concerns about morbidity. She underwent five sessions of plasma exchange and was maintained on daily oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg). Unfortunately, her clinical condition did not improve, and follow-up MRI showed evidence of new and enhanced supra-infratentorial lesions. To achieve quick and effective immunosuppression, she was treated with one dose of natalizumab administered immediately as a bridge therapy to block lymphocyte trafficking across the blood-brain barrier followed in 3 weeks by a high dose of cyclophosphamide (HiCy) using the HiCy protocol [2]. Natalizumab acts by preventing T-cell migration into the central nervous system through blockade of VLA-4 interactions with VCAM-1 on cerebral endothelial cells [3]. Additionally, treatment is associated with an increased proportion of activated leukocytes in peripheral blood, which the authors attribute to sequestration of activated cells within the circulation [3].

The treatment course was complicated by febrile neutropenia, seizures, and Gram-negative meningitis, which were successfully controlled with antimicrobials, antiepileptics, and supportive management. At the peak of illness, the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score was 9, indicating a completely helpless and bedridden state, and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was 5, consistent with severe disability. The patient’s condition stabilized and gradually improved. Even with complete bone marrow suppression, follow-up MRI showed the presence of a new enhancing cord lesion, although most of the other lesions were not enhanced. The patient gradually improved, opened her eyes, and interacted with the surroundings. However, she experienced significant cognitive impairment with residual hemiparesis and was discharged for rehabilitation. At discharge, the EDSS score was 7, reflecting wheelchair dependence with the ability to transfer, and the mRS score was 4, consistent with moderately severe disability requiring assistance with most daily activities. MRI showed no new lesions 2 months after cyclophosphamide administration. Following the recovery of the bone marrow, she started regular rituximab infusion every 6 months, which was well tolerated.

Discussion

The association between immunization and autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis has been debated. Some reports have indicated that MS exacerbates following yellow fever vaccination [4]. Another report described the development of the Marburg variant of MS shortly after pneumococcal vaccination [2]. However, larger studies have not shown an increased risk of MS after vaccination [5]. Given the close temporal relationship in our patient and the exclusion of alternative etiologies, this case is best characterized as a temporal association consistent with a possible link.

The patients in this study developed symptoms after receiving a booster dose of COVID-19 vaccine manufactured by Pfizer (BNT162b2). BNT162b2 is an mRNA vaccine with nucleoside modifications that encodes SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein [6]. mRNA vaccines have shown an activation effect on type-I interferon responses, which are part of inflammatory processes and autoimmunity [7–9].

Cosentino and Marino [10] propose that, under certain circumstances, mRNA vaccination may result in the production of relatively high levels of spike protein within various organs and tissues, with the potential for this protein to enter systemic circulation. They emphasize that findings from animal studies demonstrate systemic distribution of lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA, with expression observed in organs such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and lungs. Based on these observations, the authors raise the possibility that tissue-specific spike protein production and subsequent circulation could contribute to adverse effects, depending on the quantity produced and the sites of expression [10]. This raises concerns regarding potential central nervous system involvement.

Trougakos et al. [11] discuss that the S1 subunit can cross the blood-brain barrier in animal models and accumulate in brain tissue. They further describe that S1 activates microglia and promotes caspase-1-mediated inflammatory signaling. Importantly, they suggest that S1 may function as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern, meaning it can trigger innate immune responses and induce neuroinflammation even without active viral infection. These findings support the hypothesis that spike protein may exert pro-inflammatory effects within the central nervous system under certain conditions [11]. These mechanistic considerations provide theoretical biological plausibility; nevertheless, current evidence does not permit definitive conclusions regarding causality in individual cases.

In addition to innate immune activation, adaptive immune mechanisms such as molecular mimicry have also been proposed. Qiu et al. [12] described 2 cases of multiple sclerosis with clinical and radiological onset shortly after mRNA-based SARS-CoV-2 spike vaccination. Immunological analyses identified proinflammatory CD4+ T cell clones in both peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid that cross-recognized spike protein-derived peptides and myelin antigens, including myelin basic protein and MOG. These findings provide direct evidence of antigen-specific cross-reactivity and support molecular mimicry as a biologically plausible mechanism contributing to MS onset in rare susceptible individuals. Nevertheless, such observations remain limited to isolated cases and do not establish a causal relationship at the population level [12].

A few case reports and case series have reported a potential association between CNS demyelination and COVID-19 vaccines [13–17]. However, studies examining many patients with MS who received COVID-19 vaccine did not show increased inflammatory activity [18]. Bertozzi et al. [19] reported the outcomes of 114 MS patients who received a booster vaccine dose. In this study, only 4 patients experienced disease reactivation [19]. A meta-analysis examining the safety of COVID-19 vaccines among 14,755 patients with MS revealed that 2% experienced relapse within a few weeks after vaccination [20].

The Marburg variant of multiple sclerosis is used to describe several forms of demyelinating diseases affecting the central nervous system, such as malignant, acute fulminant, and rapidly progressing multiple sclerosis [2]. Otto Marburg initially described aggressive forms of central nervous system demyelination as MS variants in 1905 [21]. He documented 3 cases of fulminant demyelinating disease, which he believed was a specific type of MS with rapid clinical progression. In the initial cases, hypercellular demyelinating plaques, a moderate astroglial reaction, the appearance of enlarged astrocytic cells, and edema were observed during neuropathological analysis. Subsequently, acute and subacute MS subtypes have been reported. In some studies, approximately 4% of patients die within 4 years of diagnosis [22]. The clinical course is usually a few months and often presents with clinical manifestations atypical for MS, including hemiplegia, hemianopsia, seizures, aphasia, and disorientation. The most significant differential diagnoses are Schilder diffuse sclerosis, Balo’s concentric sclerosis, and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis [2]. MRI on T2-weighted images sequence reveals large demyelinating lesions with frequent hyperintensity and prevalence of edema. These lesions may resemble brain tumors in terms of size and associated edema. On further examination, these lesions showed a reduction in size after steroid treatment. Gadolinium enhancement has also frequently been observed [22]. CSF analysis showed increased protein levels, whereas cells typically ranged from slightly increased to normal. Oligoclonal bands can also be observed, although not as frequently as in MS. Neuropathological investigations have shown that most plaques appear newly formed, although older plaques have also been described [22]. A brain biopsy is not always necessary to make an accurate diagnosis and start treatment as a detailed clinical history, CSF analysis, serial MRI scans, and close follow-ups can be sufficient [22].

However, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the Marburg variant remain unclear. Some authors have suggested that the severe course is attributable to the distribution of demyelinating plaques predominantly in the brainstem. Other studies have suggested a severe immunopathological response [22]. However, some studies have demonstrated that infiltrating pathogenic peripherally driven lymphocytes through the endothelium or meningeal structures is a contributing factor [23]. The interaction between B and T lymphocytes is an important factor in early disease progression and severity and can be shown as perivascular infiltration in demyelinating lesions [24].

Given the rarity of this condition, the ideal treatment for Marburg’s demyelination and other aggressive demyelinating diseases is not well established as controlled trials are lacking. Therefore, the possible generalizability of MS treatments to patients with Marburg’s disease, while commonly practiced, is uncertain [2]. Similar to other CNS inflammatory diseases, high-dose pulsed corticosteroids and plasmapheresis have been used as the initial therapies [25]. Alternative modes of chronic immunosuppression are required. However, the ideal immunosuppressive strategy for treating this disorder remains unknown. As the condition is very severe, most physicians use highly effective immunosuppressants such as mitoxantrone or alemtuzumab [26, 27]. When the blood-brain barrier is compromised, mitoxantrone passes through and accesses the central nervous system, suppressing B and T lymphocytes and macrophages [27, 28]. Cyclophosphamide is another commonly used medication for severe forms of demyelination, although the ideal dosing regimen is not agreed upon. Krishnan et al. [29] explored HiCy therapy without hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in individuals with severe forms of MS. HiCy treatment substantially decreased the disease activity in most patients. Some patients sustain an extended duration of remission with no evident disease activity even without immunomodulatory treatment [29]. Nozaki et al. [30] recommended that once the diagnosis of Marburg MS is established, the patient should complete HiCy therapy administered at a rate of 50 mg/kg/day for 4 days and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor 6 days after HiCy [30]. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation has become increasingly popular in the treatment of aggressive relapsing-remitting MS [31]. Thus, it may be useful in the treatment of Marburg MS [29].

The Marburg variant has a poor prognosis compared with other CNS demyelinating disorders. In one case series, only 11 of 28 patients (39%) survived, and only 2 of the surviving patients had favorable outcomes following an intensive management plan [2]. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE reporting guidelines as online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553416).

Conclusions

This observation suggests a possible temporal association between the vaccine and Marburg-variant demyelination; however, no causal conclusions can be drawn as such demyelinating events remain exceptionally uncommon despite large-scale immunization efforts. Early recognition of this variant and aggressive treatment might result in a better outcome.
