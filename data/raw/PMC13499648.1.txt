Abstract

Introduction

GNE myopathy is a rare autosomal recessive distal myopathy classically characterized by symmetrical distal muscle weakness with relative quadriceps sparing. However, phenotypic variability, including asymmetric onset, may obscure the diagnosis and mimic neuropathic disorders.

Case Presentation

A 27-year-old Bangladeshi man presented with a 2-year history of progressive distal weakness, initially involving the left lower limb and later affecting the contralateral limb and distal upper extremities. Neurological examination revealed asymmetric distal weakness with bilateral foot drop and preserved sensory function. Serum creatine kinase was moderately elevated (892 U/L). Nerve conduction studies showed preserved sensory responses with reduced motor amplitudes, while electromyography demonstrated a distal-predominant myopathic pattern. Magnetic resonance imaging of the spine was unremarkable. Clinical exome sequencing identified a homozygous likely pathogenic variant in the GNE gene NM_005476.7:c.484C>T (p.Arg162Cys), confirming the diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case highlights an asymmetric presentation of GNE myopathy mimicking peripheral neuropathy. Recognition of such phenotypic variability and integration of clinical, electrophysiological, and genetic findings are crucial for accurate diagnosis and to avoid misclassification as a neuropathic disorder.

Introduction

GNE myopathy is a rare autosomal recessive distal myopathy initially described as distal myopathy with rimmed vacuoles and later recognized as hereditary inclusion body myopathy with relative sparing of the quadriceps muscles [1, 2]. The genetic basis of the disease was later established with the identification of mutations in the GNE gene, which encodes the bifunctional enzyme UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 2-epimerase/N-acetylmannosamine kinase involved in sialic acid biosynthesis [3].

Clinically, it typically presents in early adulthood with progressive distal weakness, most commonly affecting the anterior compartment of the leg and resulting in foot drop, with relative sparing of the quadriceps [4]. However, phenotypic variability, such as asymmetric weakness at onset, is increasingly recognized and can lead to diagnostic challenges if initially mistaken for neuropathic conditions.

Case Presentation

A 27-year-old Bangladeshi man presented with a 2-year history of progressive distal muscle weakness. The symptoms initially involved the left lower limb, manifesting as difficulty with ankle dorsiflexion and frequent tripping. Within 2 months, similar weakness developed in the right lower limb, followed by involvement of the distal upper limbs.

He reported progressive difficulty in ambulation and performing fine motor tasks, accompanied by distal muscle wasting. There were no sensory symptoms, fasciculations, diurnal variation, antecedent trauma, or sphincter disturbances. His past medical history was unremarkable. He was born to non-consanguineous parents, with no known family history of neuromuscular disease.

Neurological examination revealed marked distal muscle wasting in both upper and lower limbs, including intrinsic hand muscles, along with bilateral foot drop (Fig. 1). Muscle strength was asymmetrically reduced (MRC grade 3/5 in the left lower limb and 4/5 in the right lower limb). Distal upper limb strength was graded 4/5, while ankle dorsiflexion was severely impaired bilaterally (MRC 2/5).

Sensory examination was normal. Deep tendon reflexes were preserved in the upper limbs and right lower limb but reduced in the left lower limb. Plantar responses were flexor bilaterally. Gait was high stepping, consistent with bilateral foot drop (online suppl. Video 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553628). Based on the asymmetric distal weakness, initial differential diagnoses included peroneal neuropathy, lumbosacral radiculopathy, motor neuron disease, and distal myopathy.

Serum creatine kinase was elevated at 892 U/L. Nerve conduction studies showed preserved sensory responses, absent left peroneal compound muscle action potentials, and markedly reduced right peroneal compound muscle action potential amplitudes, likely reflecting recording from severely atrophic distal muscles rather than a primary peripheral neuropathy. Needle EMG demonstrated distal-predominant myopathic changes with relative sparing of proximal muscles, including the quadriceps. A summary of the electromyographic findings is provided in Table 1, and representative electromyographic waveforms are shown in Figure 2. Magnetic resonance imaging of the spine was unremarkable.

Discussion

GNE myopathy is a slowly progressive muscle disease that typically presents between age 20 and 40 years with bilateral foot drop caused by anterior tibialis weakness [4]. The disease slowly progresses over the next decades to involve skeletal muscles throughout the body, with relative sparing of the quadriceps until late stages of the disease [5].

Although GNE myopathy classically presents with symmetric distal weakness, asymmetry at disease onset is frequent. Recent data from a South Asian cohort demonstrated asymmetrical limb involvement in 40.8% of patients, with a mean time of 1.8 ± 1.5 years to involve the contralateral side [6]. This aligns with prior regional cohorts where up to 39% of individuals presented with unilateral foot drop [7]. The case presented here reinforces that asymmetric onset is a recognized variation of GNE myopathy and may contribute to diagnostic confusion with neuropathic disorders.

The initial clinical presentation suggested a neurogenic etiology. However, a structured diagnostic approach allowed systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses. Preserved sensory nerve conduction responses made peripheral neuropathy unlikely, while a normal spine MRI and absence of dermatomal symptoms and the presence of diffuse myopathic findings on electromyography made radiculopathy less likely. The absence of upper motor neuron signs and the presence of a myopathic rather than neurogenic EMG pattern made motor neuron disease unlikely.

Electrophysiological findings were crucial in establishing a myopathic process. The presence of small-amplitude, short-duration motor unit potentials with early recruitment (Fig. 2), along with distal predominance (Table 1), supported a diagnosis of distal myopathy.

To firmly establish GNE myopathy in the context of an asymmetric lower-limb presentation, it is vital to differentiate it from other genetic distal myopathies that manifest with foot drop or leg weakness. Clinically, GNE myopathy is an adult-onset disorder characterized by a normal to mildly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) level [4, 8]. This differs from anoctamin 5 (ANO5)-related distal myopathy and atypical Miyoshi dysferlinopathy, caused by mutations in the DYSF gene, both of which characteristically present with massively elevated serum CK levels, often reaching thousands or tens of thousands of U/L [8, 9]. While ANO5 and DYSF mutations share a similar young adult onset with GNE myopathy, they typically display an early, preferential targeting of the posterior compartment, particularly the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles, resulting in an inability to walk on tiptoes rather than foot drop [8]. However, certain atypical presentations of DYSF mutations can manifest as a distal anterior compartment myopathy presenting with foot drop. These are distinguished from GNE myopathy by their distinct genomic profiles, rapid progression to proximal muscles, and significantly higher serum CK elevations [9].

Valosin-containing protein-related distal myopathy, caused by mutations in the VCP gene, can mimic GNE myopathy by targeting the tibialis anterior muscle during adulthood; however, it represents an autosomal dominant trait typically characterized by syndromic, nonskeletal manifestations such as Paget’s disease of bone and frontotemporal dementia [8]. Similarly, matrin 3 (MATR3)-related distal myopathy is an autosomal dominant disorder of adult onset, usually in the late 30s–50s, that can usually be distinguished from GNE myopathy by early and prominent bulbar and respiratory muscle involvement, resulting in symptoms such as dysphonia, dysphagia, and vocal cord paralysis [8]. Finally, although nebulin (NEB)-related congenital myopathy can occasionally present atypically as a distal-dominant, asymmetric lower limb myopathy causing foot drop, it is clinically differentiated by its characteristic genetic origin in the NEB gene, an onset during infancy or early childhood, and a very slowly progressive clinical course [10], which differs from the adult-onset, steadily progressive weakness seen in GNE myopathy [4].

The identified homozygous GNE variant, NM_005476.7:c.484C>T (p.Arg162Cys), was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria [11]. This classification was supported by its location within the UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 2-epimerase domain, rarity in population databases, deleterious in silico predictions, and conservation of the affected amino acid residue. This variant is not novel and has been previously reported in patients with GNE myopathy [12, 13]. The most directly comparable prior report is the Indian series by Nalini et al., in which a patient from a consanguineous family carried the same homozygous c.484C>T (p.Arg162Cys) variant and presented with adult-onset foot drop, asymmetric onset, mild CK elevation, ambulant status after 8 years of illness, and relative quadriceps preservation [12]. This prior report indicates that asymmetric onset can occur with this genotype. Earlier, Del Bo et al. reported the same R162C substitution in two affected Italian sisters with autosomal recessive hereditary inclusion-body myopathy, where it occurred in trans with a large deletion involving GNE exons 1–9 [13]. In that report, the R162C variant was maternally inherited, absent in 100 Italian controls, located in exon 3 within the epimerase domain, and affected a conserved arginine residue [13]. The variant is also listed in ClinVar as NM_005476.7(GNE):c.484C>T (p.Arg162Cys), Variation ID 290196/VCV000290196.23, with an aggregate germline classification of pathogenic/likely pathogenic [14].

In addition to the present p.Arg162Cys variant, other amino-acid substitutions affecting the same Arg162 residue have been cataloged in genomic databases, including NM_005476.7(GNE):c.484C>G (p.Arg162Gly), c.484C>A (p.Arg162Ser), c.485G>A (p.Arg162His), c.485G>T (p.Arg162Leu), and c.485G>C (p.Arg162Pro) [15]. These alternative substitutions should not be considered independent proof of pathogenicity for p.Arg162Cys, particularly because clinical case-level evidence is limited or absent for several of them. However, the occurrence of multiple missense substitutions at the same conserved arginine residue supports the functional importance of Arg162 within the GNE epimerase domain. This interpretation is biologically plausible as molecular modeling of the bifunctional GNE enzyme has shown that disease-associated missense mutations may affect active-site regions, substrate-binding interactions, or secondary-structure interfaces relevant to enzyme function [16].

As GNE myopathy is an autosomal recessive disorder, pathogenic variants may be present in the healthy population as heterozygous carriers [17]. This allows occurrence in non-consanguineous populations due to carrier frequency.

Familial segregation analysis could not be performed; confirmation of parental carrier status would have further strengthened the genetic interpretation, particularly in the setting of a homozygous variant. However, reliable local access to confirmatory genetic testing for rare neuromuscular disorders is limited in our setting and testing often requires international sample shipment with substantial logistical and financial barriers. Therefore, segregation evidence was not applied as an ACMG/AMP criterion. In addition, this is a single-patient case report; therefore, broader genotype-phenotype correlations or population-level conclusions cannot be drawn. Nevertheless, the diagnosis is supported by the patient’s compatible distal-predominant clinical phenotype, myopathic electrophysiology, prior reports of p.Arg162Cys/R162C in affected individuals, ClinVar classification, rarity in population databases, and domain- and residue-level evidence supporting the pathogenic relevance of the identified variant.

This case highlights the importance of integrating clinical, electrophysiological, and genetic data in patients presenting with asymmetric distal weakness, particularly when clinical features mimic neuropathic disorders. Although the identified GNE variant has been previously reported, this genetically confirmed Bangladeshi case further supports asymmetric distal-onset weakness as part of the recognized phenotypic spectrum of GNE myopathy. Greater awareness of this presentation, especially in underrepresented populations and resource-limited settings, may help reduce diagnostic delay, facilitate timely genetic evaluation, and strengthen the global understanding of the clinico-genetic diversity of GNE myopathy. Learning Points

Asymmetric distal weakness is a relatively common early presentation of GNE myopathy and should not preclude the diagnosis.

Preservation of sensory function favors a myopathic over a neuropathic process.

Electrophysiological studies are essential in distinguishing myopathy from neuropathy.

Genetic testing is crucial for definitive diagnosis and may avoid invasive procedures.

Absence of family history does not exclude autosomal recessive disease.

Patient Perspective

The patient reported that progressive weakness significantly affected his mobility and daily activities. Receiving a definitive genetic diagnosis improved his understanding of the condition and allowed appropriate counseling regarding prognosis.
