Objective:

Maternal vaccination rates are low in the United States. Understanding barriers to vaccination for pregnant women from the provider’s perspective can help better address these issues. This exploratory study assesses participating prenatal health care providers’ beliefs and perspectives regarding maternal vaccination for Tdap, flu, COVID-19, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccines.

Methods:

This qualitative study included 24 virtual in-depth interviews with prenatal health care providers (12 physicians, 12 certified nurse midwives) working in rural and urban areas. Thematic analysis was conducted to identify perceived barriers and challenges to prenatal care, provider perceptions and recommendations on Tdap, flu, COVID-19, and RSV vaccines, vaccination resources shared with pregnant women, and vaccine administration practices.

Results:

Most providers reported pregnant women were receptive to vaccination when benefits and risks of prenatal vaccination were explained. Rural providers identified barriers to care that included limited transportation, lack of access to health care, and language barriers. Findings also indicate prenatal health care providers can foster trust, engage in two-way conversations, and navigate patients’ cultural influences when addressing vaccination challenges.

Conclusion:

Provider communication about maternal vaccination should be grounded in an evidence-based, participatory approach. Building trust, recognizing and addressing cultural barriers and access challenges are key to identifying practical steps to support access to and uptake of maternal vaccinations.

Introduction

In the United States, over 3 million women give birth every year.1,2 Tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis (Tdap), influenza (flu), COVID-19, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccination is safe during pregnancy and can decrease the risk of severe illness for pregnant women and young infants. 3 When this study was conducted, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Adult Immunization Schedule included a COVID-19 vaccine recommendation for all pregnant women. The COVID-19 vaccine recommendation for pregnant women has since been updated. 4 The CDC Adult Immunization Schedule and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommend administering Tdap, 5 flu, 5 and RSV 6 vaccines in pregnancy. However, maternal vaccination coverage remains low in the United States: During the 2023–2024 influenza season, 47.4% of women 18–49 years old received the flu vaccine, and 59.6% received the Tdap vaccine during pregnancy, while 30.9% received an updated COVID-19 vaccine before or during pregnancy.5,6 Maternal RSV vaccination was recommended starting August 2023 for pregnant women during 32–36 completed gestation weeks; only 23.3% of pregnant women ages 18–49 years received prenatal RSV vaccine as of September 2024.7,8 Additionally, nirsevimab is a monoclonal antibody, not a vaccine, that is recommended for eligible infants younger than 8 months of age to prevent severe RSV disease. 9

When assessing maternal vaccination differences between rural and urban areas, one study found prenatal influenza vaccination is lower among women living in rural vs. urban areas, even after adjustment for access to care and sociodemographic differences. 10 Furthermore, pregnant women living in rural areas are less frequently recommended influenza vaccination by a health care provider. Potential contributors to this gap in care include access challenges, like health care workforce shortages, hospital closures, distance to care, and cost issues. 11 Differing cultural contexts also influence trust in public health and health care systems, which impacts vaccination behaviors. 12 To support uptake of maternal vaccinations, it is essential to identify prenatal health care provider perspectives on recommended vaccines. 13 In addition, identifying specific challenges faced by pregnant women in rural U.S. settings can inform efforts to increase vaccination coverage in this population of focus.

Methods

This qualitative study included 24 virtual in-depth interviews conducted from November 14 to December 15, 2023, with prenatal health care providers: 12 medical doctors (MDs) and doctors of osteopathic medicine (DOs) and 12 certified nurse midwives (CNMs). Twelve providers self-reported working in rural locations and 12 providers self-reported working in urban locations (Table 1). To be eligible, providers had to report directly caring for pregnant women ≥20 hours per week. Providers working for private corporations or as temporary/locum tenens providers were excluded. Participants self-reported ≥25% of their patient population identified as Black/African American or Hispanic/Latinx. Providers included a mix across age, sex, race, ethnicity, practice setting, and years in practice (Table 1). Interviews were conducted using a web-based platform; participants needed an internet connection, camera, and internet browser to participate. Interviews were conducted in English by a professionally trained interviewer and lasted up to 60 minutes.

The key research questions addressed in this study were: 1. What are the demographics and cultural contexts of pregnant women seen in the practice?

2. What are the perceived patient barriers and challenges regarding vaccinations reported by providers in urban and rural practices?

3. What are prenatal care providers’ perspectives and recommendations regarding timing of vaccine administration and vaccine coadministration during pregnancy?

4. What are prenatal care providers’ perspectives and recommendations for specific vaccines during pregnancy: Tdap, influenza, COVID-19, and RSV? (note: at the time of this study, COVID-19 vaccination was recommended during pregnancy).

Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were de-identified. The codebook was developed from the interview guide and refined after coding a subset of transcripts. Major themes identified from the interview guide included: (1) patient demographics and cultural considerations, (2) provider-reported barriers and challenges to patients’ seeking prenatal care, (3) elements of vaccination conversations, including provider recommendation strength and related reasoning and perspectives on vaccine coadministration, and (4) vaccine-specific perspectives. Two coders trained on the coding scheme independently coded the same three transcripts to ensure consistent code application. Substantial agreement was reached through two rounds of intercoder reliability assessment (kappa = 0.78). 14 One coder coded the remaining 21 transcripts, which were spot-checked by a second coder.

Results

Within the key topics presented below, there were several notable differences by rurality, which are described below. There were no substantial differences between physicians and nurse-midwives, so findings for both are combined.

Patient demographics and cultural considerations

Participants indicated their patients were mostly from lower- and middle-income backgrounds and insured by Medicaid. Several participants reported having to carefully navigate pregnant women’s cultural influences when addressing health care needs. Cultural considerations often involved navigating language barriers (mostly Hispanic/Latina and Middle Eastern patients) and providing racially concordant care whenever possible. One CNM reported their practice hires CNMs of color so patients can have a provider that looks like them to improve health care outcomes. Language barriers were addressed through interpreters who speak multiple languages or proactively hiring multilingual health care providers. However, many rural providers said hiring multilingual interpreters was challenging in rural areas. A few providers reported using web-based translators and technology such as tablets to provide translation services.

Reported logistical barriers

Participants in both rural and urban settings identified transportation barriers, including patients experiencing challenges getting to appointments and being unable to afford large out-of-pocket transportation expenses. Some providers stated Medicaid did not always cover patients’ transportation needs, or patients were not Medicaid-eligible when they needed transportation support. Additionally, lack of affordable childcare made it difficult for some pregnant women to attend doctor’s appointments. A few rural providers reported challenges such as managing high-risk pregnancies and patients seeking care late in pregnancy due to health care costs, transportation issues, or fear of being reported to immigration authorities. Some rural providers noted that limited options for prenatal health care in their areas created difficulty for patients to access care.

Vaccine communication and perceived hesitancy

Vaccination conversations were reportedly most often provider-initiated. Medical assistants and/or nurses were often identified as the first to ask patients about vaccination preferences and to recommend vaccination, with the physician or CNM then giving detailed vaccination information and addressing questions or concerns. The most commonly reported patient questions about maternal vaccination were on safety, benefits, and potential short- and long-term side effects of vaccination for the baby and themselves. Most participants believed patients were primarily concerned about safety for the baby, and that reiterating the protection maternal vaccination provides to young infants can be very helpful in convincing patients to get vaccinated: “I think when I bring up that your baby is going to get this vaccine themselves once they’re a little bit older, and I explain that (the vaccine) will give (the baby) that protection[…]so it’s safe to give to (the pregnant woman) now, basically, to help protect (the baby?)…”

– CNM IDI # 10 (Urban)

A couple providers said their patients’ trust, and them being supportive and understanding during the conversation were important factors in their patients’ vaccine decision making. “[Being] inquisitive sort of, listening, saying ‘what concerns you about that’ or ‘what makes you nervous’ or ‘can you tell me why you wouldn’t want to get that vaccine’. And then hopefully using some education[…]But coming in from a non-judgmental place is generally good, being like you have to do this or this is—that just doesn’t work.”

– CNM IDI #6 (Urban)

Provider perceptions of the effect of COVID-19 on vaccination acceptance among pregnant women were mixed. Some participants felt the pandemic made patients more hesitant towards vaccines generally, while some thought vaccination rates in their practice had not changed significantly. Providers reported patients are generally receptive to prenatal vaccination and rarely refuse all vaccines. Participants felt patients with less general knowledge about pregnancy were often more hesitant towards prenatal vaccinations. “If they’re open minded to vaccinate and I explain that to them, most of them will do it. I would say 95% of them will do it.”

– CNM IDI #11 (Rural)

When specifically asked about why “certain groups are less receptive to [prenatal] vaccines”, the most commonly reported reasons were vaccine hesitancy and skepticism due to conspiracy theories, distrust in vaccines and health systems, and general low receptivity to vaccines. “So I ask them what is the reason and it depends what they have read online […]Some of the weird stuff I’ve heard […]there is not even a virus. It’s the effect of 5G internet is causing the disease. Oh, this is imported, or this is manufactured[…]I try to allay their fears but obviously without going into politics. I stay just as a medical professional, and I answer their questions from that stance.”

– MD IDI # 3 (Urban)

“We used to take it as just a normal part of everyday life, but[…] it just seems like the trust in the vaccines themselves and the people—the scientists—there’s just not the trust there that there used to be. I have to blame that on the information network that we have now because people are privy to so much information and some of it is good and some of it is bad.”

– CNM IDI #15 (Rural)

Many providers found patients in their first pregnancy were more receptive to vaccinating, while those who had prior pregnancies were likely to repeat their vaccination behavior in previous pregnancy.

Timing of vaccine conversations

Some providers said they discuss recommended vaccination(s) at the appointment prior to the potential vaccination visit, which gives the patient time to get more information and address their concerns as needed. Others said they wait to discuss vaccination until the visit at which the patient should be vaccinated. Most participants said being open, honest, and non-judgmental are important vaccination conversation techniques. “I think buy-in is important. I think we don’t want to be trying to get them something without them being able to understand why we’re doing it[…]Everybody needs a different amount of time to be able to comprehend? information and process it. Setting the stage ahead of time, I think will be very helpful for that next visit to say, “Hey, where are we at on this?” And then, “Are you okay receiving that vaccination?”

– MD IDI #17 (Rural)

“I usually try and kind of explain some of the studies that have been out there, and explain, again like we talked about, some of the benefits of receiving it. And I try to give them an out too, of saying, “If you don’t feel comfortable with this today, we can chat more about it [next] visit.” Again, a lot of times we’re seeing that patient pretty frequently, so that gives them a chance to kind of go home and think about it, maybe talk to other friends or family members so they don’t feel that pressure to make a decision right there today…”

– CNM IDI # 6 (Urban)

Most participating providers indicated they were comfortable with recommending co-administration of vaccines during pregnancy, but noted patients usually prefer to spread out vaccines due to concern about potential short-term side effects. Providers said they were generally comfortable with this choice because they would see the patient soon for the next prenatal appointment. Some providers mentioned patients have concerns about side effects of co-administration but noted that when providers explained that co-administration is safe, patients were mostly comfortable getting multiple vaccines in one visit. A few participants said patients preferred the convenience of co-administration to get the vaccinations completed as soon as feasible and appropriate. Three providers said they advise patients to spread out vaccinations to avoid exacerbating potential side effects from multiple vaccines.

Vaccine-specific perspectives

Tdap vaccine

Most participants reported strongly recommending Tdap vaccination beginning at the end of the second trimester. Providers also explain Tdap vaccine is needed for each pregnancy to maximize protection of the baby. “When I see them at their 26-week visit, I will mention to them at your next visit you will be offered the Tdap vaccine and I’ll explain to them this is the whooping cough booster and the CDC recommends it around 28 weeks in pregnancy for all pregnant women, regardless of how many pregnancies you’ve had, and regardless of when you were vaccinated last”

– CNM ID#7 (Rural)

Additionally, providers report emphasizing to patients that Tdap has been around for years and has been proven to be safe for pregnant women and their babies. Participating providers report most patients accept prenatal Tdap vaccination, particularly after an explanation of the benefits of vaccination for the baby.

Flu vaccine

All participants report recommending flu vaccine, with stronger emphasis during influenza season. Some providers reported patients who had experienced feeling ill after flu vaccinations prior to pregnancy often did not want to risk vaccination side effects again. Providers indicated they sometimes struggle to convince patients of the benefits of prenatal flu vaccination, as patients generally believe they will recover quickly if they contract influenza during pregnancy based on pre-pregnancy experience(s) with influenza. Some providers reported many of their patients stick to what they usually do when it comes to the flu vaccine, regardless of pregnancy.

COVID-19 vaccine

All participating providers reported discussing the importance of COVID-19 vaccination with pregnant women, and most discussed the protection it provides to mother and baby. A few providers said their strength of recommendation has waned since the height of the pandemic, when morbidity and mortality due to COVID-19 were higher. Many providers reported experiences with patients who had politicized opinions of COVID-19 vaccination and felt their recommendations have little impact on patients who have already made up their minds on COVID-19 vaccination. A few providers reported not making strong recommendations to patients they know are strongly opposed to COVID-19 vaccination. “It’s not something that I’m discussing at their antenatal visits at this point. It was a touchy subject during pregnancy… If I can get them to get flu and TdaP at this point, I’m pretty happy[…]I don’t want to lose their trust or overwhelm them. I can still see that—when I was bringing up COVID vaccine that I might have been losing people. I’m going to go for good and not perfect.”

– MD IDI # 16 (Rural)

Three providers reported being unsure whether COVID-19 vaccines are as effective as other vaccines recommended during pregnancy, which made them uncertain if it is worth making a strong recommendation to hesitant patients.

RSV vaccine

Most participants were concerned about RSV and said they already did or would likely recommend RSV vaccination to pregnant women. Some providers were still in the process of adopting recommendations and obtaining vaccine supply for their practices at the time of the interview. Participants generally felt RSV vaccination was very important and reported prioritizing it as they do Tdap vaccine. “Most of our patients don’t actually know the term RSV but I’ll talk about what happened last fall about when the COVID numbers were down we started to see an increase in this respiratory virus in kids that required huge numbers of hospitalizations.[…] It’s like, try to make sure they understand it’s like a serious respiratory virus, not just a cold.”

– CNM IDI # 14 (Rural)

Participants reported multiple barriers to RSV vaccination, including limited provider understanding of recommendations, limited vaccine supply, high vaccine stocking costs, and lack of insurance coverage for RSV vaccination. Providers reported needing more information on RSV vaccine and ways to overcome perceived barriers. A few providers felt the newness of the vaccine and recommendations may make their pregnant women hesitant to get vaccinated. Some providers who were not yet recommending RSV vaccination reported being aware of CDC recommendations, but their organization was awaiting recommendations from ACOG (which were released in October 2023 just prior to these interviews). Most participants were not very familiar with nirsevimab and said it would fall in the domain of pediatricians.

Discussion

Our findings emphasize the significance of cultural norms and the role they play in shaping vaccination behaviors. Providers consistently reported how cultural norms/values and roadblocks to health care access impact prenatal vaccination decisions. Our findings also suggest general acceptability of prenatal vaccination when trusted providers address patient questions and concerns. Finally, our findings highlight challenges of providing and accessing prenatal vaccination in rural settings.

Considering contextual variations and understanding how information is shared and received in rural and culturally diverse populations can inform communication to support vaccine confidence and trust. 15 In addition, rural providers identified challenges for patients accessing prenatal care such as transportation issues, availability of care for high-risk pregnancies, delayed care-seeking due to cost concerns, and high out-of-pocket costs. Rural areas also face provider shortages, making it difficult to find and schedule prenatal care. Addressing these barriers is essential for ensuring equitable access to prenatal care, including vaccination, particularly in rural communities. 16

Vaccine co-administration can be a safe, practical, and effective approach to protect patients who have difficulty regularly accessing prenatal care. Most respondents reported if they explained the safety of co-administration, patients would agree to receive the vaccines. Vaccine co-administration does not compromise vaccine efficacy, making it an efficient option to promote vaccination. 17 When acceptable to patients, co-administration can potentially improve vaccination rates by minimizing missed opportunities and ensuring timely vaccination.18,19 Additionally, highlighting the benefits of maternal vaccination for infant health can be an effective strategy to increase vaccine uptake among pregnant women.20,21

Interviews were conducted in November-December 2023, so the novelty of RSV vaccine recommendations may have impacted RSV-specific findings. One survey found 63% of obstetricians/gynecologists offered prenatal RSV vaccination, but cited challenges like safety concerns, cost, and reimbursement barriers. 22

Future surveys could corroborate our findings and explore changing trends in maternal RSV vaccination. A qualitative systematic review of 28 studies across high-income countries found that trust in providers, accessible vaccination, clear communication, and messaging around protection for the baby motivated maternal vaccine uptake, while fears of fetal harm and low risk perception were key barriers. 23 Another systematic review synthesized findings from over 300 studies and identified provider recommendation as one of the strongest drivers of maternal vaccination uptake. Both studies emphasized improving provider communication and training.

Some limitations of this study are that, as a qualitative study, the findings provide insights and direction for future work but are not intended to be generalizable. The subjectivity of qualitative analysis creates potential for researcher bias; this was minimized by having two interview coders with good agreement, although not all interviews were ‘double coded.’ The research setting may have influenced participants’ responses, potentially introducing social desirability bias. Additionally, findings related to patient beliefs were reported by providers, so there may be variations in perspectives.

Despite the limitations, this study highlights the complex interplay of socio-cultural and systemic factors influencing prenatal vaccination uptake. A holistic approach could mitigate challenges faced by providers serving diverse populations who are at the interface of individuals, communities, and health care systems. Comprehensive strategies to make prenatal vaccines widely accessible may need to address both logistical and cultural issues. Our findings reinforce the critical role of prenatal care providers in recommending vaccines and fostering trust-building discussions with pregnant women. Prioritizing these factors can empower pregnant women to make informed decisions to safeguard maternal and fetal health.
