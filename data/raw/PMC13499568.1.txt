Dear editor

We sincerely thank Dong for the careful reading of our study and for the thoughtful comments regarding the pharmacological and methodological interpretation of our findings.1,2 We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify the rationale and scope of our experimental design.

Regarding the Concentration of Semaglutide Used in vitro

The 10 μM concentration of semaglutide used in our cellular experiments was selected on the basis of preliminary experimental screening. Under the high-glucose and palmitic-acid conditions established in our study, this concentration produced a stable and reproducible protective effect on osteoblasts.

We acknowledge that the concentration used in vitro is higher than the reported receptor-binding affinity and the circulating concentrations achieved under clinical conditions. However, the present study was designed as an in-vitro mechanistic investigation rather than as a direct simulation of clinical pharmacokinetics or systemic drug exposure. Drug concentrations required to produce measurable biological effects in an isolated cell-culture system may not directly correspond to plasma concentrations observed in patients.

Therefore, our findings indicate that semaglutide at 10 μM exerted protective effects against osteoblast ferroptosis under the specific experimental conditions used in this study. We did not intend to suggest that this concentration is clinically equivalent or that it represents the clinically effective concentration of semaglutide for diabetic periodontitis. Based on an in-vitro study alone, it is difficult to determine whether the observed effects are fully consistent with the clinical pharmacodynamics of semaglutide.

We agree that future studies using a broader range of concentrations may provide additional information regarding dose-response relationships and clinical relevance. Nevertheless, we believe that the concentration selected through preliminary screening was appropriate for addressing the mechanistic objectives of the present study.

Regarding the Dose Used in the Animal Experiments

The semaglutide dose used in the animal experiments was 0.5 mg/kg. This dose was selected with reference to previously published preclinical studiesevaluating the pharmacological effects of semaglutide, together with our preliminary experimental screening in the disease model.

We appreciate the author’s consideration of the relationship between the animal dose and the approved clinical dose. However, the primary purpose of our animal experiments was to assess the biological effects of semaglutide in a specific preclinical model rather than to establish a direct dose equivalence with human treatment.

Differences in species, metabolic rate, pharmacokinetics, treatment duration, administration protocols, and disease-model characteristics may all influence drug exposure and biological response. Human-equivalent dose conversion is a useful reference, but it may not fully reflect these differences.

We therefore agree that the clinical translation of the experimental dose should be interpreted cautiously. At the same time, we believe that the selected dose was reasonable for evaluating the pharmacological effect of semaglutide within the context of the present animal model.

Regarding cAMP-Related Signaling

We appreciate the author’s important comments concerning cAMP/PKA and Epac signaling. These pathways are well-recognized components of canonical GLP-1 receptor signaling and may contribute to the biological effects of semaglutide.

Our study focused specifically on the involvement of the Wnt5a/Ror2/p38 MAPK pathway in semaglutide-mediated protection against osteoblast ferroptosis. The conclusions were based on the molecular and functional evidence obtained within this predefined experimental framework.

We did not intend to suggest that the Wnt5a/Ror2/p38 MAPK pathway is the only signaling mechanism involved in the effects of semaglutide. It is possible that cAMP-related pathways act upstream of, in parallel with, or in interaction with the Wnt5a/Ror2/p38 MAPK pathway.

Further investigation of cAMP, PKA, Epac, and their potential interactions with p38 MAPK would undoubtedly help provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms. However, these additional signaling pathways were beyond the principal scope of the present study. We therefore regard this point as a valuable direction for future research rather than as a limitation that alters the current findings.

Regarding the Solvent-Related Concern

We also thank the author for drawing attention to the potential effects of DMSO on membrane properties, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation. We agree that solvent-related effects should be carefully considered in ferroptosis research.

In our study, the experimental comparisons were performed under established vehicle-control conditions. The interpretation of the results was therefore based on differences observed between groups under comparable experimental conditions.

We acknowledge that DMSO may exert biological effects under certain circumstances. Nevertheless, without evidence of unequal solvent exposure or a measurable DMSO-dependent effect under the specific conditions used in our experiments, it remains difficult to determine whether DMSO materially influenced the observed anti-ferroptotic effects.

We agree that future studies may further strengthen the methodological evaluation by examining alternative solvents or additional vehicle controls where appropriate.

Conclusion

We are grateful to Dong for raising these thoughtful pharmacological and methodological considerations. The comments provide useful perspectives regarding dose-response relationships, clinical translation, upstream signaling, and solvent effects.

We do not exclude the possibility that lower concentrations, alternative dosing strategies, cAMP-related pathways, or additional methodological controls may yield further insights in future studies. At the same time, the concentration and animal dose used in the present study were selected on the basis of preliminary experimental screening, previously published preclinical evidence, and their observed efficacy in the experimental models.

Our study was intended to explore the protective effects and potential mechanism of semaglutide under defined in-vitro and in-vivo experimental conditions. It was not designed to establish direct equivalence with clinical drug exposure or to provide a complete characterization of all possible signaling pathways.

Accordingly, while we fully appreciate the value of the issues raised and agree that they deserve further investigation, we believe that they do not substantially affect the interpretation of the findings within the scope of the present study.

For this reason, we respectfully consider that revision of the original manuscript is not necessary.
