Background

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a frequent yet underrecognized complication in critically ill patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Existing risk assessment tools are not specifically tailored to this high-risk population. We aimed to develop and externally validate an interpretable machine-learning model for early prediction of DVT in ICU-admitted COPD patients.

Methods

Adult COPD patients admitted to the ICU were identified from the MIMIC-IV database and randomly divided into training and internal validation cohorts. Eight machine-learning algorithms were constructed and compared. The best-performing model was externally validated in MIMIC-III and eICU cohorts. Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed using AUC, calibration plots, decision-curve analysis (DCA), and Brier scores. SHAP analysis was applied for global and individual interpretability. A web-based calculator was developed for clinical application.

Results

Among 6,672 ICU patients with COPD, 462 (6.9%) developed DVT. XGBoost showed the best overall performance, with an AUC of 0.840 (95% CI 0.812–0.868) in the internal validation cohort and good calibration. External validation confirmed stable discrimination in both MIMIC-III and eICU cohorts. Model interpretation identified prolonged PTT, elevated RDW, reduced SpO2, and increased respiratory rate as important contributors to DVT risk. Decision-curve analysis suggested potential clinical benefit across relevant risk thresholds.

Conclusion

We developed and externally validated an explainable machine-learning model for early prediction of DVT in ICU patients with COPD. By providing individualized risk estimates and interpretable explanations, this tool may help clinicians identify high-risk patients earlier and support more targeted thromboprophylaxis and imaging surveillance strategies.

Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) remains a major and often underrecognized complication in critically ill patients.1,2 In the intensive care unit (ICU), multiple prothrombotic factors—including immobilization, systemic inflammation, invasive procedures, and hemodynamic instability—converge to markedly increase venous thromboembolism risk.3–5 DVT not only predisposes patients to pulmonary embolism but is also associated with prolonged length of stay, higher healthcare costs, and increased short- and long-term mortality.4,6 Although pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis is widely implemented, the incidence of DVT in critically ill populations remains substantial, underscoring the need for more precise risk stratification strategies.

Among ICU populations, patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) represent a uniquely vulnerable subgroup.7,8 Beyond advanced age and frequent comorbidities, COPD is characterized by persistent systemic inflammation, chronic hypoxemia, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and recurrent infectious exacerbations.9–11 These pathophysiological disturbances contribute to hypercoagulability and impaired fibrinolysis, creating a biological milieu conducive to thrombosis.12,13 Furthermore, acute respiratory failure and mechanical ventilation—common in ICU-admitted COPD patients—may exacerbate venous stasis and inflammatory activation.4,14 Despite these mechanisms, current DVT risk assessment tools were largely derived from heterogeneous hospitalized populations and do not specifically account for the complex interplay of respiratory dysfunction, inflammation, and critical illness that defines ICU COPD patients.15,16

Traditional statistical approaches, such as logistic regression–based models, assume linear relationships and limited interactions among predictors.17 However, thrombotic risk in critically ill patients is likely driven by nonlinear effects and high-order interactions between physiologic, laboratory, and treatment-related variables. Machine-learning techniques offer the capacity to model such complexity and have demonstrated superior predictive performance across various critical care outcomes.18 Nevertheless, concerns regarding model transparency and interpretability have hindered their translation into bedside decision-making.19 Clinicians are unlikely to adopt predictive tools that function as “black boxes” without clear mechanistic insight.

Recent studies have emphasized the vulnerability of patients with COPD during acute infectious and respiratory insults, including COVID-19, and have underscored the need for more precise risk assessment in this population.20,21 Meanwhile, machine-learning models using structured electronic health records have shown promise for predicting venous thromboembolism in broad clinical populations. However, existing models are generally not tailored to ICU-admitted COPD patients, in whom hypoxemia, systemic inflammation, immobilization, invasive support, and critical illness may jointly shape thrombotic risk. These gaps support the development of a population-specific, externally validated, and interpretable prediction model for DVT in critically ill patients with COPD. Importantly, few studies have specifically developed and externally validated explainable machine-learning models for predicting DVT in ICU-admitted patients with COPD. Existing prediction models have either focused on general hospitalized populations, broader ICU cohorts, or venous thromboembolism risk without COPD-specific stratification.15,16 This underscores an important gap in precision risk assessment for this high-risk subgroup.

Therefore, we sought to construct and externally validate an explainable machine-learning model for early DVT risk stratification in critically ill COPD patients. Model performance was comprehensively assessed, and interpretability was ensured using SHAP analysis. An online calculator was developed to support individualized clinical decision-making.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources

This retrospective multicenter study was conducted using three publicly available critical care databases: the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV), MIMIC-III, and the eICU Collaborative Research Database. MIMIC-IV served as the development cohort, whereas MIMIC-III and eICU were used for independent external validation.

MIMIC-IV contains detailed ICU data from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022, including demographic characteristics, diagnoses, laboratory results, vital signs, medications, and clinical outcomes.22 MIMIC-III comprises ICU admissions from the same institution between 2001 and 2012.23 The eICU database includes multicenter ICU data collected across hospitals in the United States between 2014 and 2015.24 All databases are fully de-identified and publicly accessible. Ethical approval was obtained by the original database investigators.

Study Population and Outcome Definition

Adult patients aged 18 years or older with a documented diagnosis of COPD, identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, were eligible for inclusion. To avoid within-patient correlation, only the first ICU admission during the study period was analyzed.

Patients were excluded if their ICU length of stay was less than 24 hours, if DVT had been documented prior to ICU admission, or if essential baseline variables required for model construction were unavailable. The primary outcome was incident DVT occurring during ICU hospitalization. DVT events were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes recorded after ICU admission to ensure temporal alignment between predictors and outcome. ICD-based definitions have been widely used in administrative and electronic health record studies of venous thromboembolism, and previous validation studies have supported their utility for identifying VTE/DVT events, particularly when inpatient diagnosis codes are used.25,26 However, because systematic imaging records and detailed diagnostic timing were not uniformly available across databases, possible outcome misclassification could not be fully excluded. Statistical Analysis

For baseline comparisons, continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics and presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when necessary. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and JD_DCPM (V6.11, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd). Statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Candidate Variables and Data Preprocessing

Baseline variables were extracted within the first 24 hours of ICU admission to reflect early clinical status. Candidate predictors were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature and included demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory measurements, comorbidities, and treatment-related variables. The full list of candidate variables and their missing data proportions is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Missingness was quantified for each variable. Predictors with more than 25% missing data were excluded. This prespecified threshold was chosen as a conservative pragmatic balance between retaining clinically relevant predictors and reducing imputation uncertainty for variables with substantial missingness. The threshold falls within the 10–30% missingness range under which missForest has been previously evaluated.27 Variables below this threshold were imputed using missForest, a nonparametric random forest–based approach suitable for mixed-type data. All preprocessing steps were performed exclusively within the training set to prevent information leakage.

To reduce redundancy and multicollinearity, highly correlated predictors were filtered before feature selection. For continuous variables, pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated, and when the absolute correlation exceeded 0.60, the variable less strongly associated with the outcome was removed. For categorical predictors, association strength was quantified using Cramér’s V, and when values exceeded 0.50, the less outcome-relevant variable was excluded.

Feature Selection and Model Development

The MIMIC-IV cohort was randomly divided into a training set and an internal validation set at a ratio of 7:3. To minimize information leakage, the internal validation set and the two external validation cohorts were not used for missing-data imputation, correlation filtering, feature selection, resampling, or hyperparameter tuning. All preprocessing and feature selection steps were fitted only within the MIMIC-IV training set. Class rebalancing using SMOTE combined with random under-sampling was applied only to the training data. During cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, resampling was restricted to the training portion of each fold, whereas the held-out fold was left unchanged. Feature selection was conducted within the training set using a two-step strategy combining least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and the Boruta algorithm.28,29 Only predictors retained by both methods were included in the final modeling process to enhance robustness and reduce selection instability. Because of class imbalance in the outcome, the training data were rebalanced using a hybrid approach integrating random under-sampling of the majority class and the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE). Eight machine-learning algorithms were subsequently developed, including logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, decision tree, random forest, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine, support vector machine, and neural network.

Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search and K-fold cross-validation within the training set, with AUC as the primary optimization metric. Key tuned parameters included tree depth, learning rate, number of trees or boosting rounds, regularization parameters, kernel parameters, and hidden-layer structure, as appropriate for each algorithm. The final tuning strategies and selected hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Model Evaluation and External Validation

Model performance was assessed in the internal validation set in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and the area under the precision–recall curve, together with sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and balanced accuracy. Calibration was examined through calibration plots and quantified using the Brier score. Clinical utility across a range of threshold probabilities was assessed using decision curve analysis and clinical impact curves. The best-performing model identified in the internal validation phase was subsequently applied to the independent MIMIC-III and eICU cohorts without retraining. Discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility metrics were recalculated to evaluate model transportability and generalizability across datasets.

Model Interpretability

To enhance transparency and facilitate clinical adoption, SHAP analysis was applied to the final model to quantify both global feature importance and individual-level contributions. Global interpretability was illustrated using SHAP summary plots and feature-importance rankings, whereas local interpretability was demonstrated using dependence plots and individualized explanations, including waterfall and force plots. In addition, a web-based interactive risk calculator was developed using the Shiny framework to support individualized DVT risk estimation in clinical practice.

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we developed and externally validated an explainable machine-learning model for predicting DVT in critically ill patients with COPD. Among eight candidate algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated superior discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility compared with conventional statistical approaches. Importantly, model performance remained stable in two independent external cohorts, supporting its generalizability across institutions and time periods. By integrating SHAP analysis and implementing a web-based risk calculator, we further enhanced model transparency and clinical applicability. Together, these findings provide a robust and interpretable framework for early DVT risk stratification in ICU-admitted COPD patients.

COPD patients admitted to the ICU represent a uniquely vulnerable population with respect to thrombotic complications. Beyond traditional risk factors such as immobility and advanced age, COPD is characterized by chronic systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and recurrent infectious exacerbations.9–11 These pathophysiological disturbances contribute to hypercoagulability and impaired fibrinolysis, thereby creating a prothrombotic milieu. In the critical care setting, additional factors including mechanical ventilation, central venous catheterization, hemodynamic instability, and sepsis may further amplify thrombotic risk.14 Our findings, demonstrating both a substantial incidence of DVT and its association with increased ICU and hospital mortality, underscore the clinical importance of early identification and targeted prevention in this subgroup. Existing DVT risk assessment tools were largely derived from heterogeneous hospitalized populations and may not adequately capture the complex interaction between respiratory failure, systemic inflammation, and critical illness that defines ICU-admitted COPD patients. This highlights the need for population-specific predictive models. Established VTE risk assessment tools, including the Padua Prediction Score and Caprini score, are useful in general hospitalized or surgical populations. However, they were not specifically developed for ICU-admitted patients with COPD and may not fully capture dynamic respiratory, inflammatory, and critical illness–related risk factors. A formal retrospective comparison was not performed because several score components, including mobility status, prior VTE history, recent surgery or trauma, and mechanical prophylaxis, were not uniformly available across the databases. Future prospective studies should directly compare this model with established VTE risk scores and assess its incremental value for thromboprophylaxis decision-making.

Traditional logistic regression models assume linear associations and limited interaction effects among predictors.17 However, thrombotic risk in critically ill patients is likely driven by nonlinear relationships and high-order interactions between physiologic, laboratory, and treatment-related variables. In our study, tree-based ensemble methods, particularly XGBoost, outperformed logistic regression and several other algorithms, suggesting that modeling nonlinearities and feature interactions yields meaningful gains in predictive accuracy. Notably, evaluation using the area under the precision–recall curve and balanced accuracy further supported the advantage of the selected model under conditions of outcome imbalance. Moreover, favorable Brier scores and calibration performance indicate that the model not only discriminates high-risk individuals but also provides reliable probability estimates, which are essential for clinical decision-making. Decision curve analysis demonstrated net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, reinforcing the potential value of the model in real-world settings.

Beyond predictive performance, interpretability remains a central concern for clinical adoption of machine-learning models. To address this issue, we applied SHAP analysis to quantify both global and individual-level feature contributions. Several key predictors emerged as influential, including prolonged partial thromboplastin time (PTT), elevated red cell distribution width (RDW), reduced peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and increased respiratory rate. These findings are biologically plausible and align with established mechanisms of thrombogenesis. Prolonged PTT may reflect coagulation pathway disturbances and underlying systemic activation of hemostatic processes.30,31 Elevated RDW has been associated with systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which contribute to endothelial dysfunction and thrombosis.32,33 Hypoxemia, reflected by reduced SpO2, may promote thrombosis through hypoxia-inducible pathways that enhance coagulation factor expression and platelet activation.34,35 Increased respiratory rate likely captures the severity of respiratory compromise and systemic stress, indirectly reflecting the overall inflammatory and prothrombotic state.36 Collectively, these predictors map onto components of Virchow’s triad—hypercoagulability, endothelial injury, and venous stasis—providing mechanistic coherence to the model outputs.37

From a clinical perspective, early and individualized DVT risk stratification has important implications. In ICU-admitted COPD patients, risk profiles may evolve rapidly during the first 24 hours. A transparent model capable of generating individualized risk probabilities could inform decisions regarding intensified thromboprophylaxis, early imaging surveillance, or closer hemodynamic monitoring. For example, patients identified as high risk might benefit from earlier duplex ultrasonography or more aggressive preventive strategies, whereas low-risk patients could potentially avoid unnecessary interventions. The integration of SHAP-based explanations further enhances clinician trust by clarifying how specific variables contribute to a given patient’s predicted risk. By translating complex model outputs into interpretable components, this approach may facilitate bedside adoption and shared decision-making. However, several implementation issues should be considered. Model performance may be affected by differences in data availability, measurement frequency, and coding practices across hospitals. Therefore, model predictions should complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment and local thromboprophylaxis protocols. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to determine whether model-guided risk stratification can improve prophylaxis allocation and reduce DVT events without increasing bleeding risk.

Our study also contributes to the growing body of literature on machine-learning–based risk prediction in critical care.18 While prior investigations have applied machine-learning techniques to venous thromboembolism prediction in general hospitalized populations,38,39 data specifically addressing critically ill COPD patients remain limited. Moreover, many existing models lack external validation or do not incorporate formal interpretability analyses. By leveraging large, independent datasets for external validation and embedding explainability within the modeling framework, we aimed to bridge this gap and enhance methodological rigor. The consistent performance across MIMIC-IV, MIMIC-III, and eICU cohorts strengthens confidence in the robustness and transportability of the proposed model.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was retrospective in nature and relied on routinely collected electronic health record data, which may be subject to residual confounding, coding bias, and misclassification. Second, DVT events were identified using ICD diagnosis codes rather than systematic imaging screening. Although ICD-based definitions have been used and validated in prior VTE studies, this approach may still lead to outcome misclassification, particularly for asymptomatic, undiagnosed, incidentally detected, or miscoded DVT events. Differences in diagnostic surveillance and coding practices across databases may also have affected the observed DVT incidence and model performance. Third, detailed thromboprophylaxis information, including timing, dose, duration, drug type, and mechanical prophylaxis, was not uniformly available across databases. These factors may represent important unmeasured confounders because they can affect both DVT occurrence and the interpretation of predictor–outcome associations. Therefore, the model should be viewed as an early risk stratification tool rather than a causal model of DVT development. Finally, although the model was externally validated in MIMIC-III and eICU, all cohorts were derived from US-based institutions, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems. Further prospective validation in non-US and more diverse clinical settings is warranted.

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated an explainable machine-learning model for predicting DVT in critically ill patients with COPD. The model demonstrated robust discrimination, reliable calibration, and meaningful clinical utility across multiple cohorts. By integrating transparent SHAP-based interpretation and a web-based deployment tool, this framework offers a practical approach for individualized DVT risk stratification in the ICU. Future prospective studies should examine whether implementation of this model can improve thromboprophylaxis allocation and ultimately reduce thrombotic complications in this high-risk population.
