Background

Despite extensive evidence demonstrating race- and sex-based disparities in periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) incidence following total joint arthroplasty, there is a lack of evidence on differences in the microbial profiles of pathogens identified. Our study evaluated the race- and sex-based differential microbial patterns of infecting organisms among patients with PJI.

Methods

The Premier Healthcare Database was queried from 2016 to 2023 for patients aged ≥18 years who underwent antibiotic spacer placement for a total joint arthroplasty PJI. Microbiology data were extracted from synovial and tissue cultures obtained during the hospital admission. Microbiological profiles were compared with respect to patient race via multivariable logistic regression. Race–sex subanalysis was then conducted via chi-squared testing.

Results

In total, 9014 patients were identified with 8189 (90.8%) White and 825 (9.2%) Black patients. Black patients had significantly higher adjusted odds of infection by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (aOR: 1.45, [1.18-1.79], P < .001) and gram-negative enterics (aOR: 1.45, [1.15-1.85], P = .01). Furthermore, Black males had the highest rates of MRSA infection (15.1%), while Black females had the highest rates of polymicrobial infections (24.3%).

Conclusions

Black patients had significantly greater risk of MRSA and gram-negative PJIs. Black males had the highest rates of MRSA and gram-negative enteric infection, while Black females exhibited the highest rates of polymicrobial infections. Further research into broader socioeconomic and health access disparities is warranted to explain the observed differences.

Introduction

Although traditional risk stratification for periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) following total joint arthroplasty (TJA) has focused primarily on medical comorbidities and surgical factors, increasing evidence suggests that sociodemographic conditions, such as race and sex, and structural inequities play an important role in shaping infectious risk and disease severity [1]. Specifically, in addition to comorbid conditions such as diabetes, social determinants of health may influence host immunity, access to care, and microbial exposure, thereby affecting both the likelihood of developing PJI and the microbiologic characteristics of infection. For example, using composite measures such as the area deprivation index (ADI) and social vulnerability index (SVI), prior studies have demonstrated that higher levels of social disadvantage are independently associated with an increased risk of gram-negative and polymicrobial PJI [1]. These findings underscore the potential contribution of socioeconomic context to the biological and clinical picture of PJIs.

Current arthroplasty literature describing the differential outcomes following primary TJA based on race and sex is limited to overall PJI risk as well as risk of complications and readmission among minority groups [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. For example, prior investigations have reported higher incidences of PJI in Black patients than non-Hispanic White patients [2]. When evaluating the intersection of race and sex, studies have found that Black males experience the highest incidence of PJI, while White females exhibit the lowest risk [2]. Prior studies have also found that Black patients were at significantly higher risk of complications, readmissions, revisions, and discharge to institutional care following primary TJA [5]. All of these studies alluded to a relationship between both comorbidities and socioeconomic factors that resulted in their findings.

Despite prior studies demonstrating racial and gender-based disparities in arthroplasty outcomes and PJI incidence, there is a lack of evidence on how these demographic factors are associated with the microbial profiles of pathogens identified in PJI cases [2,3]. This represents a critical gap in the literature as the etiology of PJI has also been significantly associated with divergent outcomes and treatment planning [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. As such, the present study sought to evaluate and characterize the patterns of microbial organisms identified in PJI cases stratified by race and sex.

Material and methods

The Premier Healthcare Database (PHD) was retrospectively queried to identify adult patients (aged ≥18 years) who underwent spacer placement for PJI following total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) between January 2016 and December 2023. The PHD is a nationally representative database that includes patient demographics, hospital characteristics, and diagnosis and procedure billing codes from over 1000 hospitals. Additionally, the PHD provides comprehensive patient-specific billing records, including charges for implants and medications administered during hospital admissions.

Cohort selection

Patients with a diagnosis of PJI or other deep infection were identified using International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table S1). Revision procedures and antibiotic spacer placements were identified using ICD-10 procedure coding system and Current Procedural Terminology codes (Supplementary Table S2). Vancomycin, gentamicin, and tobramycin powders used in antibiotic-loaded cement were identified from charge data recorded on the day of surgery (Supplementary Table S3). To accurately capture patients who underwent true antibiotic spacer placement, those with charges reflecting <2000 mg of vancomycin, 2400 mg of gentamicin, or 2400 mg of tobramycin were excluded. Direct charges for antibiotic spacers or antibiotic cement were also included (Supplementary Table S3). Based on these charge data and procedural codes, adult patients who underwent StageOne antibiotic spacer placement for PJI following TJA were included in the final cohort. Using the self-reported race and ethnicity designations provided, the cohort was also divided into non-Hispanic White vs non-Hispanic Black cohorts. Due to limitations in available power, other race and ethnicity designations were excluded for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Table S4). A subanalysis was then conducted where the White and Black cohorts were further subdivided by sex (female vs male) to produce 4 further groups (White-Males, White-Females, Black-Males, and Black-Females).

Microbial profiles

Using microbial culture and antibiotic sensitivity data available through the PHD, the microbial profiles associated with each PJIs were reviewed. Culture data obtained during the index admission were used. Samples obtained from synovial fluid, tissue, and/or wound fluid specimens were included for analysis. Patients with more than 1 pathogen identified were categorized as polymicrobial infections. Antibiotic sensitivity data were then analyzed to differentiate methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) from methicillin sensitive S aureus (MSSA) and Enterococcus species, respectively. Using a combination of clinical relevance and the relative frequency of speciation, all microbial isolates were categorized into one of fifteen subgroups which include MSSA, MRSA, Staphylococcus epidermidis, other Staphylococcal species, Streptococcal species, Enterococcus species, fungal species (including Candida species), gram-negative enterics (including Escherichia, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, Citrobacter, Serratia, and Morganella species), Cutibacterium species, Pseudomonas species, Clostridial species, Corynebacterium species, VRE, polymicrobial, and other.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean (standard deviation) or frequency (proportion). Between group comparisons were made using χ2 or Mann–Whitney U tests, where appropriate. For the subanalysis by race and sex, groups with the largest absolute percent difference (absolute difference >3%) in microbial isolates were compared using χ2. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant for two-sided tests. For tests where significance was met, multivariable logistic regression models on the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of infection by racial designation (White vs Black) were conducted to account for sex, Charlston Comorbidity Index (CCI)1, and type of joint (TKA vs THA). Collectively, these variables were considered reasonable approximations of overall patient characteristics while avoiding the risk of model overfitting, while still capturing variation in patient status which may influence the odds of isolating specific organisms. P values for multivariable models were Bonferroni adjusted (n = 5). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 17.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Discussion

The present study identified a significant association between Black race and an increased risk of MRSA and gram-negative enteric PJIs. In contrast, White patients were more commonly infected with MSSA species, underscoring race-based differences of infecting pathogens among patients being treated for PJI of the hip and knee. Our findings highlight a potential association between comorbidities, socioeconomic factors, and differences in the patterns of infecting organisms identified in PJI cases.

The risk of PJI following THA and TKA varies between racial groups. Several studies concluded comorbid conditions have been strongly linked with susceptibility to more virulent pathogens, including MRSA and gram-negative organisms [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Areti et al. [15] assessed 237,360 patients with TKA and found diabetes (OR = 1.2, P < .001) and obesity (OR = 1.2, P < .001) to be significantly associated with postoperative MRSA infection. In our study, Black patients had significantly higher rates of comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes. However, both cohorts were observed to have similar rates of several other comorbidities, including obesity, liver disease, and pulmonary disease, and both cohorts had similar CCI. As such, comorbidities alone may not sufficiently account for differences in pathogen profiles between racial groups. Our study highlights the need for further research, as the observed differences in infecting organisms may be associated with factors beyond patient comorbidities. In addition, the influence of noncomorbidity factors on the infecting organism requires further analysis.

The observed differences in infecting pathogens may be associated with noncomorbidity-related factors, including socioeconomic determinants, delays in care, and differences in healthcare access, rather than underlying health status alone [1,2,10,12,16,17]. A comprehensive study conducted by the Center for Disease Control analyzing community-associated MRSA in the United States similarly found that the higher incidence among Black patients was almost entirely explained (91%) by socioeconomic variables, with no significant association remaining after adjustment for race [10]. This mediation analysis, based on over 2500 MRSA cases, identified key contributing factors such as residence in lower-income neighborhoods, higher household crowding, lower educational attainment, reduced housing values, medically underserved regions, and limited access to health insurance.

These socioeconomic disparities, including access to care and timeliness of treatment, have also been identified in arthroplasty care. Pinkney et al. [2] found that Black patients had a significantly higher risk of composite PJI than non-Hispanic White patients, with only 26% of this disparity explained by differences in comorbidities. Lizcano et al. assessed 255 revision TKA and THA patients and reported Black patients had significantly higher ADI and SVI than White patients [1]. The authors found that both increased ADI and SVI were independently associated with gram-negative and polymicrobial PJI. Overall, Black patients often present with more advanced disease on initial consultation yet face delays of up to 35% longer time to surgery when attempting to access either TKA or THA [18]. Black patients also undergoes revision surgery at a lower rate despite experiencing higher failure rates after primary TJA [19]. The inequities in access, timeliness, and treatment utilization may contribute to differences in infection severity and microbial profiles observed at presentation. These findings suggest that the observed racial differences in PJI microbial profiles may be associated with both structural inequities and differences in comorbidity burden. Addressing social determinants of health may therefore be essential in reducing infection risk and improving outcomes among vulnerable populations.

The findings of the present study remained consistent after stratification by sex. White males demonstrated the highest overall rate of MSSA infections, while Black males had the highest rates of MRSA and gram-negative enteric infection. Prior studies have proposed that males may demonstrate differing susceptibility to skin flora pathogens such as Staphylococcus species, potentially related to sex-based differences in immune response and hormonal regulation [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Moreover, our finding that females overall, and particularly Black females, exhibited the highest rates of polymicrobial infections is consistent with prior work [20,24,25]. These studies have hypothesized that sex-based differences in immune activation and inflammatory signaling may contribute to variation in microbial colonization patterns [20,24,25].

Sociobiome-related factors may also be associated with variation in microbial patterns observed across racial groups [[26], [27], [28]]. Mallot et al. [26] demonstrated a significant difference in gut microbiome composition between races as early as 3 months of age. Furthermore, it has become increasingly clear that alterations in the gut microbiome are associated with differences in PJI pathogenesis [27]. Poor nutritional status, including malnutrition, diets promoting obesity and poor glycemic control, and micronutrient deficiencies, may also play a critical role in PJI pathogenesis [1,14,27,29,30]. McCafferty et al. [29] evaluated 387 patients with TJA and found that minority patients were more likely to have nutritionally poor diets, with these patients frequently citing the cost of healthy food as a primary barrier. In parallel, Lowik et al. conducted a retrospective study of 237 TJA patients with PJI and found obesity with poor nutritional status to be significantly associated with polymicrobial and gram-negative infections. Taken together, these findings suggest that sociobiome-related factors may be associated with the observed variation in microbial profiles across racial groups, although causal relationships cannot be established from the present study.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this analysis relies on the PHD, which is subject to potential coding inaccuracies and misclassifications. However, because the PHD is used by hospitals and payors for billing and reimbursement, coding accuracy, and standardization are incentivized, which reduces but does not fully eliminate this concern [[31], [32], [33]]. Second, the database primarily captures inpatient hospitalizations and readmissions, which restricts detection of lower-acuity or outpatient complications. While this may underestimate the true complication burden, it also ensures that the outcomes included reflect clinically meaningful events requiring inpatient PJI care. Third, microbiologic culture data were limited to the index hospitalization, precluding assessment of recurrent infections and subsequent culture patterns. This limits the ability to capture the full treatment trajectory of infection, though our focus on the initial hospitalization ensured standardized data collection and analysis across patient groups. Since cohort inclusion was limited to patients undergoing antibiotic spacer placement, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with PJI, particularly those managed with debridment, antibiotics, and implant retention or chronic suppressive strategies. However, this approach was selected to improve diagnostic specificity and reduce treatment-selection heterogeneity between mild and severe infections, thereby allowing for more standardized comparison of microbial profiles among patients requiring revision-level surgical management. Additionally, while geographic region, payer status, and hospital characteristics differed significantly between racial cohorts, these variables were not included in the multivariable model, as we conceptualized them as potential mediators rather than confounders of the race–microbiology association; as such, statistical adjustment for these downstream consequences of racial disparity may attenuate the observed differences rather than isolating a confounding effect. Future work employing formal mediation analysis would help quantify the extent to which these structural factors explain, rather than confound, the observed racial differences in microbial profile. Although adjustment was performed for overall comorbidity burden using the Charlson Comorbidity Index, residual confounding related to individual comorbidities, socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, nutritional status, and other social determinants of health may have contributed to the observed variation in microbial profiles. However, because many of these factors are closely interconnected with race and structural inequities, complete adjustment for all potential confounders may not be feasible and inadvertently obscure clinically meaningful differences in microbial profiles between populations. Furthermore, important clinical factors such as prior antimicrobial exposure, host immune status, surgical technique, timing to intervention, and access to care were not available in the dataset. These variables may influence both the pathogens identified and clinical outcomes. To mitigate this, we employed strict inclusion criteria and adjusted for a broad set of comorbidities, but residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, the database lacks detailed outcome measures such as functional recovery, reinfection rates, or patient-reported outcomes, which limits assessment of the long-term clinical impact.

Nevertheless, the strengths of this study include leveraging a large, nationally representative cohort and applying a rigorous, reproducible case identification strategy through cross-verification of procedural and billing codes. The substantial sample size permitted detailed race and further race-sex stratification which facilitated detection of meaningful microbial trends across demographic groups. Strict inclusion criteria ensured accurate identification of patients undergoing true antibiotic spacer placement for PJI, while robust statistical modeling accounted for key confounders, thereby strengthening the validity of the findings. Collectively, these strengths support the reliability of the observed associations and provide a foundation for future work examining mechanisms and outcomes in greater detail.

Conclusions

In patients undergoing spacer placement for TJA PJI, we observed clear race- and sex-associated trends in microbial profiles. Black patients had higher rates of virulent PJI pathogens, including MRSA and gram-negative organisms. Furthermore, Black males had the highest rate of MRSA infections, while Black females had the highest rate of polymicrobial infections. Although these patterns may in-part be explained by differences in comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, socioeconomic and sociobiome factors shaping microbial exposure and vulnerability to certain pathogens should be considered. Our findings emphasize the need for future studies to integrate social determinants of health to better comprehend these differences in microbial profiles across race and sex.
