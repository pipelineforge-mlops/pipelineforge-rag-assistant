Immunoglobulin A vasculitis (IgAV), formerly Henoch–Schönlein purpura, is a small-vessel vasculitis characterized by palpable purpura, arthralgias, gastrointestinal involvement, and renal disease. Although IgAV is common in children, adult-onset disease is rare and may be associated with more severe manifestations, particularly renal involvement. In adults, IgAV may be difficult to recognize because of overlap with more common gastrointestinal, infectious, or medication-related conditions. We present a case of adult-onset IgAV initially attributed to presumed diverticulitis and later to a drug eruption following antibiotic exposure. This case highlights diagnostic pitfalls and the importance of early recognition and renal surveillance.

1. Introduction

Immunoglobulin A vasculitis (IgAV), formerly known as Henoch–Schönlein purpura (HSP), is a small-vessel vasculitis characterized by palpable purpura, arthralgias, gastrointestinal involvement, and renal disease 1–7. Although IgAV is the most common vasculitis in childhood, with an annual incidence of approximately 10–20 per 100,000 individuals, 1,7 it is rare in adults, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0.1 to 1.8 per 100,000.2 Compared with pediatric disease, adult-onset IgAV is more frequently associated with severe manifestations, particularly renal involvement, which may progress to chronic kidney disease or end-stage renal failure 1–3,6–8.

In clinical practice, IgAV may be difficult to recognize in adults due to its rarity and overlap with more common conditions, particularly when abdominal pain or rash is initially attributed to alternative gastrointestinal, infectious, or medication-related diagnoses.4,7,9 We present a case of IgAV that was initially attributed to presumed diverticulitis and a cutaneous adverse drug reaction following antibiotic exposure.

2. Case presentation

A 48-year-old male with a history of hypertension presented to the emergency department with left lower quadrant abdominal pain. He denied nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, hematochezia, melena, and urinary symptoms. On examination, he was afebrile and hemodynamically stable, with mild tenderness to palpation in the left lower quadrant and a nonpruritic, nonpainful, non-blanching palpable purpuric rash involving both feet. There were no urticarial or targetoid lesions and no mucosal involvement. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. He denied recent viral illness, vaccination, or changes in medications. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a mildly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), preserved renal function, no thrombocytopenia, and urinalysis notable for microscopic hematuria and proteinuria (Table 1). Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated scattered diverticulosis involving the descending and sigmoid colon with mild inflammatory changes at the distal descending-sigmoid junction, consistent with possible diverticulitis. Based on the abdominal pain, elevated ESR, and CT findings, diverticulitis was favored, and he was discharged on oral ciprofloxacin with instructions to follow up regarding the rash.

Three days later, the patient returned to the emergency department because of progression of the rash and new-onset joint symptoms. Although his abdominal pain had resolved, the rash had spread up his legs to the lower abdomen, trunk, and upper extremities (Fig. 1). He also reported severe arthralgias involving the hips, knees, and feet. Given the temporal association with ciprofloxacin initiation, the progressive rash was initially attributed to a cutaneous adverse drug reaction despite its presence at the initial presentation and the patient’s prior tolerance of ciprofloxacin. Ciprofloxacin was discontinued and added to his allergy list. He was prescribed a course of oral prednisone with instructions to follow up with his primary care physician.

At follow-up with his primary care physician, the persistent purpuric rash, microscopic hematuria, and proteinuria raised concern for a systemic vasculitic process rather than an isolated drug eruption. He was referred to dermatology for evaluation of the rash and nephrology for persistent microscopic hematuria and proteinuria. Dermatology obtained a skin biopsy from a lower extremity lesion, which demonstrated leukocytoclastic vasculitis. Direct immunofluorescence (DIF) showed weak vascular C3 and fibrinogen fluorescence without IgA deposition, supporting an immune-mediated small-vessel vasculitis. Serologic testing ordered by the primary care physician during the vasculitis evaluation, including antinuclear antibody, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, and complement levels, was negative. Subsequent nephrology evaluation confirmed persistent microscopic hematuria and proteinuria, with a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.37. With palpable purpura, arthralgias, abdominal pain, and renal involvement, the patient fulfilled the 2010 European League Against Rheumatism/Pediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organization/Pediatric Rheumatology European Society classification criteria5 for IgA vasculitis (Table 2). Ciprofloxacin was later removed from the patient’s allergy list, as the rash had preceded antibiotic exposure and a drug eruption was considered unlikely.

The patient was started on colchicine while completing the 15-day prednisone taper, resulting in improvement in both cutaneous and joint manifestations. Given the mild degree of proteinuria and stable renal function, the nephrology team initiated an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, with plans for close monitoring of renal function, hematuria, and proteinuria. Over several months of follow-up, renal function remained stable, with resolution of hematuria and proteinuria and no recurrence of systemic symptoms.

3. Discussion

Adult-onset IgA vasculitis (IgAV) can be difficult to recognize because its manifestations may overlap with more common gastrointestinal and medication-related conditions.2,4,7 In this case, abdominal pain with CT findings suggestive of diverticulitis initially focused attention on an intra-abdominal process, while subsequent ciprofloxacin exposure led the progressive rash to be interpreted as a hypersensitivity reaction. However, the overall presentation was more consistent with a unifying vasculitic process, given the palpable purpura, arthralgias, microscopic hematuria, and proteinuria. The fact that the rash preceded antibiotic exposure, together with prior tolerance of ciprofloxacin, made a drug-induced eruption less likely.4,7 Recent adult data also suggest that gastrointestinal involvement is associated with higher rates of misdiagnosis and may be accompanied by more extensive skin and joint involvement.9

The diagnosis of IgAV in this patient was supported by the overall clinicopathologic picture. Skin biopsy demonstrated leukocytoclastic vasculitis, and serologic evaluation was unrevealing for alternative systemic vasculitides. Direct immunofluorescence did not show IgA deposition. Although IgA-predominant vascular deposition on direct immunofluorescence is characteristic of IgAV, its absence does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly in this case, where the biopsy was obtained several days after symptom onset and after corticosteroid initiation, both of which may reduce or bias immunofluorescence yield as lesions evolve over time 1,7,10,11. The patient also fulfilled the 2010 European League Against Rheumatism/Pediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organization/Pediatric Rheumatology European Society classification criteria for IgA vasculitis.5 Although these criteria were developed for pediatric populations, they appear to retain supportive value in adults; one adult cohort reported a sensitivity of 99.2% and specificity of 86.0%, similar to the high diagnostic performance reported in the original pediatric validation study.12

Renal involvement is the major determinant of long-term prognosis in adult IgAV and warrants close surveillance.2,3,6,7 In this case, microscopic hematuria and mild proteinuria were consistent with early renal involvement, but kidney function remained preserved. In adults with preserved renal function and low-grade proteinuria, management is generally supportive and includes blood pressure optimization, consideration of renin-angiotensin system blockade when proteinuria is persistent or clinically significant, and close longitudinal monitoring of renal function, hematuria, and proteinuria. 2,6,7 Although systemic glucocorticoids may improve cutaneous, joint, and gastrointestinal symptoms, their effect on long-term renal outcomes in adult IgAV remains uncertain 2,6,7. Colchicine has been reported as an adjunctive option for persistent or recurrent cutaneous IgAV, although supporting evidence remains limited and largely observational. 6,8 Given this patient’s mild renal involvement, stable kidney function, and improvement in hematuria and proteinuria with supportive therapy, further immunosuppressive escalation was not indicated.

4. Conclusion

Adult-onset IgA vasculitis can present with nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms and purpuric rash that overlaps with more common infectious or medication-related conditions, which may delay diagnosis. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining suspicion for IgAV when palpable purpura is accompanied by abdominal pain, arthralgias, and urinary abnormalities. Early recognition is essential to ensure appropriate renal surveillance and risk-stratified management, given the potential for progression of renal involvement in adults.
