Purpose

Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) evades antibiotics and drives inflammation by surviving within macrophages. This study explored metformin (MET), a common AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activator, as a host-directed therapy against hvKp.

Patients and Methods

Using hvKp strain NTUH-K2044-infected THP-1 macrophages, assays measured intracellular bacterial survival, autophagy flux, phagocytosis, and cytokine expression. AMPK-autophagy involvement was tested with agonists/inhibitors and siRNA. A murine liver abscess model assessed MET’s efficacy on bacterial burden and immunopathology.

Results

MET reduced intracellular hvKp in macrophages and improved phagocytosis. It activated AMPK, restored autophagy, and increased phagosome-lysosome fusion—effects blocked by AMPK or autophagy inhibition. MET also lowered pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). In mice, MET monotherapy decreased liver bacterial loads and reduced tissue damage and inflammation.

Conclusion

MET reprograms macrophages to clear intracellular hvKp through AMPK-driven autophagy and phagocytosis while curbing excessive inflammation, showing promise as a host-directed adjunctive therapy for persistent hvKp infections.

Introduction

The escalating crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a serious threat to global public health, with carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae, identified as critical priority pathogens by the World Health Organization. Among these, hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) has emerged as a significant global pathogen, causing severe community-acquired infections such as pyogenic liver abscesses and metastatic meningitis, even in immunocompetent individuals.1 Unlike classical K. pneumoniae (cKp), hvKp is characterized by hypermucoviscosity, enhanced resistance to phagocytosis, and a remarkable capacity for systemic dissemination.2,3 The epidemiology and pathogenic mechanisms of K. pneumoniae, including the critical role of siderophores in its virulence, have been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere.4,5

A key facet of hvKp pathogenicity is its ability to subvert host innate immunity by surviving within macrophages. hvKp disrupts phagosome maturation, establishing a protected intracellular niche within Klebsiella-containing vacuoles (KCVs) that allows it to replicate and evade antibiotic clearance.6–9 This intracellular persistence not only facilitates systemic dissemination but also triggers dysregulated inflammatory responses, leading to excessive inflammasome activation, pro-inflammatory cytokine release (eg, IL-1β, IL-18), and subsequent tissue damage.10 Consequently, conventional antibiotics often fail to eradicate these intracellular reservoirs, highlighting an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies—particularly in the context of rising carbapenem-resistant hvKp (CR-hvKp) strains.11,12 Host-directed therapy (HDT), which aims to enhance the host’s antimicrobial immune functions while mitigating pathological inflammation, presents a promising alternative.13–15 Autophagy, a lysosome-dependent degradation pathway regulated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), plays a dual role in pathogen clearance and inflammation resolution.16 Metformin (MET), a well-tolerated AMPK activator widely used for type 2 diabetes, has demonstrated immunometabolic benefits in other bacterial infections by promoting autophagy and dampening inflammation.17,18 Given its widespread clinical use and safety profile, exploring its host-directed effects holds significant translational potential for managing infections in diabetic patients.

In this study, we investigated whether metformin could enhance the phagocytic and intracellular killing capacity of macrophages against hypervirulent K. pneumoniae. Specifically, we aimed to determine: (i) whether MET promotes intracellular clearance of hvKp through AMPK-mediated autophagy restoration; (ii) whether MET enhances macrophage phagocytosis and modulates inflammatory responses; and (iii) whether MET confers protective effects in a murine model of hvKp infection. By elucidating these mechanisms, we seek to provide a preclinical rationale for repurposing metformin as a host-directed therapy against hvKp, particularly in diabetic populations who are at increased risk for severe hvKp infections.

Material and Methods

Bacterial Strains, Cell Culture, and Reagents

The hypervirulent K. pneumoniae strain NTUH-K2044 (a well-characterized reference strain carrying classical virulence genes such as rmpA and iucA) and the classical strain ATCC 700603 were used in this study. Both strains were cultured in LB broth at 37°C with shaking; for infections, mid-logarithmic phase bacteria (OD600 ≈ 0.6) were harvested, washed, and resuspended in antibiotic-free RPMI-1640. THP-1 monocytes were maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics, and differentiated into macrophages with 100 nM PMA for 48 h followed by a 24 h rest period. Metformin (Sigma) was dissolved in PBS. AICAR, rapamycin, bafilomycin A1 (MCE), 3-MA, and Compound C (Selleckchem) were used per manufacturer’s instructions, with DMSO concentrations not exceeding 0.1%.

In vitro Infection and Intracellular Killing Assay

PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages were infected with hvKp at an MOI of 10:1 for 4 h. Extracellular bacteria were then removed by washing and a 1 h treatment with gentamicin (100 µg/mL). Subsequently, fresh medium containing gentamicin (50 µg/mL) was added to maintain suppression of any remaining extracellular bacteria throughout the incubation period. At designated time points, cells were lysed with sterile water and serial dilutions were plated on LB agar for bacterial enumeration (CFU assay). Bacterial CFU were normalized to total protein concentration of the lysed cell samples, as determined by a BCA protein assay, to account for any variations in cell number or viability across wells.

Confocal Microscopy for Lysosomal Colocalization and Autophagic Flux

For lysosomal colocalization, macrophages were infected with GFP-expressing hvKp (NTUH-K2044) for 4 h, then treated with MET or rapamycin for 4 h, followed by staining with LysoTracker Red (LTR). For autophagic flux analysis, cells transfected with an mCherry-GFP-LC3B plasmid were differentiated, infected with hvKp, and then treated with or without MET for 6 h. All samples were fixed and imaged using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV1000/Nikon A1R) with a 60× oil-immersion objective lens. Images were analyzed with ImageJ software, quantifying LTR-GFP-hvKp co-localization or LC3 puncta formation in 30–40 cells per condition.

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were quantified by BCA assay, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were probed with antibodies against LC3B (Abcam), AMPKα, p-AMPKα, ATG5, and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology).

RNA Interference and Transfection

PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages were seeded in 12-well plates at 2.5×105 cells/well in antibiotic-free RPMI-1640/10% FBS and transfected with siRNAs ATG5 and AMPKα1/α2 using Lipo8000™ (Beyotime, China) for 48 h. Genes knock down efficiency was confirmed with real-time PCR or Western blot assays.

RNA Isolation and Gene Expression Analysis

Following 6 h infection of THP-1 macrophages with hvKp (MOI 10:1) either untreated or treated with 2 mM MET, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed in triplicate with SYBR Green (Takara, Japan) to quantify the levels of IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α genes under cycling conditions: 95°C for 30s; 40 cycles of 95°C for 5s and 60°C for 30s. GAPDH was amplified as an internal control. The primers used in this study were designed based on gene sequences obtained from GenBank (NCBI) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd. The following primers were used: IFN-γ, GCATCGTTTTGGGTTCTCTTT (forward) and CCTTTTTCGCCTTGCTGTT (reverse); IL-1β, ATGATGGCTTATTACAGTGGCAA (forward) and GTCGGAGATTCGTAGCTGGA (reverse); IL-6, ACTCACCTCTTCAGAACGAATTG (forward) and CCATCTTTGGAAGGTTCAGGTTG (reverse); TNF-α, CCTCTCTCTAATCAGCCCTCTG (forward) and GAGGACCTGGGAGTAGATGAG (reverse); GAPDH, TCGTCCCGTAGACAAAATGG (forward) and TTGAGGTCAATGAAGGGGTC (reverse).

Mouse Infection Model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 1996) and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval number: 2021-291). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Mice were infected intraperitoneally with 106 CFU of hvKp (NTUH-K2044). Two hours post-infection, mice received either MET (250 mg/kg/day) or saline control via drinking water. At 48 h post-infection, mice were euthanized for collection of organs (liver, spleen, kidney) and blood. All mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation followed by cervical dislocation at the end of the experiment, in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). No surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia. Bacterial loads in organs were determined by CFU plating. Serum cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) were quantified by ELISA. For histopathology, organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E.

Histological Analysis

The collected organs (liver, spleen, and kidney) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3 µm thickness. The tissue sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining for histological examination. Pathological sites were observed under a microscope and photographed.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. All in vitro experiments were conducted in at least three independent biological replicates, with two technical replicates each. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and equality of variances was confirmed using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For animal experiments, two-way ANOVA was used for group comparisons. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No formal power calculation was performed; the sample size (n=5 per group for animal experiments) was chosen based on previous studies in the field.

MET Restores Autophagic Flux to Overcome hvKp’s Lysosomal Evasion

We found that the antibacterial effect of MET was independent of reactive oxygen species, as hvKp infection suppressed mitochondrial ROS, and MET did not reverse this effect nor alter cytoplasmic ROS levels (Figure S3A and B). We therefore investigated autophagy, a key host pathway for lysosomal degradation of intracellular pathogens.19 MET significantly increased autophagy, indicated by LC3-II accumulation, in both infected and uninfected macrophages (Figure 2A). To confirm this reflected enhanced autophagic flux rather than blocked degradation, we used the lysosomal inhibitor Baf A1. The greater accumulation of LC3-II in MET-treated cells upon Baf A1 inhibition demonstrated that MET indeed stimulates robust and complete autophagic flux (Figure 2B). Imaging of macrophages expressing the mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter confirmed enhanced autophagic flux, shown by increased autophagosome (yellow puncta) and autolysosome (red puncta) formation upon MET treatment (Figure 2C–E). Critically, MET also significantly promoted the co-localization of GFP-expressing hvKp with lysotracker-stained lysosomes (Figure 2F and G), indicating successful restoration of phagosome-lysosome fusion, a process hvKp normally disrupts. To further validate that pharmacological activation of autophagy is sufficient to eliminate intracellular hvKp, we treated infected macrophages with rapamycin, a well-established autophagy inducer. The central role of AMPK was confirmed pharmacologically: the AMPK agonist AICAR mimicked MET’s antibacterial effect, while the inhibitor Compound C blocked it (Figure 4B and C). Crucially, genetic knockdown of AMPKα completely abrogated the ability of both MET and AICAR to clear intracellular bacteria (Figure 4D and E). MET Enhances Bacterial Clearance and Reduces Immunopathology in a Murine Liver Abscess Model

To translate our findings in vivo, we established a murine liver abscess model via intraperitoneal injection of NTUH-K2044. MET monotherapy significantly reduced bacterial burdens in the liver, spleen, and kidney compared to untreated controls (Figure 6A–C). This enhanced clearance was accompanied by a marked attenuation of the systemic inflammatory response, with serum levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β being significantly lower in MET-treated mice (Figure 6D–F). Discussion

Hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKp) presents a formidable global health challenge, exacerbated by rising multidrug resistance and its ability to evade antibiotics by persisting within macrophages.20–22 Host-directed therapy (HDT) has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these limitations by modulating host immunity.23 Our study establishes metformin (MET) as a compelling HDT candidate against hvKp. We demonstrate that MET reprograms macrophages through AMPK-dependent enhancement of phagocytosis, suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and—most critically—restoration of autophagic flux to enable lysosomal degradation of intracellular hvKp, effects that translated to reduced bacterial loads and attenuated immunopathology in vivo.

A key immune evasion mechanism of hvKp is the blockade of phagosome-lysosome fusion, allowing its survival within macrophages.11 Our data show that MET counteracts this evasion by activating AMPK and restoring full autophagic flux, culminating in lysosomal degradation of hvKp. The causal role of autophagy was confirmed by loss of MET’s bactericidal effect upon ATG5 knockdown. Furthermore, MET enhanced phagocytosis in an AMPK-dependent manner, consistent with prior reports,24–27 consolidating the role of this pathway in orchestrating multiple antimicrobial functions against hvKp.

Beyond enhancing bacterial clearance, MET potently suppressed hvKp-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, consistent with AMPK’s established role as a negative regulator of NF-κB signaling.28 This dual capacity to concurrently disrupt intracellular bacterial persistence and temper immunopathology addresses the core clinical challenges in severe hvKp infections.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our findings are based on a single hvKp strain and a single macrophage cell line; validation across diverse clinical isolates and primary cells is needed. We also did not test MET in combination with conventional antibiotics or in diabetic models, which limits conclusions about its role as an adjunctive therapy. Additionally, our anti-inflammatory conclusions rely on mRNA data and require protein-level confirmation. Finally, the small sample size (n=5) in animal experiments may limit statistical power. Future studies addressing these limitations are warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that metformin enhances macrophage anti-hvKp defenses through AMPK-dependent activation of both phagocytosis and autophagic flux, while simultaneously curbing excessive inflammation. These findings suggest that metformin has the potential to be explored as a host-directed therapeutic candidate for hvKp infections, particularly in clinical contexts where intracellular persistence and antibiotic resistance pose major challenges. However, further studies—including combinatorial testing with antibiotics, validation across diverse clinical isolates, and assessment in diabetic models—are required to translate these preclinical observations into clinical practice.
