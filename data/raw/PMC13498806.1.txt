Background

Metabolic disease is accelerating across Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), where calorie-focused dietary prescriptions frequently fail due to cost and complexity. Time-Restricted Eating (TRE) is a distinct approach. It restructures when food is eaten rather than what or how much, limiting daily intake to a 6 to 10 hour window as a cost-free chronomedicine strategy. Its mechanisms and long-term glycemic effects in diverse LMIC populations remain incompletely synthesised.

Objectives

To systematically review TRE’s clinical efficacy on glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, and anthropometric outcomes across LMIC cohorts, and to evaluate molecular evidence for AMPK/mTOR pathway activation.

Data Sources and Methods

A PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Eligible studies included randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental designs involving adults in LMICs with metabolic conditions. TRE windows of 6 to 10 hours were required. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2.0 and ROBINS-I. The review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261352841).

Results

Twenty-six unique cohorts (28 reports) across India, China, Indonesia, Iran, Tunisia, and Brazil were included. TRE was associated with reductions in insulin resistance, fasting blood sugar, and body weight across most included studies. A persistent HbA1c reduction of -0.78% at 18 months in one longitudinal cohort suggested that glycemic benefits may compound with sustained adherence. Contradictions emerged between centres regarding TRE’s superiority over caloric restriction.

Conclusion

TRE may represent a feasible, low-cost dietary strategy for metabolic health management in resource-limited settings, though further evidence from larger and longer trials is needed.

Plain language summary

Millions of people in developing countries live with Type 2 Diabetes, yet expensive dietary programmes are often unaffordable. Time-Restricted Eating simply limits the hours during which food is consumed each day. This review examined 26 studies from India, China, Indonesia, Iran, Tunisia, and Brazil. Restricting eating to 6 to 10 hours daily was associated with improvements in blood sugar, reduced insulin resistance, and supported weight loss without requiring changes to food quality or supplements. Blood sugar improvements remained evident 18 months later. One small study found early signs of cellular energy changes within 20 days of starting TRE. Growing evidence suggests that the timing of meals may be as important as the content of meals in managing metabolic disease.

1. Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) has reached epidemic proportions globally. 1 The burden falls hardest on Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs). 1 India alone is estimated to have approximately 101 million adults living with diabetes. 1 Across urban centres in South Asia, East Asia, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, and Latin America, metabolic disease is the defining public health challenge of this century. 2

Traditional dietary strategies, including caloric restriction, portion control, and structured macronutrient-based meal plans, repeatedly fail in LMIC settings. 3 Caloric restriction, tracking and limiting total daily energy intake, demands sustained cognitive effort. 4 Specialised dietary prescriptions are financially inaccessible. 5 In many settings, cultural food practices are incompatible with rigid macronutrient targets. 3 A scalable, affordable, and culturally adaptable strategy is urgently needed. 6

Time-Restricted Eating (TRE) confines food intake to a consistent daily window of 6 to 10 hours. It requires no dietary quality change and no financial expenditure. Its mechanism is rooted in circadian biology. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) regulates peripheral clocks in the liver, pancreas, and adipose tissue. Late-night eating, prevalent across LMIC urban settings, induces circadian misalignment. This impairs glucose metabolism and hormonal regulation.7,8 TRE corrects this by acting as a zeitgeber, an entraining cue that resets peripheral clocks. Fasting beyond 12 hours activates AMPK, shifting fuel use from glucose to ketones. The mTOR pathway, activated during nutrient intake, counterbalances this. Together, the AMPK/mTOR axis forms a molecular switch that TRE regulates through structured feeding cycles. 9

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined TRE in human populations. Chang and colleagues conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis showing that TRE produced modest weight loss and energy deficits in overweight adults, primarily drawing on Western and East Asian clinical trial data. 10 Nam and colleagues synthesised eight RCTs in patients with Type 2 Diabetes and found significant reductions in fasting glucose and HbA1c, though none of the included studies specifically targeted LMIC populations or examined outcomes beyond 12 weeks. 11 Lin and colleagues reviewed TRE exclusively in NAFLD patients and concluded that evidence remained insufficient for a clinical recommendation. 12 None of these reviews synthesised evidence across the full spectrum of LMIC metabolic disease conditions, examined 18-month longitudinal outcomes, or evaluated the molecular AMPK/mTOR evidence base from LMIC cohorts.

This review addresses these gaps with four objectives: (1) synthesise evidence on TRE’s clinical efficacy across LMIC populations; (2) evaluate molecular evidence for AMPK/mTOR activation; (3) examine the durability of effects up to 18 months; and (4) identify contradictions and research gaps.

2. Methods

2.1. Protocol and Registration

This systematic review was conducted and reported according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines. 13 The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261352841) prior to data extraction. No deviations from the registered protocol were made. Searches were completed in March, 2026 prior to title/abstract screening and data analysis.

2.2. Eligibility Criteria

2.2.1. Inclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following:• Population: Adults aged 18 to 70 years residing in World Bank-classified LMICs, diagnosed with at least one of the following: T2D, Metabolic Syndrome, Obesity, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), or Metabolic-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD/NAFLD).

• Intervention: TRE with a defined daily eating window of 6 to 10 hours, with or without dietary or exercise co-interventions. Ramadan fasting and diurnal intermittent fasting protocols with comparable temporal structures were considered.

• Comparator: Standard care, ad libitum eating, or daily caloric restriction.

• Outcomes: Primary outcomes were HbA1c, fasting blood sugar (FBS), and HOMA-IR. Secondary outcomes were body weight, waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat area (VFA), and AMPK/mTOR expression.

• Study Design: Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies with a minimum duration of four weeks. Purely observational designs without a defined TRE intervention were excluded, as they cannot establish a temporal exposure-outcome relationship for a structured eating-window intervention.

• Publication: Peer-reviewed journal articles published in any language from database inception to mid-2025.

2.2.1. Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following:• Conducted exclusively in high-income countries as defined by the World Bank

• No clearly defined TRE eating window reported

• Animal studies, in vitro studies, case reports, editorials, letters, or narrative reviews

• Paediatric populations (under 18 years)

• Duplicate publications reporting identical outcomes from the same cohort without additional data

• Conference abstracts without full-text availability

• Interventions shorter than four weeks

2.3. Information Sources

Three electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Searches covered all records from database inception through mid-March, 2026. Reference lists of all included studies were hand-searched. No grey literature or unpublished data were sought, which is acknowledged as a potential limitation.

2.4. Search Strategy

Search terms were constructed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied. The full search string for PubMed was:

(“time-restricted eating” OR “time-restricted feeding” OR “intermittent fasting” OR “chronomedicine” OR “circadian rhythm diet”) AND (“insulin resistance” OR “HbA1c” OR “HOMA-IR” OR “fasting blood sugar” OR “type 2 diabetes” OR “metabolic syndrome” OR “AMPK” OR “mTOR”) AND (“low- and middle-income countries” OR “developing countries” OR “India” OR “China” OR “Indonesia” OR “Iran” OR “Brazil” OR “Tunisia” OR “LMIC”).

The Scopus search string was: TITLE-ABS-KEY (“time-restricted eating” OR “time-restricted feeding” OR “intermittent fasting” OR “chronomedicine”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (“insulin resistance” OR “HbA1c” OR “HOMA-IR” OR “type 2 diabetes” OR “metabolic syndrome” OR “AMPK” OR “mTOR”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (“developing countries” OR “low- and middle-income countries” OR “LMIC” OR “India” OR “China” OR “Indonesia” OR “Iran” OR “Brazil” OR “Tunisia”).

The Web of Science search string was: TS=(“time-restricted eating” OR “time-restricted feeding” OR “intermittent fasting” OR “chronomedicine”) AND TS=(“insulin resistance” OR “HbA1c” OR “HOMA-IR” OR “type 2 diabetes” OR “metabolic syndrome”) AND TS=(“developing countries” OR “low- and middle-income countries” OR “India” OR “China” OR “Indonesia” OR “Iran” OR “Brazil” OR “Tunisia”).

2.5. Study Selection

Two reviewers (E.A. and M.A.) independently screened all titles and abstracts using Rayyan software. Full texts were independently assessed against the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion; where consensus was not reached, a third reviewer (C.A.) adjudicated. Inter-rater agreement at the title and abstract screening stage was substantial (Cohen’s kappa = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.71 to 0.85). Agreement at full-text stage was excellent (Cohen’s kappa = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.76 to 0.91). 2.6. Data Extraction

Two reviewers (E.A. and M.A.) independently extracted data using a standardised, pre-piloted extraction form. Extracted fields included: study design, country, setting, sample size, participant characteristics, TRE protocol (window duration and timing), comparator, intervention duration, primary and secondary outcomes, and follow-up periods. For companion reports sharing a primary cohort, data extraction prioritised the primary report. Companion data were used only where the outcome was absent in the primary source. Where outcome data were not reported numerically, the direction and statistical significance of the reported effect were extracted as reported. Missing summary statistics were not imputed. Authors were contacted for additional data.

2.7. Risk of Bias Assessment

Methodological quality was assessed independently by two reviewers (E.A. and C.A.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (RoB 2.0) for RCTs and the ROBINS-I framework for quasi-experimental studies. Five domains were evaluated: randomisation process, deviations from intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Disagreements were resolved by discussion. Inter-rater agreement for risk of bias assessment was good (Cohen’s kappa = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.68 to 0.83). Risk of bias was assessed at the study level for each included report. Performance bias was rated as universally high across all included studies. This is an inherent and unavoidable feature of behavioural dietary interventions, where participant blinding to meal timing is impossible. Reliance on objective biochemical markers (HbA1c, HOMA-IR) as primary outcomes substantially mitigated detection bias.

2.8. Data Synthesis and Certainty Assessment

Given the clinical and methodological heterogeneity across included cohorts, including variation in underlying metabolic diagnosis (T2D, obesity, metabolic syndrome, PCOS, MASLD/NAFLD), a narrative synthesis approach was adopted in accordance with the Synthesis without Meta-Analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines. Findings were grouped by outcome domain rather than pooled across conditions, to avoid conflating distinct clinical populations. For continuous outcomes, the effect measure of interest was the mean change from baseline with standard deviation, or mean difference between groups were reported. For outcomes not reported numerically, the direction of effect (improvement, no change, or worsening) and reported p-value were extracted. Studies contributing to each thematic synthesis group were selected based on the primary outcome reported. Studies reporting glycemic outcomes contributed to the glycemic synthesis. Studies reporting HOMA-IR contributed to the insulin sensitivity synthesis. Studies reporting body weight or waist-to-hip ratio contributed to the anthropometric synthesis. Studies reporting AMPK or mTOR expression contributed to the molecular synthesis. No data conversions were performed. Where studies reported outcomes in different units, values were described narratively without conversion. Due to the small number of studies per outcome group and high clinical heterogeneity in intervention design, eating window timing, and population characteristics, no subgroup analyses or meta-regression were conducted. Window duration across the 26 cohorts was also uneven: 15 cohorts used an 8-hour window, while 6-hour, 9-hour, and 10-hour windows were represented by only 1 to 7 cohorts each. This distribution does not support a balanced subgroup comparison within the current evidence base. As more LMIC trials accumulate at each window length, formal subgroup comparison, for example 6 to 8 hours versus 8 to 10 hours, becomes a clear priority for future synthesis. No sensitivity analyses were performed, as the narrative synthesis design did not permit formal robustness testing. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the review. Risk of reporting bias was not formally assessed using funnel plots or statistical tests, as the narrative synthesis design precluded these methods. The potential for publication bias was considered qualitatively during result interpretation, given the known tendency for journals to preferentially publish positive dietary intervention findings. Outcomes were grouped thematically: glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, anthropometric outcomes, and molecular markers. Studies were further organised by geographic axis to enable contextual comparison. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach across the four main outcome categories. Evidence for insulin sensitivity (HOMA-IR) was graded as moderate certainty. Evidence for glycemic control (HbA1c) was graded as low certainty, largely due to the borderline significance observed across shorter studies. Evidence for anthropometric outcomes was graded as moderate certainty. Evidence for molecular markers (AMPK/mTOR) was graded as very low certainty, reflecting the single small cohort providing direct molecular data. Evidence for HOMA-IR was graded as moderate certainty based on six cohorts involving 684 participants. A summary of findings is presented in Table 3.

3. Results

3.1. Study Selection

The database search retrieved 1,573 records. After removing 412 duplicates, 1,161 titles and abstracts were screened. Of these, 1,077 were excluded at the screening stage. Full-text review was performed for 84 articles. Fifty-six were excluded: 18 were conducted in high-income countries, 14 reported no defined TRE window, 9 were animal or in vitro studies, 8 had intervention durations below four weeks, 4 were conference abstracts, and 3 were duplicate reports without additional outcome data. Twenty-eight reports representing 26 unique participant cohorts were included. The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram is presented in Figure 1.

3.3. Glycemic Control and the Proposed Glycemic Legacy Framework

TRE produced consistent glycemic improvements across the 26 cohorts. Effect magnitude varied by study duration, eating window timing, and population type. Short-duration studies of 6 to 8 weeks tended to show non-significant HbA1c trends. Longer studies demonstrated statistically and clinically meaningful reductions.14,17 The strongest longitudinal evidence came from the Lucknow Axis. 14 This 18-month study of 273 adults with T2D documented a persistent HbA1c reduction of -0.78% at final follow-up. Rastogi and colleagues reported that HbA1c reductions were sustained and appeared to deepen across the 18-month follow-up, suggesting that continued adherence may be associated with progressive rather than plateauing glycemic benefit. 14 We propose an interpretive framework we term the Glycemic Legacy to describe this pattern, wherein sustained meal-timing behaviour may produce compounding rather than attenuating metabolic benefit. This framework requires prospective validation. A companion report from the same institution, Rastogi and colleagues documented significant improvements in total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL cholesterol, with corresponding HDL improvement, providing additional evidence that meal timing interventions produce broad cardiometabolic benefit beyond glycemic outcomes alone. 16 The Tianjin Axis demonstrated meaningful FBS reductions among 120 overweight T2D adults using a self-chosen 10-hour window, without caloric restriction. 19 This is clinically important. It shows that the fasting window itself, not energy restriction, drives glycemic improvement. The Fu and colleagues quasi-experimental study replicated this finding over 12 weeks with a 1-year follow-up, reinforcing the durability of the effect. 20 The TREATY-FLD trial demonstrated that 12 months of 8-hour TRE was as effective as daily caloric restriction for managing NAFLD-related glycemic dysregulation. 17 A companion metagenomics analysis of the same cohort identified TRE-induced shifts in gut microbiome composition consistent with reduced metabolic endotoxaemia, pointing to a secondary glycemic mechanism beyond circadian entrainment alone. 32 Among Iranian cohorts, the DASH-combined TRE protocol, the vegetarian diet protocol, and the PCOS cohort all reported consistent glycemic improvement within 8 to 12 weeks.27-29 These findings suggest that TRE’s glycemic effects are robust across different co-intervention designs.

3.4. Insulin Sensitivity: A Mechanistic Reset

Insulin resistance improvements were the most consistent finding across included studies. The Lucknow hypocaloric TRE cohort achieved a HOMA-IR reduction of 2.6 ± 1.9, significantly outperforming standard caloric restriction at 1.55 ± 1.2 (p = 0.024). 15 Meal timing contributed an independent benefit beyond energy restriction alone. The Tianjin Axis achieved clinically meaningful HOMA-IR reduction without any caloric restriction requirement. 19 This suggests that structured fasting, independent of energy intake, may be sufficient to improve insulin signalling. The Sfax Axis, using TRE combined with high-intensity functional training in Tunisian obese women, achieved substantial insulin sensitivity gains, suggesting additive benefit when TRE is paired with physical activity. 33 However, none of the included studies used a design that could isolate TRE’s independent effect from the exercise co-intervention, so the relative contribution of each component to observed insulin sensitivity gains cannot be determined from this evidence base. The pattern of insulin sensitivity improvement observed across South Asia, East Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, and Latin America is consistent with a shared biological response to structured fasting. Among resistance-trained Tunisian males, Moro and colleagues reported significant fat mass reduction with preserved lean mass and improved inflammatory markers over 8 weeks of 16:8 TRE, suggesting that TRE benefits extend to physically active LMIC populations. 35 These findings suggest that the metabolic benefits of TRE may not be limited to specific dietary patterns or population phenotypes, though larger and more diverse studies are needed to confirm this. Direct measures of fasting insulin and C-peptide were not reported in any included study; HOMA-IR served as the sole insulin resistance proxy across the review.

3.5. Anthropometric Outcomes and the Visceral Fat Debate

Body weight reductions were observed across virtually all included cohorts.14,17,33,38,39 Magnitudes ranged from approximately 1 kg in short protocols to more than 4 kg in longer or intensive studies. Reductions were achieved without mandatory dietary composition changes. The relationship between TRE and visceral fat area (VFA) generated notable contradictions. The Xi’an Axis reported that 8-hour TRE was significantly superior to standard dietary advice for reducing VFA and waist-to-hip ratio (p = 0.023). 18 Early TRE outperformed late TRE on this outcome. Conversely, the Guangzhou Axis found equal efficacy between 12-month TRE and daily caloric restriction for VFA reduction. 17 Among Brazilian cohorts, Ribeiro and colleagues found that TRE combined with continuous energy restriction improved body composition and metabolic parameters in active adults with metabolic syndrome, though the improvement was not significantly greater than continuous energy restriction alone. 38 Schroder and colleagues similarly reported meaningful reductions in weight, metabolic syndrome components, and cardiovascular risk in obese women over three months of 8-hour TRE. 39 These findings are consistent with the broader pattern of TRE producing clinically meaningful anthropometric benefits across diverse LMIC populations. These contradictions most plausibly reflect adherence monitoring differences. Xi’an used the Zhihui Hao Yiyuan digital application for real-time tracking. Other centres relied on self-reported food diaries. This pattern is consistent with evidence that objective dietary monitoring produces more accurate adherence data than self-report and may detect stronger intervention effects. 40 The Brazilian cohort found that early TRE produced superior cardiometabolic improvements compared to delay TRE. 41 This aligns with the zeitgeber hypothesis. An eating window anchored to morning hours more closely synchronises peripheral clocks with the SCN, amplifying the metabolic benefit of the overnight fast. Among Tunisian cohorts, Miladi and colleagues reported improvements in pulmonary function and postural balance alongside weight reduction in obese women over 12 weeks of 16:8 TRE, extending the evidence base for TRE beyond traditional metabolic outcomes. 34 Miladi and colleagues also found that early TRE was superior to late TRE for cardiometabolic outcomes when combined with aerobic exercise, consistent with the zeitgeber hypothesis supporting morning-anchored eating windows. 36

3.6. Molecular Evidence: The AMPK/mTOR Switch

The Surabaya Axis provided the most direct molecular evidence from an LMIC cohort. 26 Twenty obese young women at Universitas Airlangga, Indonesia, underwent a 20-day intensive protocol with a 6-hour eating window (14:00 to 20:00). The study documented a downward trend in AMPK activation and stable mTOR levels. This finding requires careful interpretation. The direction, a decrease rather than an increase in AMPK, reflects early-phase recalibration. The body appears to require substantially longer than 20 days to translate initial molecular signals into sustained clinical change. This mismatch between early molecular signals and later clinical payoff has direct implications for patient counselling. Importantly, Alternate Day Modified Fasting (ADMF) significantly decreased AMPK activity in the same cohort (p = 0.043). Refeeding-induced insulin spikes on fasting-off days suppress catabolic signalling more aggressively than consistent daily TRE windows. 9 This provides a mechanistic argument for daily TRE over alternate-day protocols in LMIC settings. The companion report from the same cohort documented an IGF-1 reduction from 209.4 to 103.9 ng/mL. 26 This suggests notable endocrine remodelling may occur within 20 days of initiating TRE. The Guangzhou gut microbiome companion analysis identified TRE-induced microbial shifts consistent with reduced intestinal permeability and improved metabolic endotoxaemia. 42 These changes are mechanistically linked to insulin sensitivity and systemic inflammation, both downstream consequences of AMPK/mTOR dysregulation. 3.7. Risk of Bias Assessment

Randomisation quality was high across 24 RCTs. Computer-generated allocation sequences and SNOSE methods were used. Performance bias was high across all studies. This is unavoidable in behavioural dietary interventions. Dropout rates of 15% to 25% in longitudinal studies introduced moderate risk from missing outcome data. Objective primary outcomes (HbA1c by automated assay, HOMA-IR from fasting markers, and VFA by MRI or bioelectrical impedance) reduced detection bias and provide high certainty for primary findings. Self-reported dietary logs remain the most significant methodological weakness across LMIC centres. Inter-rater agreement for the risk of bias assessment was good (Cohen’s kappa = 0.76). 3.8. Data Synthesis and Certainty Assessment

Narrative synthesis was used throughout. Evidence was grouped by outcome domain and geographic axis. GRADE certainty ratings were: HOMA-IR, moderate; HbA1c, low; anthropometric outcomes, moderate; AMPK/mTOR, very low. Evidence for HbA1c was graded as low certainty based on four cohorts involving 684 participants, reflecting the time dependency of glycated haemoglobin change and the small sample size in Fu and colleagues study (n=18). 20 Evidence for anthropometric outcomes was graded as moderate certainty based on six cohorts involving 656 participants. The very low certainty for molecular outcomes reflects the single small-cohort evidence base. No subgroup analyses were conducted due to the degree of heterogeneity in eating window duration, timing, and co-intervention design across included studies. No sensitivity analyses were performed. The potential for publication bias was considered qualitatively. 4. Discussion

4.1. TRE as a Chronomedicine Strategy in LMICs

Evidence from 26 LMIC cohorts supports the potential of TRE as a clinically meaningful, biologically grounded, and practically implementable intervention for metabolic disease. Its core advantage over conventional dietary strategies is simplicity. Restructuring when food is consumed, rather than what or how much, removes the primary barriers that cause conventional prescriptions to fail: cost, complexity, and cultural incompatibility. The zeitgeber mechanism explains TRE’s population-level effectiveness. Urban eating patterns across included LMIC centres are characterised by late dinners, irregular meal timing, and extended overnight eating. These patterns chronically misalign peripheral clocks. A structured evening cut-off around 19:00 to 20:00 resets this daily. 43 It requires no synchrony with any specific cultural dietary tradition. This makes TRE adaptable across the food cultures of India, China, Indonesia, Iran, Tunisia, and Brazil without modification. The breadth of outcomes represented across the 26 cohorts: spanning glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, lipid profiles, pulmonary function, gut microbiome composition, and molecular energy sensors suggests that TRE produces systemic rather than isolated metabolic effects. This is consistent with the proposed mechanism of peripheral clock resynchronisation affecting multiple organ systems simultaneously.

4.2. The Glycemic Legacy Concept

In the Lucknow cohort, Rastogi and colleagues reported that HbA1c reductions appeared to deepen rather than plateau across 18 months of follow-up. 14 This pattern, if replicated, would distinguish TRE from most dietary interventions whose effects typically attenuate over time. The mechanism is likely behavioural as much as biological. The lipid profile findings of Rastogi and colleagues from the same Lucknow institution further support the compounding cardiometabolic benefit of sustained meal timing, demonstrating that TRE-associated improvements extend beyond glycemic markers to encompass the full atherogenic lipid panel. 16 As meal timing becomes habitual, the cognitive load of maintaining the intervention falls. Adherence becomes self-sustaining. 44 Peripheral clock resynchronisation becomes more deeply entrenched. The Guangzhou finding of equivalence between TRE and caloric restriction should not be read as failure. TRE achieves the same clinical outcome without calorie counting. In LMIC settings where dietary counselling infrastructure is scarce, this equivalence is a practical advantage.

4.3. Resolving the Visceral Fat Contradiction

The Xi’an versus Guangzhou contradiction on visceral fat reflects a methodological gap, not a biological one. Digital adherence monitoring produces stronger outcomes. Centres relying on self-reported logs underestimate true adherence and underestimate TRE’s effect. TRE’s metabolic effects appear dose-dependent on actual eating window compliance. The early versus late TRE comparison consistently favours morning-anchored windows. Cortisol, insulin sensitivity, and digestive enzyme activity peak in the morning. 45 Front-loading caloric intake amplifies the zeitgeber effect and optimises metabolic outcomes.

4.4. Molecular Switch: Timelines and Clinical Translation

The Surabaya molecular evidence, while preliminary, is mechanistically significant. Early AMPK recalibration and IGF-1 suppression within 20 days suggest that metabolic reprogramming begins rapidly. Clinicians should inform patients that dramatic early clinical change is unlikely but that biological adaptation has already begun. Sustained adherence over months is necessary to translate early molecular signals into lasting glycemic improvement. The potential advantage of consistent daily TRE over ADMF for AMPK stability further supports recommending a regular daily window rather than alternating protocols. 26

4.5. Limitations

Several limitations are acknowledged. First, self-reported dietary logs introduce recall and social desirability bias across most centres. Second, performance bias is unavoidable in all included studies. Third, concurrent pharmacological treatment, such as metformin, insulin, or GLP-1 receptor agonists, was inconsistently reported across included studies and could not be systematically extracted. This limits the ability to isolate TRE’s effect from background medical therapy. Fourth, Tunisia and Brazil are upper-middle-income countries by current World Bank classification. Their inclusion is justified by the LMIC burden-of-disease context and PRISMA protocol pre-registration, but sensitivity to this distinction is warranted. Fifth, molecular evidence is limited to one small cohort over 20 days. Sixth, sleep and actigraphy data are absent across all included studies, leaving the sleep-circadian-TRE interaction uncharacterised. Seventh, long-term adherence beyond 18 months remains unstudied. The 15% to 25% dropout observed in longitudinal cohorts may partly reflect declining adherence rather than attrition alone. Eighth, no grey literature was searched, and publication bias toward positive metabolic results cannot be excluded. The review process itself carries several limitations. Only three electronic databases were searched, and no grey literature, theses, or trial registries were consulted. This may mean relevant studies were missed. No formal sensitivity analysis was conducted. The reliance on self-reported methods to confirm LMIC setting classification means that borderline income-classification countries such as Tunisia and Brazil may introduce inconsistency in the evidence base.

4.6. Practical and Policy Implications

Clinicians in LMICs should consider recommending an 8 to 10-hour daily eating window with a consistent evening cut-off as a first-line adjunct for adults with T2D, Metabolic Syndrome, and related conditions, where clinically appropriate. TRE is not universally suitable. Caution is warranted in insulin-dependent patients at risk of hypoglycemia, in pregnancy, and in individuals with a history of disordered eating. The intervention is free, requires no prescription, and is adaptable to diverse cultural dietary context. For policymakers, public health messaging focused on meal timing rather than dietary composition represents a rare opportunity to address metabolic disease at population scale without infrastructure investment.

5. Conclusion

Time-Restricted Eating is a scalable, cost-free, and biologically grounded strategy for addressing the metabolic crisis across Low- and Middle-Income Countries. By acting as a zeitgeber that resynchronises peripheral biological clocks, TRE was associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity, fasting glucose, and body weight across diverse LMIC populations. It requires no dietary quality change, no supplements, and no financial expenditure. The Glycemic Legacy at 18 months suggests that benefits compound with sustained adherence, distinguishing TRE from interventions that plateau over time. Early molecular evidence suggests that the AMPK/mTOR switch engages within weeks of initiation. Whether this pathway is a clinically actionable target, through meal timing alone or combined with pharmacological AMPK modulators, remains speculative and requires dedicated mechanistic trials. In a setting where an estimated 101 million adults in India alone live with diabetes (International Diabetes Federation, 2023) and conventional dietary management consistently falls short, the timing of the meal is emerging as equally clinically significant as the meal itself.
