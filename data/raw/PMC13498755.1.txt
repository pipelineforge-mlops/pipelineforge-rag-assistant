ABSTRACT

Alcoholic ketoacidosis (AKA) is a metabolic disturbance triggered by excessive alcohol intake and fasting, typically presenting as a high‐anion‐gap metabolic acidosis with ketosis. We report a severe case of AKA with extreme hyperlactatemia, in which the patient developed shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation, emphasizing early recognition and timely intervention.

Key Clinical Message

AKA with concurrent profound hyperlactatemia and DIC necessitates aggressive multisystem support in critical illness.

1 Introduction

Alcoholic ketoacidosis (AKA) is defined by high anion gap metabolic acidosis and ketosis, which arise from excessive alcohol consumption and starvation. Alcohol can induce tissue hypoxia and elevate lactate production; its metabolism further contributes to lactate accumulation, resulting in potentially life‐threatening hyperlactatemia. We present a severe case of AKA accompanied by extreme hyperlactatemia; the patient's condition progressed to shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). This case illustrates the complex interactions among AKA, hyperlactatemia, and underlying alcoholic cirrhosis, while emphasizing the critical importance of early recognition and comprehensive multisystem support.

2 Case History/Examination

A 57‐year‐old man presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath for 5 h. He had consumed an unknown amount of alcohol 12 h prior. Five hours before his arrival, he experienced chest tightness and dyspnoea that worsened when lying flat, accompanied by four episodes of vomiting (gastric contents). Following the administration of intravenous fluids at a local clinic, his respiratory distress intensified, and he became moderately confused (Glasgow Coma Scale: E3V4M5), necessitating transfer to our emergency department. On examination, his heart rate was 90 beats per minute, respiratory rate was 31 breaths per minute, and blood pressure measured 131/71 mmHg. He remained confused, with pupils equal and reactive (3.0 mm), no conjunctival oedema, and mild bilateral pitting oedema of the lower extremities. Initial arterial blood gas analysis revealed a pH of 6.770, PaO2 of 159 mmHg, PaCO2 of 13.7 mmHg, lactate levels exceeding 20 mmol/L, base excess of −33.0 mmol/L, HCO3− of 2.0 mmol/L, glucose of 3.9 mmol/L, and an anion gap of 45.5 mmol/L. The concentration of β‐hydroxybutyrate (β‐OHB) was measured at 2128.50 μmol/L. A diagnosis of high‐anion‐gap metabolic acidosis with severe lactic acidosis and probable alcoholic ketoacidosis was established. His past medical history was significant for cerebral infarction, liver dysfunction, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, hyperglycaemia, alcoholic cirrhosis, and lower extremity venous thrombosis.

3 Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Based on heavy drinking, starvation, severe metabolic acidosis, and normal blood glucose, we considered alcoholic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, and a mixed high‐anion‐gap acidosis. DKA was unlikely without hyperglycaemia. Toxic alcohols were possible, but the presentation fit AKA with lactic acidosis best.

Aggressive fluid resuscitation was initiated with crystalloid at 15–20 mL/kg/h for the first hour, along with bicarbonate therapy guided by base deficit. A repeat blood gas showed a lactate level exceeding 20 mmol/L with persistent severe acidosis (Table 1), indicating a refractory state. Continuous renal replacement therapy was therefore commenced to help stabilize the internal milieu. Owing to worsening delirium, the patient was sedated, intubated, and mechanically ventilated (GCS E3VTM5 under sedation). His blood pressure dropped to 75/40 mmHg, prompting a continuous metaraminol infusion. To better characterize the haemodynamic profile, a pulmonary artery catheter was inserted and revealed a distributive shock pattern with high cardiac output and low systemic vascular resistance. Accordingly, the vasopressor was switched to a combination of norepinephrine and vasopressin, targeting a systolic pressure of 80–90 mmHg. With these measures, the patient's condition gradually stabilized, and he was transferred to the coronary care unit for ongoing monitoring.

Subsequent urinalysis was positive for ketones, confirming the diagnosis of alcoholic ketoacidosis. On hospital Day 2, the platelet count fell to 42.00 × 109/L and coagulation parameters became abnormal, raising concern for disseminated intravascular coagulation; plasma and cryoprecipitate were administered. The platelet count declined further to 25.00 × 109/L on Day 3 with worsening coagulopathy, so plasma and cryoprecipitate were continued. On Day 4, the platelet count rose slightly to 27.00 × 109/L and coagulation began to improve, at which point platelet transfusion was added. From Day 5 onward, platelet counts trended upward and coagulation tests normalized (Table 2). The patient was liberated from invasive ventilation on Day 8 and transitioned to non‐invasive support. By Day 14, his mental state had improved markedly (GCS E4V5M6) and cognitive function, assessed clinically, was documented as normal.

4 Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

After 16 days of intensive therapy including CRRT, respiratory support, transfusions, management of shock, and electrolytes, he was discharged much improved. Follow‐up labs were nearly normal.

5 Discussion

Alcoholic ketoacidosis is a major cause of severe metabolic acidosis [1]. Ethanol metabolism depletes NAD+, raising the NADH/NAD+ ratio, which inhibits gluconeogenesis and disrupts fatty acid oxidation. Pyruvate is converted to lactate to regenerate NAD+. Although binge drinking normally suppresses lipolysis, starvation overrides this effect, leading to excess ketone body production, especially beta‐hydroxybutyrate [2, 3]. Alcohol‐induced dehydration causes peripheral hypoperfusion, adding to lactate accumulation [4].

In this patient, high‐anion‐gap metabolic acidosis with extremely elevated lactate and beta‐hydroxybutyrate after heavy drinking confirmed alcoholic ketoacidosis [5, 6, 7]. Methanol or ethanol poisoning was excluded based on history—commercial alcohol consumption, asymptomatic companions—though assays were unavailable. A blood glucose of 3.9 mmol/L argued against diabetic ketoacidosis [8]. Underlying cirrhosis was both a predisposing and exacerbating factor, as reduced glycogen stores, impaired gluconeogenesis, and diminished lactate and ketone body clearance explained the severe metabolic disturbance [9, 10]. Alcoholic ketoacidosis must be suspected in alcoholic patients with severe acidosis and normoglycemia, and lactic acidosis with organ complications should be evaluated.

During management, hypotension and tachycardia prompted the use of an alpha‐adrenoceptor agonist. Pulmonary artery catheterisation, however, unequivocally demonstrated a distributive shock profile with high cardiac output and low systemic vascular resistance, leading to an immediate change in vasopressor strategy from metaraminol to combined norepinephrine and vasopressin [11]. This adjustment successfully averted the risks of pulmonary oedema and intra‐abdominal hypertension linked to aggressive fluid loading, and the patient's blood pressure stabilized at 80–90 mmHg. The case highlights the pitfall of relying solely on clinical signs in cirrhotic shock, where the baseline hyperdynamic circulation can obscure the true haemodynamic state; early invasive monitoring may thus prove decisive in preventing misjudgement and enabling precise therapy.

With respect to CRRT in lactic acidosis, its indications must be precisely defined. CRRT does not address the underlying cause, yet the present case posed a distinct challenge: cirrhotic impairment of hepatic lactate and ketone body clearance rendered conventional fluid resuscitation and bicarbonate therapy nearly futile (pH 6.844, lactate > 20 mmol/L). Under conditions of excessive production coupled with compromised clearance, CRRT served as a bridging intervention rather than a curative one, continuously removing acidic metabolites and inflammatory mediators to buy time for internal milieu stabilization [12]. We therefore suggest that, in patients with cirrhosis and acute kidney injury, the threshold for CRRT initiation could be judiciously lowered to create a therapeutic window for organ recovery—though this proposition warrants prospective validation.

The hospital course was further complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation. Pre‐existent thrombocytopenia from hypersplenism and cirrhosis, together with impaired coagulation factor synthesis, complicated the picture. Severe acidosis and shock triggered widespread endothelial injury and tissue factor release, activating the extrinsic coagulation pathway and leading to consumption of clotting factors and activation of fibrinolysis [13]. Management targeted the root insults by correcting acidosis with CRRT and optimizing tissue perfusion with vasoactive agents to limit ongoing endothelial damage, while dynamically monitoring coagulation indices and promptly transfusing plasma, cryoprecipitate, and platelets. By hospital Day 5, platelet counts had recovered and coagulation parameters normalized. Aggressive supportive care for complications such as coagulopathy is therefore essential. The prognosis of alcoholic ketoacidosis depends not only on the acidosis itself but also on the management of its precipitants and associated complications; even when initial laboratory derangements are extreme, favorable recovery can be achieved through comprehensive organ support.
