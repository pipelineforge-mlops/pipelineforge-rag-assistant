Transradial access (TRA) has emerged as a transformative approach in interventional cardiology and is increasingly being adopted in peripheral vascular interventions, including gynecological and obstetric procedures. Traditionally performed via transfemoral access (TFA), uterine artery embolization (UAE) and other pelvic interventions require prolonged bed rest and carry inherent access-site complications. This review synthesizes current evidence on the feasibility, safety, and clinical efficacy of TRA in interventional gynecology and obstetrics, covering UAE for fibroids, management of obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage, technical considerations, and future directions. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for studies published up to 2025. Meta-analyses demonstrate that TRA is a safe and effective alternative to TFA for UAE, with advantages including reduced radiation exposure (MD −207.54 mGy), shorter procedure times (MD −7.38 min), and higher likelihood of same-day discharge (RR 9.50). In obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage, cohort data support comparable efficacy with improved patient quality of life. A ten-year single-center experience reported a 97.35% angiographic success rate and a 7% mild complication rate. TRA offers particular advantages in obstetric patients, including immediate ambulation, reduced radiation exposure, and preserved fertility. However, the steeper learning curve, risk of radial artery occlusion, and limited long-term data remain challenges. Wider adoption requires structured training programs and further prospective studies. TRA represents a promising paradigm shift in interventional gynecology and obstetrics, offering comparable efficacy to TFA with potential advantages in radiation reduction, faster recovery, and improved patient comfort.

1 Introduction

Uterine fibroids, also known as leiomyomas, are the most common benign tumors of the female reproductive tract, with a cumulative incidence reaching 70–80% by the age of 50 years [1], [2]. Approximately 25–50% of women with fibroids experience symptoms including menorrhagia, pelvic pain, pressure symptoms, and infertility, leading to impaired quality of life and substantial healthcare costs [2]. Meanwhile, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) remains the leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide, accounting for 25–30% of all maternal deaths [3], [4].

Uterine artery embolization (UAE) has become a well-established, minimally invasive treatment for symptomatic uterine fibroids and is increasingly used in the management of obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage [5]. Traditionally performed through transfemoral access (TFA), UAE requires patients to remain supine with prolonged bed rest post-procedure to prevent access-site complications such as hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, and retroperitoneal bleeding. While this approach is technically mature, prolonged immobilization causes considerable inconvenience to patients, particularly in obstetric patients who need to care for their newborns immediately after delivery.

Transradial access (TRA) has emerged as a transformative strategy in interventional cardiology and is now considered the preferred access route for many coronary procedures due to its advantages in faster post-procedural recovery, reduced vascular access-site complications, and greater patient preference [6]. In recent years, this paradigm has extended to peripheral vascular interventions, including visceral artery embolization [7]. A growing body of evidence supports the feasibility and safety of TRA in uterine artery embolization. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that TRA offers advantages including reduced radiation exposure, shorter procedure times, fewer access-site complications, and faster recovery compared with TFA [5], [6]. Randomized controlled trials have further shown that TRA may achieve a higher success rate in bilateral catheterization with lower radiation doses and shorter procedural times [8]. In the setting of obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage, cohort studies have confirmed comparable efficacy and safety between TRA and TFA, with improved patient quality of life in the TRA group [9].

Despite these advantages, the adoption of TRA in interventional gynecology and obstetrics has been comparatively slower than in cardiology, and the technique remains underutilized [7]. This review aims to synthesize the current evidence on TRA in interventional gynecology and obstetrics, covering uterine artery embolization for fibroids, management of obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage, technical considerations, and future directions, providing a comprehensive overview for interventional radiologists, vascular surgeons, and obstetricians considering the adoption of this patient-centered approach.

2 Technical considerations and learning curve

2.1 Anatomical and technical fundamentals

Transradial access involves puncturing the radial artery at the wrist (typically the right radial artery) using a dedicated radial artery puncture needle and a short hydrophilic sheath. The procedural workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. Key technical considerations include：● Pre-procedural assessment: The Allen test or Barbeau test is traditionally recommended to assess collateral circulation via the ulnar artery, though its routine use has been debated in contemporary practice [10].

● Radial artery puncture: A 21-gauge micropuncture needle is used, followed by insertion of a 4–5 French hydrophilic sheath [11].

● Vasoactive cocktail: A combination of heparin, nitroglycerin, and verapamil is typically administered intra-arterially to prevent radial artery spasm [12].

● Catheter selection: Judkins right or Simmons catheters are commonly used for catheterization of the uterine arteries from a radial approach, depending on operator preference [13].

● Post-procedural management: Hemostasis is achieved with a radial compression device, allowing immediate ambulation [14].

2.2 Learning curve and complications

Compared with TFA, the main disadvantage of TRA is a steeper learning curve. Operators accustomed to the femoral approach must adapt to different catheter handling, aortic arch navigation, and bilateral uterine artery catheterization techniques [15], [16]. Procedural failure rates can be as high as 10–16% during the first 30–40 cases, though most operators gain confidence after the first 100–200 cases with appropriate training [17]. An international survey identified "long learning curve" (45%) and "lack of training" (32%) as the most frequent detractors to TRA adoption among interventional radiologists [18]. Nevertheless, with structured training programs and adequate case volume, the learning curve can be successfully overcome [19], [20].

Radial artery occlusion (RAO) is the most common complication specific to TRA, followed by radial artery spasm, perforation, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, and nerve injury [21], [22]. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 41 studies including 30,020 patients reported an overall RAO incidence of 13% (95% CI: 0.09–0.16), with early RAO (within 24 h) occurring in 14% and late RAO (after 24 h) in 10% of cases [23]. Another prospective study of 427 patients reported an RAO incidence of 11.24% [24]. In the peripheral vascular intervention setting, a 2024 meta-analysis reported that TRA was associated with a higher procedural failure rate (3.9% vs 1.0%) but fewer total access site complications and less minor bleeding compared with TFA, albeit with more frequent access vessel occlusion (1.9% vs <0.1%) [25]. Radial artery spasm is another common complication, affecting approximately 5% of cases, with severe spasm occurring in about 2.7% [15]. Radial artery perforation occurs in less than 1% of procedures [26], while pseudoaneurysm is rare, occurring in 0.009–0.6% of cases [27], [28]. Nerve injury, though uncommon, has been reported and may lead to persistent neurologic deficits [22], [29].

Use of smaller sheaths (4 French versus 5 French) and adequate heparinization can significantly reduce RAO rates [23], [30], [31]. The main modifiable predictors of RAO include insufficient heparinization and occlusive hemostasis [24]. Preventive strategies should focus primarily on these factors to reduce the risk of RAO [24], [30]. Additionally, the use of patent hemostasis (maintaining radial artery patency during compression) and shorter compression times have been shown to reduce RAO incidence [23], [32].

3 Clinical applications in uterine artery embolization

3.1 Evidence from meta-analyses

Two systematic reviews and meta-analyses have quantitatively synthesized the comparative data between TRA and TFA in UAE. The first, conducted by Sami et al. (2025), included seven studies with 710 patients and found comparable procedural and fluoroscopy times between the two approaches, with a significant reduction in radiation exposure only on sensitivity analysis. No significant differences in major access-site complications were observed, though a trend favored TRA for fewer minor complications [6]. The second, a more recent meta-analysis by Alhebshi et al. (2026) encompassing six studies and 639 patients, reported more pronounced advantages for TRA, including significantly lower radiation exposure (MD −207.54 mGy), shorter procedure time (MD −7.38 min), a 9.5-fold higher likelihood of same-day discharge, and fewer access-site complications, particularly hematomas [5].

Although both meta-analyses concluded that TRA is a safe and effective alternative to TFA, the magnitude of the reported benefits differs notably between them. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study inclusion, operator experience, and variation in the definitions of procedural success and complications across the primary studies [5], [6]. Critically, both meta-analyses were limited by the small number of included studies (six and seven, respectively), the predominance of retrospective or non-randomized designs, and considerable heterogeneity in outcome definitions across the primary studies. Neither analysis included a formal assessment of publication bias due to the limited number of studies, leaving the possibility of selective reporting of favorable outcomes. Larger, well-designed prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and to establish standardized definitions of key outcomes to facilitate future meta-analyses.

Synthesis of evidence from meta-analyses: Although both meta-analyses concluded that TRA is a safe and effective alternative to TFA, the magnitude of the reported benefits differs notably between them. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study inclusion criteria, operator experience, and variation in the definitions of procedural success and complications across the primary studies [5], [6]. A common finding across both analyses is the consistent trend toward reduced radiation exposure and fewer minor access-site complications with TRA, though the effect sizes vary [5], [6]. However, neither meta-analysis demonstrated a significant difference in major complication rates, suggesting that the safety profiles of the two approaches are comparable. Critically, both analyses are limited by the small number of included studies and the predominance of retrospective or non-randomized designs, highlighting the need for larger prospective studies to confirm these findings.

3.2 Evidence from randomized controlled trials

To date, only one randomized controlled trial has directly compared TRA with TFA in UAE. Bessar et al. (2025) randomized 42 patients with symptomatic fibroids and reported technical outcomes favoring TRA, including a higher success rate in achieving bilateral catheterization [8].

Although this RCT provides the highest level of evidence currently available in this field, its findings should be interpreted with caution. The small sample size (n = 42) limits the statistical power to detect differences in less common outcomes, particularly major access-site complications. The open-label design (non-blinded) introduces potential performance and detection bias. The single-center design limits generalizability to other settings, and the short-term follow-up does not capture late complications or long-term clinical outcomes. Larger, adequately powered, multi-center RCTs with blinded outcome assessment and longer follow-up are urgently needed to confirm the findings suggested by this preliminary study.

Synthesis of evidence from RCTs and comparative studies: The single published RCT by Bessar et al. provides the highest level of evidence currently available, but its small sample size limits its conclusiveness [8]. Across the available comparative studies (including both RCTs and cohort studies), a consistent pattern emerges: TRA is associated with comparable or superior technical outcomes, particularly in achieving bilateral catheterization, with lower radiation exposure and faster recovery [5], [6], [8]. However, discrepancies exist regarding the magnitude of these benefits, which likely reflect differences in operator experience, patient selection, and outcome definitions across studies. Notably, all comparative studies to date have been conducted in elective UAE settings for fibroids; the comparative effectiveness of TRA versus TFA in emergent obstetric hemorrhage remains largely unknown. This represents a critical evidence gap that warrants future investigation.

3.3 Real-world data and long-term experience

Long-term real-world data further support the safety and efficacy of TRA. Jiménez-Rámila et al. (2025) reported a ten-year single-center retrospective study (2012–2022) involving 189 planned procedures. The study demonstrated a 97.35% angiographic success rate (184/189 procedures), with 85.4% of patients achieving complete angiographic success and avoiding surgery at 24 months. The complication rate was only 7% (13/185), all of which were mild. At 6 months, significant reductions were observed in uterine volume (−146.24 mL, 95% CI −115.41 to −177.06; p < 0.001) and largest fibroid volume (−53.98 mL, 95% CI −42.79 to −65.17; p < 0.001), along with significant improvements in hemoglobin levels (from 11.4 to 12.85 g/dL; p < 0.001) and quality of life scores. The authors concluded that the transradial approach should be adopted as a safe and effective alternative for the treatment of symptomatic uterine fibroids [33].

Studies on the distal transradial approach have also shown promising results. A multicenter study of 47 patients undergoing various noncoronary interventions reported a technical success rate of 97.9% without major complications, with only one minor complication (2.2%), a thrombotic segmental occlusion of the distal radial artery [34]. In the context of uterine artery embolization specifically, the distal radial artery approach has been shown to be safe and feasible, with potential to replace femoral access in the absence of contraindications [35].

Himiniuc et al. (2021), in a systematic review of ten studies published in Medicina, concluded that TRA is as safe and efficient as TFA for UAE, with advantages including fewer complications, shorter hospitalization, and rapid mobilization, though it has a steeper learning curve [7].

4 Applications in obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage

4.1 Evidence from cohort studies

Ma et al. (2023) conducted a single-center cohort study comparing TRA (n = 31) with TFA (n = 40) in patients undergoing transarterial embolization for obstetric and gynecological bleeding. The TRA group demonstrated significantly reduced radiation exposure (343.89 ± 108.81 mGy vs. 469.29 ± 198.66 mGy; p = 0.029) and improved quality of life scores on the day of discharge (EQ−5D index: 0.72 ± 0.12 vs. 0.65 ± 0.11; p = 0.017) [9].

While this study provides the only direct comparative data on TRA in the obstetric hemorrhage setting, it has several important limitations. The relatively small sample size (71 patients) and the imbalance between the two groups (31 vs. 40) limit the robustness of the comparisons. As a single-center, non-randomized study, the findings are susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Additionally, the short follow-up period (30 days) does not capture long-term outcomes, such as the durability of embolization effects or delayed access-site complications. Importantly, the study excluded patients with hemodynamic instability, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the most critical obstetric emergencies. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility and patient-centered benefits of TRA in this setting, while underscoring the need for larger, multi-center studies with standardized outcome measures.

Synthesis of evidence in obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage: The available evidence on TRA in the emergent setting is considerably more limited than in elective UAE. Beyond the single cohort study by Ma et al. that directly compared TRA with TFA in obstetric and gynecological bleeding [9], the remainder of the literature consists of case reports and small case series. A common finding across these reports is the feasibility of TRA in achieving hemostasis when performed by experienced operators. However, substantial discrepancies exist in patient selection, timing of intervention, and outcome reporting, making direct comparisons challenging. Furthermore, studies that reported negative outcomes or procedural failures are likely underrepresented due to publication bias. Importantly, the applicability of TRA to hemodynamically unstable patients, the subgroup in which rapid access is most critical, has not been adequately evaluated, as most studies excluded these patients [9]. This evidence gap underscores the need for dedicated studies in acute obstetric settings before TRA can be recommended as a routine alternative to TFA in emergencies.

4.2 Applications in specific conditions

Transradial UAE has been successfully applied in the treatment of various obstetric and gynecological hemorrhagic conditions. Transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE), especially uterine artery embolization (UAE), is widely used in the treatment of uterine fibroids, postpartum hemorrhage, scar pregnancy, and pelvic trauma due to its advantages of minimal trauma, rapid hemostasis, and precise localization [9]. In postpartum hemorrhage, selective uterine artery embolization can control bleeding in over 90% of women with severe postpartum hemorrhage [36]. In cesarean scar pregnancy, transarterial embolization via the radial artery has been used to manage ectopic pregnancies in cesarean section scars [9], [37]. In addition, this approach is applicable to embolization of pelvic arterial injuries and management of hypervascular retained products of conception [9], [38].

In obstetric patients, TRA offers particular patient-centered advantages: patients can sit up and move freely immediately after the procedure, which is especially valuable for postpartum patients who need to care for their newborns; reduced radiation exposure is particularly important for women of reproductive age; patients can adopt comfortable positions during and after the procedure; and embolization techniques preserve future fertility [9], [39], [40].

However, the evidence base for many of these applications remains limited. For cesarean scar pregnancy and hypervascular retained products of conception, the literature is confined to case reports and small case series with limited follow-up [9], [37], [38]. The reported outcomes are heterogeneous, and the lack of standardized reporting makes it difficult to aggregate evidence. For postpartum hemorrhage, while the success rates are high, the evidence is derived mainly from studies where the majority of procedures were performed via femoral access; the specific role of TRA in this setting requires further evaluation. Notably, all existing studies in this area are retrospective, single-center series, leaving important questions about patient selection, procedural timing, and long-term reproductive outcomes unanswered [9], [36].

4.3 Fertility considerations and patient counseling

A critical consideration when offering UAE to reproductive-age women with symptomatic fibroids is its potential impact on future fertility. Several meta-analyses have now provided evidence on this important question. A 2024 meta-analysis of four randomized trials found no difference in live birth and miscarriage rates between UAE and myomectomy [41]. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 33 studies including 4287 women reported a pregnancy rate of 52.1% after UAE, with higher rates in younger women (67.8% in those <30 years) compared with older patients (31.5% in those >40 years) [42]. Another systematic review reported a pregnancy rate of 40.5% (95% CI 33.3–48.2%) in women desiring pregnancy after UAE [43].

Regarding ovarian reserve, available evidence suggests that UAE does not significantly affect serum levels of reproductive hormones, including FSH, LH, estradiol, and anti-Müllerian hormone, nor does it impair ovarian reserve indicators such as antral follicle count [44]. However, concerns regarding compromised uterine perfusion and endometrial receptivity after UAE have been raised in the literature.

For patients desiring future pregnancy, myomectomy or other fertility-sparing options should generally be preferred when surgically feasible, as UAE may be associated with a lower pregnancy rate compared with myomectomy in some studies [45] and an increased risk of postpartum hemorrhage in subsequent pregnancies [46]. However, the most recent meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials have shown no difference in live birth or miscarriage rates between UAE and myomectomy, suggesting that UAE can be a reasonable option in appropriately selected patients [41]. The decision should be individualized through shared decision-making, incorporating patient age, ovarian reserve, reproductive goals, fibroid characteristics, and the relative risks and benefits of each treatment option. Patients should be counseled that the evidence base, while growing, still has limitations, and that fertility outcomes depend on multiple patient-specific factors [42].

6 Challenges and limitations

Despite the significant advantages of TRA in interventional gynecology and obstetrics, a number of technical challenges and evidence limitations remain. At the technical level, radial artery spasm (RAS) is one of the most common difficulties encountered during the procedure, with reported incidences varying up to 51.3% depending on the patient population and definition used [47]. Key risk factors include female sex, advanced age, small body size, emergency procedures, and the use of multiple catheters [47]. Preventive measures include distal radial access, hydrophilic-coated sheaths, and appropriate catheter sizes, while treatment involves intra-arterial nitroglycerin and verapamil, as well as mechanical methods such as balloon-assisted tracking [47]. Radial artery anatomical variations, such as high radial origin, extreme tortuosity, and complete radioulnar loops, are present in approximately 14.8% of patients and may increase procedural difficulty [24]. Achieving bilateral uterine artery catheterization from a radial approach may be more challenging than from a femoral approach, although some studies report higher success rates with TRA [8]. In addition, the steeper learning curve may limit wider adoption in the early stages, with one systematic review noting that while TRA offers advantages including fewer complications and shorter hospitalization, it carries the disadvantage of a longer learning curve compared to TFA [7].

At the evidence level, most current studies are relatively small, single-center series, lacking large-scale multicenter prospective studies. The heterogeneity in outcome definitions and complication reporting across studies limits the generalizability of findings [6], [47]. Moreover, most evidence is concentrated in uterine fibroid embolization, and data on specific obstetric applications remain limited [7].

While the majority of published evidence on TRA in gynecologic interventions derives from elective uterine artery embolization for fibroids, the role of this approach in obstetric emergencies, such as postpartum hemorrhage, ectopic pregnancy rupture, or pelvic trauma, remains less defined. In emergency settings, time to intervention is critical, and the learning curve for TRA may pose a barrier to its widespread adoption. Furthermore, hemodynamic instability may complicate radial artery cannulation and vasospasm management. However, for hemodynamically stable patients with anticipated complex pelvic arterial anatomy, TRA may offer an advantage in accessing certain arterial targets. Currently, evidence is limited to case reports and small series; further studies are needed to define the safety and feasibility of TRA in acute obstetric scenarios [9].

A current limitation of TRA is the incompatibility with large-bore devices and the limited availability of dedicated, lower-profile, long devices for more distal vascular territories [13]. The assessment should be performed on an individual basis, tailoring the access site to the specific patient population to achieve the best risk-to-benefit ratio [13].

6.1 Patient selection and access route decision

Not all patients are suitable candidates for TRA, and appropriate patient selection is essential for optimizing outcomes and minimizing procedural failure. Ideal candidates for TRA include patients with favorable radial artery anatomy (palmar arch collateral circulation confirmed by pulse oximetry or Barbeau test, adequate vessel caliber), those undergoing elective UAE for fibroids where early ambulation is desired, and patients with aortoiliac occlusive disease, groin infection, or morbid obesity in whom femoral access may be challenging [13]. Additionally, TRA offers particular advantages in obstetric patients who need to care for their newborns immediately after delivery [48].

Conversely, transfemoral access (TFA) may remain preferable in several clinical situations. In emergency settings, such as postpartum hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability, the rapid access and familiarity of TFA may outweigh the potential benefits of TRA. Patients with small radial artery diameter (<2.0 mm), previous radial artery intervention or occlusion, Raynaud's phenomenon, or severe peripheral vascular disease are generally not ideal candidates for TRA [13]. When large-bore access (>6 French) is required, for example when embolization requires larger catheters or when concomitant procedures are anticipated, TRA may be technically challenging due to the smaller caliber of the radial artery [13]. Furthermore, in centers without adequate operator experience with TRA, TFA remains the default approach to minimize procedural risks during the learning curve phase [7].

It is also important to recognize that the suitability of TRA may vary depending on the specific clinical context. For instance, while TRA has demonstrated feasibility in elective UAE, its role in acute obstetric hemorrhage remains less well defined, and the decision to use TRA in such scenarios should be made cautiously, taking into account the operator’s experience and the patient’s hemodynamic status. The decision between TRA and TFA should be individualized, taking into account patient anatomy, clinical urgency, operator experience, and available institutional resources. A balanced, patient-centered approach that considers both the advantages of TRA and the specific clinical context is essential to achieve the best risk-to-benefit ratio [13].

7 Future directions

Looking ahead, the development of TRA in interventional gynecology and obstetrics can be approached from three levels: technology, research, and applications.

At the technological level, the distal transradial approach (snuffbox approach) is emerging as an alternative to conventional TRA, with studies reporting technical success rates exceeding 93% and favorable safety profiles [34], [35]. Ultrasound-guided puncture may improve first-pass success rates and reduce complications [49], [50]. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated a strong trend toward improved first-pass success with ultrasound guidance (RR 1.29; 95% CI 1.00–1.66; p = 0.05) [49]. while a comparative study reported significantly higher first-pass success rates with ultrasound-guided dynamic needle tip positioning (88.5% vs 46.2%; p < 0.001) [50]. Hydrophilic-coated sheaths have been shown to reduce radial artery spasm (RR 0.38; 95% CI 0.23–0.63) and peri-procedural pain [11]. These technological innovations collectively contribute to improving the safety, feasibility, and patient experience of TRA in gynecological and obstetric interventions.

In clinical research, future efforts should focus on large multicenter randomized controlled trials to confirm the advantages of TRA in gynecological interventions [8], long-term follow-up studies to assess the durability of TRA outcomes and late complications, cost-effectiveness analyses to evaluate the economic impact of TRA adoption [51], and further research on patient-reported outcomes including patient satisfaction and quality of life. Establishing standardized training programs and competency criteria is also critical to promoting wider adoption of TRA [52]. A study on learning curves for transradial and ultrasound-guided arterial access found that 25 punctures are required to attain competency in ultrasound-guided radial access, and 50 punctures appear adequate for proficiency in a standard transradial approach, providing useful benchmarks for incorporating TRA into training programs for interventionalists [52].

In terms of expanding applications, beyond UAE and hemorrhage control, several emerging indications have been proposed, though the evidence base for these applications is considerably more limited. These include embolization of ovarian and pelvic veins for pelvic congestion syndrome [53], embolization of uterine arteriovenous malformations [54], intra-arterial chemotherapy infusion for gynecological malignancies [55], and prophylactic balloon occlusion or embolization for placenta accreta spectrum disorders [56]. For pelvic congestion syndrome, transcatheter venous embolization has been reported as an effective management option, and while most procedures are currently performed via femoral or jugular venous access, the principles of upper extremity access may be adaptable to these procedures [53]. However, the literature on TRA specifically for pelvic venous embolization remains limited to case reports and small series. Uterine arteriovenous malformations, though rare, can cause life-threatening hemorrhage and are increasingly managed with endovascular embolization [54]. The role of TRA in this setting has not been systematically evaluated, and current evidence is derived from anecdotal reports rather than comparative studies. Intra-arterial chemotherapy has been investigated for locally advanced cervical cancer, and TRA has been employed for intra-arterial infusion in other tumor types, suggesting potential applicability in gynecological oncology [55]. However, this remains a preliminary area of investigation, and no dedicated studies have evaluated TRA specifically for intra-arterial chemotherapy in gynecological malignancies. Prophylactic balloon occlusion of the internal iliac arteries has been reported to reduce intraoperative blood loss in patients with placenta accreta spectrum disorders [56]. The transradial approach may theoretically offer advantages in terms of patient comfort and early mobilization in this obstetric population, but data supporting its use in this specific context are currently lacking. Further research is needed before TRA can be recommended for these emerging indications.

8 Conclusion

Transradial access represents a significant paradigm shift in interventional gynecology and obstetrics. Current evidence from systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, and large cohort studies consistently demonstrates that TRA is a safe and effective alternative to traditional transfemoral access for uterine artery embolization and management of obstetric and gynecological hemorrhage [5], [6], [8], [9]. The advantages of TRA, including reduced radiation exposure, shorter procedure times, faster recovery, fewer access-site complications, and improved patient comfort, align with the principles of patient-centered care and value-based medicine [5], [33], [39]. Ten-year real-world experience further supports the safety and efficacy of TRA, with a 97.35% angiographic success rate and a 7% mild complication rate [33]. However, the steeper learning curve, potential for radial artery occlusion, and limited long-term data remain challenges that must be addressed through structured training programs and further prospective research [6], [7], [23]. As the body of evidence continues to grow, TRA is likely to become increasingly adopted as the preferred access route for interventional gynecological and obstetric procedures, offering patients a less invasive, more comfortable, and faster-recovery alternative to traditional approaches [5], [6], [39].
