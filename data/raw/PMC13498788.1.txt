Background

Interprofessional communication between midwives and physicians is essential for the successful management of obstetrical cases, including emergencies. The Situation-Background-Assessment- Recommendation (SBAR) tool is an established communication model that facilitates communication among medical teams.

Aim

This cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate midwifery students’ attitudes toward interprofessional communication and determine whether a course on the SBAR communication tool could enhance their self-assessed competence.

Methods

We conducted a course for midwifery students in their final year of study, focusing on training interprofessional communication. The course included a lecture on SBAR, followed by a role-play exercise simulating communication between a midwife and a physician in obstetrical emergencies. Students were assessed using an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE). They were invited to complete a two-part questionnaire before and after the intervention, evaluating their attitudes toward interprofessional communication and the use of SBAR in their clinical routine.

Findings

The assessed midwifery students (n=20) demonstrated positive attitudes towards the application of SBAR. The students rated their knowledge of SBAR as one grade higher after completing the course. After the intervention, 95% of the participants stated that they intended to use SBAR in the future. The role-play was evaluated as important for their learning approach on SBAR by the students. The major barriers to interprofessional communication identified by the questionnaire included a lack of clarification of responsibilities, work overload, and different views on medical assessments within the interprofessional team. The quality of interprofessional communication was evaluated by the midwifery students as balanced and positive, but they noted a lower application of SBAR by physicians from the students’ perspective.

Conclusion

We conclude that SBAR is important for midwifery students in interprofessional communication with physicians. SBAR training for midwifery students may improve their interprofessional communication skills. A role-play on SBAR may improve the learning success.

Introduction

Obstetrical emergencies are often unexpected and require interdisciplinary management by midwives and obstetricians to resolve problems quickly. 1 Poor communication in obstetrical teams can cause adverse maternal and fetal events and outcomes.2,3 Human factors, especially inadequate communication, have been identified by the World Health Organization as a risk to patient safety. 4 Structured handovers such as the Situation-Background-Assessment-Recommendation (SBAR) scheme should facilitate safe communication and can lead to an improved collaboration in interprofessional teams.5,6

The SBAR scheme, originally developed in a military context to facilitate communication for submarines, is an established scheme for precise and simplified communication.7-9 SBAR is also part of the national German anesthesiological guidelines for perioperative handover and is good clinical practice in intensive care unit teams.6,10 Training SBAR focused originally on phone calls by nurses and its training is part of national teaching strategies for nurses by the National Health service. 11 Teaching courses on SBAR have shown positive effects on precise and concise communication, which can reduce communication failures.7,8,12,13

The current use of the SBAR tool within the obstetrical setting is informal and guideline recommendations are still missing, although the application of SBAR might reduce failures in interprofessional communication. 3 The use of SBAR is mostly restricted to the obstetrical anesthesia, where it supports communication within the anesthesiological team. 1 SBAR training has become the focus in midwifery education over the past few years to foster a more standardized communication through different professions and dicsipilines. 14 It is also part of the didactical development for midwifery students in Germany. 15 Only few studies have investigated SBAR training for obstetrical teams.16,17 They showed positive trends on the application of SBAR, but investigated either mixed cohorts including midwives, residents and students or focused interaction between nurses and physicians in an obstetrical unit.16,17

Given the interprofessional team of midwives and obstetricians with its distinctive features we performed a cross-sectional study on midwifery students teaching SBAR in interprofessional communication on this topic. The course was tailored to the obstetrical context and used a role-play simulating communication between physicians and midwives. The students were confronted with communication in obstetrical emergencies, since these situations are often interface of daily interprofessional collaboration. The students’ oral performance of the application of SBAR was evaluated by an objective structured assessment of skills (OSCE). The course was accompanied by a questionnaire to evaluate the midwifery students’ attitudes toward interprofessional communication and their self-assessed knowledge on SBAR.

Material and Methods

Setting

We performed this cross-sectional study in midwifery students on 25th of June 2025 at the Department of Midwifery, University of Applied Sciences, Saarbrücken, Germany.

The authors asked the local Institutional Review Board [(IRB; Ethikkommission der Ärztekammer des Saarlandes, Saarbrücken, Germany)] prior to starting the project. The IRB waived the need for official bioethical approval and registered the project. (Identification-Code: 77/25). The reporting adheres to the Checklist of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE statement on cross-sectional studies) (Supplementary 1 STROBE checklist).

Participants

Twenty undergraduate midwifery students in their last year before examination were enrolled in this study. The cohort size represents a complete semester at the investigating department. Participants were only included if written informed consent was obtained and the participant performed at least one role-play applying SBAR. Participants’ data were excluded if the data were incomplete. The participation of this study was voluntary and the course and their evaluation were free from academical grades. Informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to the start of the course.

Methods

Midwifery students participated in a six-hour training course on interprofessional communication between physicians and midwives. The course started with a 30- minute lecture on SBAR by an obstetrician and an experienced midwife. The lecture was tailored for the application of SBAR in the obstetrical setting and focused on the items of the OSCE.

After the lecture, students participated in a role-play either as a physician or midwife training communication within obstetrical emergencies, that is, preeclampsia/HELLP syndrome, shoulder dystocia, and peripartum hemorrhage. The student in the midwife role had to inform the physician about the emergency and the student in the physician role had to answer the call; both had to apply SBAR for managing the case.

For practical reasons, to enable multiple role-plays and smaller groups for discussion, the students were random assigned to two groups either lead by the midwife or the obstetrician. The students were given the opportunity to act in both roles, if they wanted to, but had to perform at least one role-play applying the SBAR tool. This approach was chosen to enable another perspective on interprofessional communication and to reflect the other side. The students’ performance was assessed by teachers (an obstetrician or an experienced midwife) using an OSCE on the application of SBAR and received direct feedback from the teachers on performing communication using SBAR.

OSCE

The OSCE on SBAR was adapted to obstetrical situations and refers to the German guidelines of the German Society for Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (DGAI).6,18,19 The OSCE assessed 11 items. Each item was scored with one credit, if it was performed correctly [Situation: (1.) Identification of the patient, (2.) location, (3.) description of the situation; Background: (4.) age, (5.) Gravida/Para, (6.) pregnancy week and stage of labour, (7.) abnormalities during pregnancy and labour; Assessment: (8.) stating the diagnosis, (9.) previous results, (10.) further examination; Recommendation: (11.) expression of the recommendation].

Additional to the items of the OSCE, the expertise for solving the emergency cases and professionalism were evaluated using a numeric rating scale (range 0: worst result- 10: best result) as separate items. The level of expertise was tailored to the midwifery students’ situation. The OSCE was applied by the obstetrician or the midwife to evaluate the oral performance of the students during the role-play. For the final analysis, the students’ first result of the OSCE was incorporated for final analysis, in order to avoid any bias caused by habituation effects.

Questionnaire

The students were invited to complete a questionnaire. The questionnaire was non-standardized and was developed for this project (Supplementary 2 questionnaire). The first part of the questionnaire was filled in after the lecture on SBAR and evaluated the students’ attitudes on interprofessional communication and their knowledge, routines, and opinions about SBAR prior to the intervention. The second part of the questionnaire has been completed at the end of the course after the intervention and evaluated students’ self-assessed knowledge about SBAR. At the end, the students rated the course and they had the opportunity to state comments on the course in the required field of the questionnaire.

Statistical Analysis

We systematically entered all the collected data into Microsoft Excel (Version 16.103.4). In accordance with the German standards, questions assessed using ordinal scales were presented in an inverted Likert scale format, where 1 represented the most favorable option and 5 the least favorable. The data were then transformed into an ordinal Likert scale.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi, Version 2.3.21.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all quantitative variables, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for ordinal or continuous parameters. For comparisons of quantitative variables between different groups (such as different OSCE evaluators, physician and midwife), we used the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The results of all 20 students were included in the final analysis. Data for the questionnaire were complete and every student performed at least one role-play exercise applying SBAR.

The students rated qualitative aspects of the interprofessional communication between midwives and doctors on an ordinal scale (1-10). The communication was rated as more likely to be objective (8.0), cooperative (8.0) and inclusive (7.5) than emotional, distanced or confrontational. Investigating the style of the daily communication between midwives and doctors, six students (30%) rated that the daily communication is coordinated by the midwife. In contrast, seven (35 %) found the daily communication coordinated by the physician and another seven (35%) found it neither nor. For the midwifery students, major communication barriers were differences in the assessment of the medical facts by midwives and physicians (n=12, 60%), work overload (n=12, 60%) followed by a lack of clarification of responsibilities within the team (n=10, 50%) (Table 1).

Prior to the intervention, most students rated their knowledge of SBAR as sufficient (n=12, 60%). The assessed students partly agreed (n=8, 40%) to use SBAR in their clinical routine. All students (n=20) found the SBAR tool helpful (very true/true) for interprofessional communication and agreed (n=20, 100%) with the statement that a course on this topic would be helpful (very true/true). The physicians’ use of SBAR was rated by four (20%) students with very true or true. Eight midwifery students (40%) consented that physicians use SBAR partly (n=8, 40%), and the other eight (40%) rated this statement as less true or not at all true (Table 2).

After the intervention, 18 (90%) participants rated their knowledge about SBAR as very good or good. The majority (n=19; 95%) of the students agreed to use SBAR in the interprofessional communication in the future (very true or true). Seventy percent of students (n=14, very true) agreed that the course prepared them well for the SBAR application, and 75% (n=15 (very true)) found the role-play helpful for practicing. The course was rated as very good by 65% of participants (n=13) and by 35% (n=7) as good (Table 3).

The OSCE results demonstrated that the participants attained a median overall score of 11, with an IQR of 9–11 for the doctor role and with an IQR of 10–11 for the midwife role. Regarding the expertise, the median score was 9 (role: midwife IQR 7–10; role: doctor 7–9). For the professionalism domain, the median score was 9.5 for both roles, with an IQR of 7–10 (Table 5). With respect to the students’ roles during the intervention, our analysis indicated that there were no statistically significant differences in any of the scores based on whether the student assumed the role of physician or midwife within the scenarios (Table 5).

Analysis of the scoring outcomes revealed significant differences between the assessors. Specifically, when comparing evaluations conducted by the obstetrician to those by the midwife, the OSCE scores were notably lower in the physician group (median 10 versus 11; Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.0022). Similarly, scores for expertise (median 7 versus 10; Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.0004) and professionalism (median 7.5 versus 10; Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.0021) were significantly lower when assessed by the physician than by the midwife.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study in midwifery students demonstrated positive effects of the SBAR training on the self-assessed knowledge and attitudes to apply SBAR in clinical routine. The course achieved good and very good ratings through its design and its interprofessional approach. The structure including the role-play featuring an emergency communication between midwife and obstetrician was evaluated helpful for their learning. The positive effect of SBAR training is also reflected by the results of OSCE, in which most students scored nearly a perfect score; of note interobserver variability between obstetrician and midwife rating was significant.

Interprofessional communication is crucial in handling obstetrical emergencies and has become the focus of teaching in midwives and midwifery students.3,14,15 A lack of team communication can lead to fetal and maternal morbidity. 3 SBAR is suggested as an option to optimize communication within obstetrical teams, originally located within obstetrical anesthesia.1,3

The quality of interprofessional communication between midwifes and obstetricians was evaluated mainly objective, cooperative and inclusive by the investigated midwifery students, reflecting positive attitudes. An Interview study by Janssens et al revealed agreement towards a co-leadership in an obstetrical team including midwives and obstetricians. 20 In contrast, Romijn et al conducted a cross-sectional study of 379 obstetricians, clinical midwives, primary care midwives, and nurses on interprofessional collaboration using an online survey. 21 Obstetricians rated their relationships with other teams more positively than vice versa. Obstetricians felt more isolated from clinical midwives and primary care midwives, a sentiment reflected in lower ratings; this suggests that there was less exchange of views and mutual respect between the groups. 21 Our data suggest a trend towards a more positive rating of interprofessional communication although the design and the setting is different to Romijn et al. 21 According to the coordination of the daily communication between midwifes and obstetricians, our results did not show a clear trend towards one profession. The results of the questionnaire demonstrate the lack of clarification of responsibilities, work overload and different views on medical facts as major communication barriers. These factors also have been identified by. Koldehof et al as potential barriers in communication. 14 Work overload and unclear responsibilities are organizational issues that should be addressed. 22 Different views in an obstetrical team should be encountered with inclusion of midwives in the shared-decision-making-process to avoid discomfort as a qualitive study in obstetrical team in Switzerland suggests. 22

Our study showed basic knowledge on SBAR and used SABR partially in clinical routine prior the intervention. Touomi et al performed a prospective longitudinal study in an obstetrical-gynecological department. 16 They participated in a 4-hour course on SBAR training, which included a lecture and role-play training for the application of SBAR. 16 They performed a course in a larger but more heterogeneous collective compared to ours, with 62 participants, including medical trainees, residents, midwives, and nurses, to practice SBAR. 16 The majority of their collective (89%) had little knowledge about SBAR, whereas our participants showed more background information on SBAR (90% found their knowledge good or sufficient). 16

After the intervention, the midwifery students showed an increase of knowledge by a higher self-rating and wanted apply SBAR in the future. In the study of Toumi et al. 75.8% of the participants expressed enjoyment with the course and wanted to include SBAR in their practice after the intervention (80.7%), which is in line to our findings, in which 95% of the investigated midwifery students agreed to use SBAR in the future (true and very true). 16

SBAR was rated as helpful in communication with doctors and its teaching was addressed as important for interprofessional communication by the midwifery students. Ting et al performed an educational study on nurses in an obstetrical department. 17 They introduced the SBAR-Scheme for communication of fetal heart rate with a handover and evaluated the introduction of the SBAR tool with the safety attitudes among nurses with the Chinese version of Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ). 17 The authors demonstrated higher ratings for teamwork climate, safety climate, job satisfaction, and working conditions after the implementation of SBAR, underlying the importance SBAR in interprofessionalism. 17 However, using SBAR did not influence the APGAR values of low-birth-weight neonates. 17

Our investigation in midwifery students showed that physicians may use less SBAR in their communication. Pilcher et al investigated the use of SBAR in handovers in the gynecological department of three different Indian hospitals. They found deficiencies in the use of SBAR in the fields Assessment and Recommendation by the nurses’ handovers. Doctors showed inadequate appreciation of handovers and were not trained on patients’ safety; but were satisfied with information transfer methods in their unit. 23 Pilcher et al emphasize the need for a training not only in midwifes but also for physicians and other collaborating disciplines.

Overall ratings of the course were good or very good and for the role-play was mentioned as important reflected by ratings and the comments of the midwifery students. The positive feedback on training course on SBAR is also reported for other courses in obstetrics and gynecology.16,24 Toumi et al used a combination of lecture and role-play for the course as we did. 16 The learning success for SBAR with the additionally introduction of a role-play to a lecture is described for by Kesten et al and Raurell-Torredà for nursing students, in which the interventional groups achieved significantly higher scores in the application of SBAR.25,26 Therefore, our course included multiple role-plays for the midwifery students to exercise SBAR.

The correct application of SBAR was evaluated by the OSCE which revealed high scores, indicating an appropriate learning outcome by the course. The scores for the categories expertise and professionalism trend to decrease, but not significantly, for the doctors’ role compared to the midwife role, which could be explained by the midwifery students’ own profession. Investigations of phone calls between nurses and physicians showed that background information from nurses was often lacking and physicians did not inquire about the information.27,28 In contrast to our study, they showed a low quality of SBAR use rated by scores and the authors concluded that more training is needed for pre- and postgraduate nurses. 28 Bowling et al performed a comparative study on 73 pediatric nursing students trained in communication using SBAR for safe medication administration, assessed using a mini-OSCE. 29 They showed that 55.7% of participants did not assess the patient’s ID and 75.3 did not state the purpose of the given medication after the training. 29 They showed in their study an improvement in scores according to patient identification through training, but the students still had difficulties with the recommendations. 29 The different results compared to our investigation might be explained by different designs and study populations.27-29 We suggest that our training cohort had already background information on SBAR prior the intervention, which might lead to higher application scores, measured by the OSCE.

Our study has several limitations inherited to the study design, sample size and generalizability. We performed a cross-sectional study in a monocentric design which restricts data to our institution. Further, the cohort included only 20 individuals which might be a small sample size, but reflects a complete semester at our institution. In German midwifery faculties, the semester size ranges from 12 to 88 students per semester with a median of 35 students. 30 Our study assessed the effects of SBAR training in a classroom setting and did not investigate effects on their application in clinical routine. This limitation is addressed in a systematic review by Lo et al enrolled 28 studies on teaching SBAR. 5 They indicated that SBAR strengthens precise communication, but studies showed a lack of evidence because of the heterogeneity. 5 The included studies showed partial effects in a classroom setting but did not assess the effects in a clinical context. 5 In contrast to Lo et al., Cohen et al found positive short- and long-term effects of training SBAR in gynecologic unit. 24

The OSCE assessors differed significantly in their ratings in our study due to the course structure. This interobserver variability might be influenced by their different professions and attitudes towards midwifery students, which is described as potential bias for OSCE assessment in literature.31-34 The interobserver variability limits the generalizability of our results.

Conclusion

Interprofessional communication is an important aspect for midwifery students, which should be addressed in their education. We conclude that teaching SBAR in midwifery students strengthen their interprofessional communication skills and improves their attitude for its application positively. Using a role-play model tailored to the obstetrical setting might facilitate the transfer of their learnings on SBAR towards its application.
