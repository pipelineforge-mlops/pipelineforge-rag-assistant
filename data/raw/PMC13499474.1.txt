Introduction and aims

Periodontitis is a chronic multifactorial inflammatory disease that damages periodontal tissues and is associated with systemic diseases. It shows a global younger onset trend, with rising incidence in Chinese young adults, but studies mostly focus on those ≥35 years, lacking systematic exploration of 18 to 35-year-olds.

Methods

From December 2024 to May 2025, 2888 adults aged 18 to 35 years were recruited from Tongji Hospital. Periodontitis was diagnosed per the 2018 Classification. Questionnaires collected demographic, physical and mental health, oral hygiene and lifestyle data. Saliva samples underwent 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics. Statistical analyses included independent samples t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, chi-square test and binary logistic regression.

Results

Periodontitis prevalence was 25.00%. Logistic regression identified marital status, suboptimal health, adverse childhood experiences, smoking and alcohol drinking were correlated with periodontitis, while longer per-brushing duration was negatively correlated with periodontitis. Salivary microbiota community richness and composition differed significantly between groups, with higher Fusobacteriota in periodontitis patients. Untargeted metabolomics identified 267 differential metabolites (mainly lipids, amino acids) and enriched metabolic pathways (e.g., nucleotide metabolism). Fusobacteriota was negatively correlated with 5-phosphoribosyl-4-carboxy-5 aminoimidazole.

Conclusions

Multiple factors were associated with periodontitis in young adults. Salivary microbiota and metabolic abnormalities may be associated with its pathogenesis, providing a scientific basis for prevention and management. The generalizability of these findings was limited by single-center convenience sampling and exploratory multi-omics profiling performed on a small matched subgroup.

Clinical relevance

This study identified a 25% prevalence of periodontitis in Chinese young adults aged 18 to 35 years, clarified its multidimensional associated factors, and revealed salivary microbiome–metabolome perturbations. It provided a scientific basis for the prevention of periodontitis in this young population.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic multifactorial inflammatory disease that destroys the periodontal supporting tissues, leading to tooth loosening and loss, and impairing chewing, speech, and aesthetic functions.1 Additionally, the disease is associated with systemic illnesses. Evidence has demonstrated that periodontitis increases the mortality rate of gastrointestinal cancer in young people.2 and is also linked to an elevated risk of diseases including stroke.3 obstructive sleep apnea.4 and severe asthma.5

In recent years, the incidence of periodontitis worldwide has shown a distinct trend of younger onset.6,7 and its prevalence among young people in China is expected to continue rising.8,9 However, most previous studies have focused on populations aged 35 and above.10 with insufficient attention paid to young adults aged 18-35, making it difficult to meet the research needs for periodontal health prevention and control in this group.

As a multifactorial disease, periodontitis aligns with the core tenets of the biopsychosocial and social ecological models. The biopsychosocial model suggests that disease occurrence is correlated with biological, psychological and social factors, and the social ecological model focuses on mutual interrelationships across multiple levels, including individual characteristics, interpersonal relationships, social environments and health behaviors.11,12

From a sociodemographic perspective, characteristics such as age, gender, income level, and education level can indirectly affect periodontal health by influencing access to medical resources and health management awareness.8,10 At the physical and mental health level, conditions like suboptimal health status, depression, and adverse childhood experiences (ACE) are associated with alterations in the body's immune-inflammatory response and increased colonization and abundance of periodontal pathogenic bacteria.13,14 From a health behavior perspective, oral hygiene behaviors (e.g., tooth brushing, floss use) and lifestyle habits (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption) also show significant associations with the presence of periodontitis.15,16

Dysbiosis of the oral microbiota is strongly associated with the pathogenesis of periodontitis.17 The rapid proliferation of pathogenic bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Tannerella forsythia, along with the release of their virulence factors, are correlated with periodontal tissue destruction.18,19 Meanwhile, the role of bacterial or salivary metabolites in periodontal health has been gradually uncovered. For instance, metabolites like pyroglutamic acid and butyrophenone are positively correlated with the 5-year tooth loss risk in periodontitis patients.20 additionally, phenylacetic acid, a metabolite of subgingival anaerobic bacteria, shows a positive correlation with periodontal probing depth and is associated with higher levels of pathogenic bacteria.21

These theoretical frameworks and existing research evidence indicate that single-dimensional analyses fail to fully characterize the association profiles of periodontitis, underscoring the urgent need for comprehensive multidimensional research. However, most current studies include only some of the aforementioned factors, focus primarily on middle-aged and elderly populations, and lack systematic analyses of factors associated with periodontitis in young adults.8,10,22,23 Therefore, this study enrolled young adults as participants, integrated multi-level survey data on sociodemographic characteristics, physical and mental health status, and health behaviors, and simultaneously performed 16S rRNA sequencing of salivary microbiota and untargeted metabolomics analysis.

This study aimed to explore the epidemiological characteristics of periodontitis in young adults, analyze the strength of the associations between the aforementioned factors and periodontitis, and ultimately provide a scientific basis for the prevention and management of periodontitis and the improvement of oral health in this population.

Material and methods

Study subjects

All participants were recruited from Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, from December 2024 and May 2025. All subjects were hospital outpatients, and no community-based general population was enrolled in this study. Recruitment covered multiple outpatient departments including stomatology, physical examination centers and internal medicine. The study subjects fell into three categories: patients seeking dental treatment, young adults attending routine physical examinations, and individuals visiting internal medicine clinics.

Inclusion criteria: aged 18 to 35 years; willing to participate in the study and sign the informed consent form; mentally healthy, with the ability to complete the survey independently. Exclusion criteria: poor compliance; concurrent enrollment‌ in other clinical studies incompatible with this study.

Additional exclusion criteria for saliva collection: taking systemic antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs (glucocorticoids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) in the past 3 months, or long-term (≥1 month) use of probiotics and prebiotics; history of supragingival scaling, subgingival curettage, or periodontal surgery within 3 months; presence of oral mucosal diseases(e.g.,oral lichen planus,oral ulcers); acute upper respiratory infection symptoms (e.g.,common cold,influenza)within 7 days; unhealed oral or maxillofacial trauma; patients undergoing orthodontic treatment; patients with gastroesophageal reflux; positive for hepatitis B, tuberculosis, HIV, or syphilis; pregnant or lactating women.

This study was approved on September 6, 2024 by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No.: TJ-IRB202409066) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) with the registration number ChiCTR2500103464. All procedures were performed in accordance with the 2013 revised version of the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation for this study was performed using the pwr.f2.test function in the pwr package of R (version 3.6.3), under the generalized linear model framework. Based on prior relevant literature.24 the effect size was set to f² = 1.374, with the significance level α = 0.05 and statistical power (1-β) = 90% (β= 0.1) controlled. Given the multiple covariates included in the analyses, the numerator degrees of freedom (u, corresponding to the independent variables) was set to 15.

Entering these parameters into the function yielded a residual (error) degrees of freedom (v) of 112, leading to an initial raw sample size of n = u + v + 1 = 128. Multiplying this initial estimate by a design effect of 8 (to account for the stratified study design) brought the adjusted sample size to 1024; adding a 30% buffer for potential sample attrition resulted in a further adjusted sample size of 1332.

Considering the practical feasibility of participant recruitment, the target sample size was rounded to 2000. Ultimately, 2888 eligible participants were included in the study, meeting the prespecified statistical power requirement. A combination of accidental and convenience sampling was used to meet the required sample size.

Oral examination

All participants underwent oral examinations by ≥2 licensed periodontists using the periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy, USA, Model PCP-12, scale 1-15 mm), with periodontal probing depth (PPD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and bleeding on probing recorded. X-rays and other imaging were used to assist in assessing alveolar bone resorption. All dentists received special training to ensure examination consistency, achieving intra- and inter-examiner Kappa values and intraclass correlation coefficients of ≥0.75, which meets the reliability criteria established for clinical research.

Primary outcome: periodontitis

According to the 2018 World Workshop Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions, the diagnostic criteria for periodontitis are.25 detectable interdental CAL at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth, or detectable buccal/lingual CAL ≥3 mm accompanied by PPD >3 mm at ≥2 teeth. Meanwhile, non-periodontal factors such as traumatic gingival recession, cervical dental caries, CAL at the distal surface of the second molar associated with impacted third molar, periodontal pockets communicating with periapical lesions, and vertical root fractures must be excluded.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted with the Magnetic Bead Genome Extraction Kit (MAGEN, China). DNA quality, concentration, and purity were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). A 50 μL PCR reaction system (2 × Phanta Max Master Mix, VAZYME) containing 30 ng template DNA and 338F/806R primers (V3–4 region) was used for amplification.23 Purified products were subjected to library construction and PE300 sequencing on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (BGI, China).

Raw reads were denoised and chimeras removed using the divisive amplicon denoising algorithm 2 plugin in quantitative insights into microbial ecology 2 to generate amplicon sequence variant sequences. Taxonomic annotation was performed against the Silva database using RDP Classifier (v2.2). Alpha diversity (Sobs, Chao, Ace, Shannon, Simpson) was analyzed in Mothur. Beta diversity was evaluated using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), with statistical significance assessed by permutational multivariate analysis of variance. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe, P < .05, LDA ≥2) and Wilcoxon rank-sum test identified differentially abundant taxa.23,26

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

100 μL salivary samples were used for analysis; pooled quality control (QC) samples were inserted every 10 samples. Samples were extracted with 700 μL solution (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 4:2:1, v/v/v) containing d3-leucine, incubated at −20°C for 2 h, centrifuged (25000 × g, 4°C, 15 min), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, reconstituted (methanol:water = 1:1, v/v), and recentrifuged for detection.

Metabolomic profiling was performed on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Mobile phase A (water–acetonitrile 95:5, v/v, 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile–isopropanol–water 47.5:47.5:5, v/v/v, 0.1% formic acid) were used with gradient elution (5% B: 0-2 min; 5%-95% B: 2-10 min; 95% B: 10-12 min; 5% B: 12-15 min). Column temperature 40°C, flow rate 0.4 mL/min, injection volume 5 μL. Mass spectrometry was operated in positive/negative ion modes (full-scan resolution 120000, m/z 70-1050; tandem mass spectrometry resolution 30000, fragmentation at 20/40/60 eV).

Compound Discoverer 3.3 was used for peak extraction and metabolite annotation by matching against the Human Metabolome Database and Metlin database. Peak areas were normalized by probabilistic quotient normalization; metabolites with coefficient of variation >30% in QC were excluded. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was used for modeling; differential metabolites were screened by variable importance in projection ≥1 and P < .05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; https://www.kegg.jp/) pathway enrichment (P < .05) was performed. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the associations between differential microbiota and metabolites.

Statistical analysis

In general statistical description, quantitative data were presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as count (n) and percentage (%). For intergroup difference comparison: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (a normality test method for sample size greater than 50) was used for quantitative data; if normality was satisfied, the independent samples t-test was applied; if variance was unequal or normality was not satisfied, the Mann-Whitney U rank sum test was used; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Binary logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1: General demographic characteristics only; Model 2 added physical health indicators to Model 1; Model 3 added mental health indicators to Model 2; Model 4 added oral hygiene behaviors to Model 3; Model 5 added lifestyle indicators to Model 4. The significance level α was set at 0.05, and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0 and R 3.6.3.

For the metabolome analysis, OPLS-DA showed clear separation between subgroups with acceptable model fitness (Figure S1B and S1C). A total of 267 differential metabolites (161 upregulated, 106 downregulated) were identified, which were mainly enriched in lipids, amino acids and peptides (Figure S1D). These metabolites were primarily involved in purine metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism and D-amino acid metabolism (Figure 2E). Spearman correlation analysis indicated a negative association between Fusobacteriota and 5-phosphoribosyl-4-carboxy-5-aminoimidazole (Figure 2F).

The clear intergroup separation observed via PCoA and OPLS-DA indicated that Fusobacteriota and the 267 lipid- and amino acid-dominated differential metabolites could distinguish patients with periodontitis, preliminarily supporting their potential as candidate salivary biomarkers for periodontitis in young adults.

Discussion

The study focused on young adults aged 18-35 years. By integrating epidemiological surveys with 16S rRNA sequencing of salivary microbiota and untargeted metabolomics analysis, it revealed the multifactorial association network of periodontitis in this population. This filled the gap in previous studies that mostly focused on populations aged ≥ 35 years and provided a scientific basis for the prevention and control of periodontal health in young adults.

In the study, the prevalence of periodontitis among young adults reached 25.00%, suggesting that periodontitis may have become a public health issue affecting the oral health of young people in China. From the perspective of demographic characteristics, marital status was identified as a factor significant positively associated with periodontitis (OR = 1.838, P < .001). This finding differs from previous studies that suggested a positive effect of marital status on oral health.27 The discrepancy may be attributed to the marital stress faced by young adults, which may alter health behaviors (e.g., tooth brushing) or lifestyle habits (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption), disrupt host immune balance against periodontal pathogenic bacteria, and thereby be associated with exacerbated periodontal inflammation and tissue destruction.28,14 However, the specific mechanism required verification by subsequent cohort studies. In addition, differences in gender (prevalence: 27.27% in males vs. 23.81% in females, P = .042) suggested that males may be key populations for intervention. This was closely associated with males being more likely to neglect oral care, having a higher smoking rate, and differences in androgen-mediated immune responses.29

From the perspective of physical and mental health, suboptimal health status (OR = 1.021, P < .001) and ACE (OR = 1.271, P < .001) were key associated factors. These conditions have been reported to correlate with overactivation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and elevated cortisol levels, which may be linked to altered function of local periodontal immune cells and reduced clearance of periodontal pathogens.14,30, 31, 32, 33 Notably, the impact of ACE was independent of depressive symptoms in adulthood (CESD score of the periodontitis group: 18.8 ± 6.09, P < .001), suggesting that childhood trauma showed a stronger association with periodontitis than adult mental health issues. In future prevention and control of periodontitis in young adults, ACE assessment should be incorporated, and enhanced periodontal monitoring is needed for individuals with high ACE scores.

For lifestyle habits, smoking (OR = 1.338, P = .010) and alcohol drinking (OR = 1.269, P = .012) were observed to correlate with periodontitis in the present study. Previous studies had confirmed that infrequent tooth brushing increases the risk of periodontitis, while regular tooth brushing reduces this risk.34 However, this study further found that, unlike tooth brushing frequency, longer toothbrushing duration per session was negatively associated with periodontitis (OR = 0.696, P < .001). This result provided a quantitative basis for oral health education among young adults – compared with the number of daily tooth brushing sessions (P = .397), brushing duration better reflected cleaning efficiency.

A wealth of multi-omics evidence from oral population cohort studies demonstrates that host factors including age, body mass index (BMI), tobacco and alcohol consumption, dietary patterns and socioeconomic status alter oral microecology and salivary metabolic profiles. Xu et al’s multi-omics cohort study of Chinese participants confirmed that unhealthy lifestyles such as smoking, abdominal obesity, insufficient physical activity and unbalanced diets disrupt oral ecological homeostasis, elevating the relative abundance of Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium and Treponema while reducing the proportion of the beneficial symbiotic genus Rothia.35 Ribeiro et al similarly observed widespread oral microbial dysbiosis among individuals with high BMI, excessive trans fat intake and low socioeconomic status, accompanied by enrichment of periodontal pathogens including Saccharibacteria, Porphyromonas endodontalis and Fusobacterium.36 Salivary metabolomics studies confirm that age, BMI, circadian rhythm, smoking and daily diet alter the abundance of various salivary metabolites and remodel the oral metabolic microenvironment.37,38 Given that all the above factors can perturb integrated salivary microbiome–metabolome signatures, we adopted a 1:1 matched subcohort comprising 20 patients with periodontitis and 20 healthy controls for joint salivary microbiota and metabolite profiling. No statistically significant intergroup differences were found in age, gender, education, sub-health status, adverse childhood experiences, smoking and alcohol drinking, and toothbrushing duration per session (P > .05), suggesting that the differences in salivary microbial and metabolic profiles between the two groups are most likely attributable to periodontitis per se.

As an exploratory hypothesis-generating analysis in a small subgroup, our preliminary microbiome data showed that young adults with periodontitis presented increased microbial community richness but unchanged diversity, with distinct separation in community structure between groups. The relative abundance of Fusobacteriota was notably elevated in the periodontitis group, which was preliminarily supported by LEfSe analysis and consistent with existing reports on periodontal pathogens.39

Untargeted metabolomics preliminarily identified 267 differential metabolites that were mainly enriched in purine metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, and D-amino acid metabolism pathways. These metabolic pathways have been reported to be associated with inflammatory response and microbial colonization in periodontal diseases, suggesting their potential association with periodontitis progression.40, 41, 42, 43, 44

Spearman correlation analysis indicated a preliminary negative association between Fusobacteriota and 5-phosphoribosyl-4-carboxy-5-aminoimidazole (CAIR). This may be partially explained by accelerated purine degradation in periodontitis, which reduces CAIR generation and its inhibition on Fusobacteriota.40,44,45 Based on the above findings, the combined signature of Fusobacteriota and purine metabolites provided theoretical support for non-invasive saliva-assisted diagnosis of periodontitis in young adults.

Although this study systematically investigated potential factors associated with periodontitis among young adults, several limitations remain: First, participants were enrolled via a combination of convenience sampling and accidental sampling, a non-probability sampling strategy. This single-center hospital-based recruitment inevitably introduced selection bias and Berkson’s admission bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings to community young adults. Furthermore, the cross-sectional observational design only permitted the analysis of association strength between variables and periodontitis, while it failed to identify causal timelines and causal relationships. In addition, genetic susceptibility indicators were not included in the present analysis, leading to inadequate exploration of pathogenic mechanisms at the genetic level. Meanwhile, as hypothesis-generating exploratory analyses in a small subgroup, our findings on the salivary microbiome and metabolome represented preliminary associations without definitive mechanistic conclusions. In addition, the multi-omics matched subcohort contained merely 40 subjects, and host-related variables lacked gradient stratification, resulting in insufficient statistical power to quantify the correlations between individual host factors and salivary microbiome-metabolome signatures. Finally, functional and clinical validation of differential metabolites was not conducted, leaving their diagnostic value and pathogenic mechanisms unclarified.

Future research should be carried out in the following aspects. Multi-center prospective cohort studies should be conducted to expand population coverage, reduce selection bias, improve the generalizability of study findings, and clarify causal associations among factors. In addition, the sample size of multi-omics detection should be enlarged to screen stable biomarkers for periodontitis, and sufficient samples with stratified host variables need to be collected to analyze the interactive relationships between individual host factors and multi-omics profiles. Finally, functional and clinical validation of differential metabolites should be performed.

Conclusions

The study indicated that periodontitis among young adults aged 18 to 35 years was associated with multiple factors: marital status, ACE, suboptimal health status, smoking, and alcohol consumption showed positive associations, while longer single toothbrushing duration per session showed a significant negative association. Meanwhile, salivary microecological imbalance and metabolic disorders may be involved in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, and the negative correlation between the Fusobacteria and differential metabolites related to purine metabolism provided new clues for exploring the potential mechanisms underlying the disease.
