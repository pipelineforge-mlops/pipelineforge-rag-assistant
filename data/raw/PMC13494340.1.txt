Objective

Dietary advice is increasingly shifting towards personalisation enabled by digital technologies. As personalised nutrition recommendation applications rely on user data, little is known about how users differ in attitudes towards data sharing, desired app features, and expected benefits.

Methods

In a cross-sectional survey conducted in Germany, adults aged ≥18 years completed a standardised online-questionnaire assessing participant characteristics and attitudes towards personalised nutrition in health applications. Behavioural intention (BI) was measured using the Technology Acceptance Model 3 questionnaire. Four items yielded count-based outcomes (number of selected options). Descriptive statistics, generalised additive models, and exploratory cluster analysis based on age, body mass index (BMI), and BI were conducted using RStudio.

Results

In total, 1,070 participants completed this cross-sectional survey (42.4 ± 15.3 years; BMI 25.6 ± 6.0 kg/m2; 75% female). An exploratory cluster analysis (age, BMI, BI) identified three user groups. Clusters differed in expected benefits, willingness to share parameters, core aspects of personalised advice, and perceived importance of app features (all P ≤ 0.01), with small to moderate effect sizes (η2 = 0.006 – 0.10).

Conclusion

Age, BMI, and BI modify users’ attitudes towards personalised dietary advice in health apps, while their overall explanatory power was limited. Responses varied within similar clusters, suggesting apps should be flexible and modular to accommodate individual preferences.

Introduction

There is a paradigm shift from generalised dietary recommendations to personalised approaches aiming at optimising individual health outcomes. 1 Although there is currently no consistently accepted definition of the term 'personalised nutrition’, 2 it is broadly understood as an approach that provides tailored dietary recommendations based on individual characteristics, such as genetic, phenotypic, and behavioural factors.3–5 Studies show that personalised nutrition recommendations, whether face-to-face or digital, lead to more sustainable dietary changes than general recommendations.5–8 By employing innovative methods such as machine learning and deep neural networks, AI-based systems can process these inputs to provide highly personalised guidance.8–10 Therefore AI plays an increasing role in digital health technologies, 11 including personalised nutrition, by enabling the integration and analysis of complex data, such as genetic, phenotypic, and behavioural data, thereby supporting the generation of tailored recommendations. 12 Moreover, these developments enable the efficient automation and remote provision of personalised nutrition recommendations.13,14 Digital platforms offer a scalable and cost-effective way to implement personalised nutrition without compromising effectiveness. 7

These advances are increasingly reflected in nutrition-related mobile applications (apps), which support users in monitoring dietary intake, generating personalised feedback, and integrating vital and lifestyle data.15–17 However, the successful implementation of personalised nutrition solutions critically depends on the availability of and willingness to share individual data. 18 Users must provide detailed information about their health, behaviour, and preferences.

Previous research has examined user acceptance and behavioural intention towards digital health and nutrition applications, often drawing on technology acceptance models to explain uptake and continued use.19–21 These studies generally report a moderate to high willingness to use nutrition-related apps, while also demonstrating substantial variability depending on perceived usefulness, ease of use, and personal relevance.21,22 Additionally, several studies have explored users’ willingness to share health-related data with digital apps. The acceptance differs markedly by data type, with a higher willingness to share lifestyle-related information compared to more sensitive health or biometric data.23,24 Further work has investigated user expectations towards personalised nutrition, due to the fact that individuals differ widely in the aspects they consider important and in their expectations of personalised dietary advice.2,25 Most existing studies have addressed these dimensions in isolation or within specific subgroups. Integrated evidence that jointly examines expectations towards personalised nutrition, perceived relevance of app features, and willingness to share data within a heterogeneous population remains limited.2,21,26

This cross-sectional survey aimed to gain a differentiated understanding of users’ expectations and preferences regarding personalised nutrition recommendations in health apps, operationalised as the breadth of expected app features, willingness to share health-related parameters, and expectations towards personalised nutrition advice. In particular, we examined whether user characteristics, such as gender, age, Body Mass Index (BMI), and Behavioural Intention (BI), are associated with differences in these outcomes. In a further step, we applied an exploratory cluster analysis to identify distinct user profiles and to explore patterns in user expectations and preferences in personalised nutrition recommendations in health apps.

Methods

Survey

This open online cross-sectional survey was accessible from June 2023 to June 2025. During this period, participants were recruited online and completed the questionnaire immediately upon accessing the survey link. Participants were recruited through multiple channels (e.g., outpatient clinics, universities, public events, newsletters, and social media) in Germany. Recruitment was supported by printed flyers containing a QR code; additionally, the survey link was disseminated online, enabling access independent of location. Interested individuals were directed to the online survey hosted on the SoSci Survey platform (Version 3.6.11). The participants were required to confirm the data privacy statement and provide informed consent before participating in the survey. Furthermore, they had to confirm that they were of legal age (≥18 years). No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied beyond the ability to participate in the online survey. The survey results were anonymised. No incentives were offered to participants.

Questionnaire

The standardised German-language questionnaire was developed ad hoc for this study by a team that included nutritionists and medical practitioners (Supplement 1). The questionnaire consisted of both previously validated 27 and newly developed items. The non-validated items capturing preferences, expectations, and willingness to share data, were newly developed for this study. The ad hoc questionnaire was not pilot-tested, and no formal reliability or validity assessments were conducted. These items were designed to explore a broad range of user perspectives regarding personalised nutrition in health apps.

The survey consists of 40 questions (closed, open, single, or multiple choice) on demographic and anthropometric factors (9 questions), nutrition behaviour and personality (8 questions), the personalisation of nutritional recommendations (5 questions), technology usage (11 questions), and personalised nutritional recommendations via app (7 questions). The survey could be answered in about five minutes. Each question had to be answered before continuing. Participants were free to withdraw from the survey at any time.

The main outcomes of this study were derived from four questionnaire items assessing participants’ preferences and expectations regarding a hypothetical health app and personalised nutrition recommendations. The first two questions addressed the domain “personalised nutritional recommendations via app”, whereas the other two questions addressed the domain “personalisation of nutritional recommendations”. Within each question, participants could select multiple predefined options. For the analyses, the number of selected options per participant was calculated and used as the outcome measure. This approach provides a standardised and comparable metric reflecting the breadth of preferences and expectations across participants and allows a quantitative analysis of multiple-response items.

The following brief description of a hypothetical health app that provides personalised nutrition recommendations was presented to the participants. “This app would contain scientifically based information on nutrition, exercise, and weight development. Potential users could use the app to record their health data (e.g., diet, exercise, weight, blood pressure) and set personal goals. At regular intervals, they would receive feedback, tips, and motivational messages to assist them in optimising their health.” Based on this hypothetical app, participants were asked to answer the following questions: “Which features are important to you in a health app?” and “Which parameters would you be willing to share with this app to enable and improve personalised nutrition recommendations?”. In addition, two questions explored participants’ preferences regarding personalised nutrition more generally: “How important are the following aspects for your personalised nutrition recommendations?” and “What are your expectations of personalised nutrition recommendations?”.

Demographic and anthropometric variables were used to describe participant characteristics. Disease status was assessed as a single dichotomous item (“having a disease”: yes/no) and reported as part of the sample characteristics.

Technology acceptance model 3 (TAM 3)

The dimension BI from the validated Technology Acceptance Model 3 (TAM 3) questionnaire 27 was included in the questionnaire (domain: personalised nutritional recommendations via app) and completed by all participants. The BI captured participants’ intention (i.e., likelihood and plans) to use the hypothetical health app and was assessed using three items rated on a 5-level Likert scale. Mean BI scores were calculated, ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating a greater intention to use the hypothetical app. Based on BI tertiles, participants were categorised into three equally sized groups (low BI: n = 357, medium BI: n = 357, high BI: n = 356) to facilitate the comparison across low, medium, and high levels of BI.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, four questions were rephrased. The rephrased questions reflect the primary outcomes: A) the number of app features rated as important, B) the number of shared parameters in a health app, C) the number of core aspects considered important for personalised nutritional recommendations, and D) the number of expected benefits of such recommendations (Figure 1).Figure 1. Original and rephrased questions, the corresponding item labels, and the predefined response options from the questionnaire.

In total, 1,593 people confirmed the data privacy statement and gave informed consent. Of these, 1,070 completed the survey fully and were included in the final analysis. The description of population characteristics included calculating means for the total sample and for female and male participants separately.

A Generalised Additive Model (GAM) was fitted to the data to account for potential non-linear associations among age, BMI, and BI as continuous variables, gender as a categorical variable, and participants’ responses. To obtain adjusted means (estimated marginal means), each variable of interest was treated as categorical (e.g., age groups, BMI categories, BI tertiles), while the remaining variables were entered as continuous covariates. Estimated marginal means were calculated using the R package “emmeans”. 28 The overall significance of each categorical variable was assessed using a joint F-test, as implemented in the “emmeans” package. P-values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The proportion of deviance explained was calculated from a GAM including smoothed continuous variables (BMI, age, and BI), and gender as the only categorical variable.

The k-means clustering algorithm, 29 was applied to cluster participants based on age, BMI, and BI. The Elbow method 30 was used to determine the optimal number of clusters. The normality assumption was tested and violated (P < 0.05 for all clusters); therefore, the Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted to assess cluster differences. Eta2 (η2) was calculated as a measure of effect size, indicating the proportion of variance explained by the clusters. For the effect size of “having a disease”, Cramer’s V was used. All analyses were performed using RStudio version 4.3.2.

Cluster characteristics

Cluster analysis based on age, BMI, and BI identified three distinct potential app user groups (Table 6). Cluster 1 (n = 447) represented the youngest group of participants, with a mean age of 30.7 ± 8.6 years, a mean BMI of 22.3 ± 2.7 kg/m2, and a mean BI score of 4.2 ± 0.5. Cluster 2 (n = 202) included participants with a mean age of 43.0 ± 15.5 years, a slightly higher mean BMI of 23.6 ± 3.9 kg/m2, and the lowest mean BI score of 2.5 ± 0.7. Cluster 3 (n = 421) was characterised by a mean age of 54.5 ± 10.4 years, the highest mean BMI of 30.0 ± 6.6 kg/m2, and a mean BI score of 4.3 ± 0.6. The prevalence of disease differed significantly across clusters (Cluster 1: 27.7%, Cluster 2: 17.2%, Cluster 3: 55.0%, P < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.27). The number of app features considered important for personalised nutrition apps significantly varied across clusters (P < 0.001, η2 = 0.06). Cluster 3 rated the greatest number of app features as important (8.9 ± 2.5), followed by cluster 1 (8.3 ± 2.4), whereas cluster 2 (6.6 ± 3.5) rated the fewest features as important. Significant differences were found in the willingness to share parameters with the hypothetical health app (P < 0.001, η2 = 0.07). Cluster 1 reported the highest number of shared parameters (7.8 ± 2.7), followed by cluster 3 (7.1 ± 2.6), while cluster 2 showed the lowest number of shared parameters (5.1 ± 3.9). The analysis of the number of core aspects rated as important for personalised nutrition recommendations also showed statistically significant differences between the clusters (P = 0.01, η2 = 0.006). Cluster 1 reported the highest number of core aspects as important for personalised nutrition recommendations (8.9 ± 2.1), followed by cluster 3 (8.7 ± 2.4), while cluster 2 showed the lowest number of core aspects, rated as important (8.2 ± 2.8) In addition the number of expected benefits of personalised nutrition recommendations significantly differed between clusters (P < 0.001, η2 = 0.1). Cluster 3 reported the highest number of expected benefits of personalised nutrition recommendations (5.5 ± 1.2), followed by cluster 1 (4.8 ± 1.3) and cluster 2 (4.4 ± 1.4) (Table 6).Table 6. Discussion

This survey examined attitudes regarding personalised nutrition recommendations in health apps and showed differences between gender, age, BMI, and BI groups. As preferences and expectations towards personalised nutrition advice may differ across age groups, our findings are consistent with previous research. 31 In our sample, younger participants rated more app features and core aspects of personalised nutrition advice as important, whereas individuals older than 60 years were willing to share fewer parameters but expressed more expected benefits regarding personalised nutrition recommendations. The lower number of shared parameters aligns with explanations from the literature, may reflect heightened concerns about privacy.21,32

The findings also raise important ethical considerations related to the use of personal health data in digital nutrition applications. Personalised nutrition relies on the collection and processing of potentially sensitive information, including behavioural and health-related data. While participants in this study indicated a willingness to share certain types of data, this should be interpreted in the context of broader issues such as user autonomy, data security, and the potential misuse of personal information. Balancing the benefits of personalised recommendations with the protection of user privacy remains a key challenge in the development of digital health applications. These considerations align with growing calls in the digital health literature for transparent data governance frameworks and ethical guidelines that prioritise user rights alongside technological innovation. 33

Gender-related differences were observed, though inconsistently, aligning with mixed findings in the literature.21,34 Furthermore, BMI was associated with differences in expected benefits of personalised dietary advice. This finding is consistent with previous studies.31,35,36 Participants with a high BI indicated a greater number of shared parameters with the app, a higher number of expected benefits from personalised nutrition recommendations, and rated more app features as important. In contrast, individuals in the low BI group rated fewer expected benefits of personalised nutrition recommendations, fewer app features in a health app as important and shared fewer parameters with the app. This pattern can be interpreted in light of the TAM 3, 25 in which BI reflects an individual’s motivation and readiness to use a technology. Higher BI may be associated with a greater perceived usefulness and acceptance of app functionalities, which could explain why these individuals reported a broader range of expected benefits, valued more app features as important, and were more willing to share personal data. Unlike gender, age, or BMI, the BI represents a potentially modifiable factor. Previous research suggests that flexible personalisation options, allowing users to decide which app features to use and what data to provide, can help increase app acceptance and improve app usability. 32

While age, gender, BMI, and BI shape individuals’ preferences and expectations towards personalised dietary advice in health apps, unmeasured factors or individual differences likely contribute substantially to the observed variation in responses. In addition, the relatively low proportion of explained deviance in the GAMs indicates that a considerable part of the variability in the outcome measures remains unexplained. This suggests that additional factors not captured in the present study may contribute to individual differences in attitudes towards personalised nutrition in health apps. Potential factors that may warrant consideration in future research include digital literacy, socioeconomic characteristics, trust in technology, and attitudes towards data privacy. Furthermore, these results highlight the complex nature of personalised nutrition recommendations.

To further explore patterns in user characteristics, a cluster analysis was conducted based on age, BMI, and BI to identify user groups with similar characteristics that are potentially relevant for app development. Clusters 1 and 3 both showed higher BI scores, but differed substantially in age and BMI. Cluster 1 consisted of younger participants with lower BMI, while cluster 3 included older individuals with higher BMI. This suggests that a higher BI score is not confined to a specific demographic profile but is shaped by motivational and contextual factors, as highlighted in previous research37,38 and conceptualised in technology acceptance models such as TAM 3. 27 To increase engagement among individuals with lower BI, additional motivation and technical support may be needed to foster trust and confidence in the app. 38 This underlines the critical role of motivation and intention in shaping user behaviour and implies that interventions targeting BI could enhance the adoption and use of personalised nutrition tools. An Indian study 37 found that behavioural intention to use dietary and fitness apps was influenced by users’ attitudes, those who perceived the apps as helpful and reliable were more likely to adopt them. Hence, it is essential to clearly communicate the potential benefits to users of such apps.

Disease prevalence was highest in cluster 3 (P < 0.001), consistent with prior research indicating that chronic conditions are linked to higher engagement with health apps. 39 All four response items showed significant differences between the clusters. Clusters differed in the number of app features rated as important, the number of expected benefits, core aspects rated as important for personalised recommendations, and the number of shared parameters. While these findings indicate potential for group-based segmentation in the development of personalised nutrition apps, they should be interpreted with caution. The clustering was based on a limited set of variables (age, BMI, and BI), and the observed low to moderate effect sizes, together with the high variability within clusters, suggest that the identified groups are not clearly distinct. Rather than representing well-defined user profiles, the clusters reflect general patterns in a heterogeneous population. Additional variables not captured in the present study may be required to identify more robust and meaningful user segments. Beyond the number of desired app features or the number of health parameters, the variation in user expectations and preferences (Supplements 2-5) further indicates that the features users consider relevant and the parameters they are willing to share differ substantially between individuals. These results align with Cai and Li (2023), who argue that tailoring app features solely based on broad user profiles may be insufficient. 40 Instead, they highlight the need to dynamically adapt features to individual characteristics such as motivation, emotional responsiveness, and self-regulation, to prevent user disengagement. 40

This perspective is supported by a systematic review by König et al., 32 which identifies personalisation as a key factor in user engagement with nutrition apps. Their findings suggest that users value content reflecting their personal goals and preferences. Failure to adapt app content accordingly can lead to a loss of motivation and reduced usage over time. 41 Other research similarly indicates that insufficient responsiveness to changing user needs can negatively affect sustained engagement with digital health applications. 42 In line with this body of literature, the present findings highlight substantial heterogeneity in users’ expectations and preferences regarding personalised nutrition apps. Notably, considerable variability was observed even within the identified clusters, suggesting that uniform or one-size-fits-all approaches may be insufficient to reflect the diversity of user expectations. Rather than supporting specific design solutions, these results underscore the relevance of flexibility and user-centred conceptualisation of digital applications for personalised nutrition.

Previous research has proposed a range of adaptive and customisable elements in digital health applications, such as personalised feedback, behavioural tracking, and goal-oriented functionalities, to accommodate diverse user needs. 42 Similarly, conceptual approaches emphasise the potential value of modular or configurable app structures that allow users to align app functionality with their individual preferences and expectations.40,41,43,44 In this context, allowing users to indicate which parameters they are willing to share and how personalised nutrition support is structured may contribute to better alignment with user expectations and perceived relevance. Overall, while personalised dietary recommendations rely on algorithmic processing of individual input data, 12 the present findings suggest that user expectations regarding personalisation and data sharing are heterogeneous. These results reinforce previous work highlighting the importance of customisability and adaptability in health app concepts, while also indicating the need for further research to evaluate how specific design features influence user engagement and long-term use.

Strengths and limitations

The rather large sample size and the focussed analyses strengthen our results. Summarising responses into numeric scores helps to capture the complexity of personalised dietary recommendations and serves as an adjunct to specifying individual expectations and preferences. Therefore, this study provides valuable insight into user preferences regarding personalised nutrition apps.

However, this approach does not account for differences in the relative importance of individual options and may therefore simplify complex user preferences. The findings thus reflect the breadth rather than the prioritisation of preferences.

While BI was assessed using a validated instrument (TAM 3), the remaining questionnaire items were developed ad hoc and were not formally validated. Consequently, no information on internal consistency or construct validity is available for these measures. This should be considered when interpreting the findings, which are therefore primarily exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

A response bias may have occurred, as individuals with a particular interest in the topic may have been more likely to participate. Participants were recruited through a non-probabilistic, online-based approach, and the sample showed a predominance of female respondents. In addition, the study was conducted exclusively in Germany. Consequently, the sample is not representative of the general population, and the findings may not be generalisable to other populations. This should be taken into account when interpreting the results. However, the observed sample structure (e.g., more females than males) aligns with patterns commonly reported in other nutrition and health app studies.34,45 In addition, a population-based survey 39 reported similar age-related patterns in mobile technology use, supporting the relevance of our findings despite limited representativeness. Furthermore, it is important to mention that the app-related outcome in our study was assessed based on hypothetical scenarios rather than actual usage.

Conclusion

Personalised nutrition recommendations in health apps rely on access to relevant user data, which can be processed by AI to generate tailored dietary advice. Our findings suggest that preferences and expectations regarding such recommendations in apps are associated with age, gender, BMI, and BI. Cluster analysis revealed groups with shared characteristics, yet considerable variation within clusters highlights that group-based profiling is insufficient to fully capture individual user needs. Consistent with this heterogeneity, the design of health apps may benefit from avoiding a one-size-fits-all approach and instead adopting a modular, toolbox-like structure that allows users to decide which app features to use and which parameters to share. This level of personalisation appears necessary to reflect the individual preferences identified in our study and to support a more user-centred app experience. Further research is needed on these individual components.
