Objective

This study examines how user control is constructed, communicated, and experienced in digital health tracking, using Apple Health as a case study. Rather than treating control as either fully empowering or simply illusory, the study conceptualizes it as bounded, relational, and discursively produced within platform environments.

Methods

The study uses a qualitative, triangulated design combining critical discourse analysis of Apple’s official “Health App and Privacy” text with semi-structured interviews with Apple Watch and Apple Health users (n = 18). The analysis compares corporate privacy discourse with users’ everyday experiences of metrics, goals, notifications, permissions, and health-related feedback.

Results

Apple’s privacy discourse frames users as active decision-makers who can manage privacy, sharing, and platform participation. At the same time, this discourse situates user choice within predefined platform architectures, permissions, and data-processing conditions. Interview findings show that users often understand the limits of platform options and engage with Apple Health in pragmatic and informed ways. However, their everyday practices are also shaped by goal-setting, notifications, quantified feedback, and affective responses such as motivation, reassurance, pressure, guilt, and dependence.

Conclusions

The findings suggest that user control in digital health should be understood as bounded rather than simply false. Apple Health provides functional choices and privacy settings, but these operate within a wider system of platform-defined options, behavioral prompts, and datafication. The study contributes to digital health and health communication scholarship by showing how privacy discourse, interface design, and self-tracking practices jointly produce structured forms of autonomy under algorithmic governance.

Introduction

Digital health technologies have transformed how the body is measured, interpreted, and governed in everyday life. Through smartphones, wearable devices, sensors, dashboards, and app-based feedback, bodily activity is increasingly translated into data that can be monitored, compared, and acted upon.1,2 Apple Health is a significant example of this transformation because it aggregates health-related data, visualizes bodily activity, and encourages users to engage with metrics, goals, reminders, and personalized feedback.

This study approaches Apple Health not simply as a technical tool for health monitoring, but as a digital health communication infrastructure. The platform not only collects or displays health data; it also communicates specific meanings about health, responsibility, privacy, and user control. Its privacy discourse emphasizes protection, choice, and user agency, while its interface features organize everyday health practices through quantification, goal-setting, and feedback. For this reason, Apple Health provides a useful case for examining how privacy discourse and self-tracking practices intersect in the production of bounded control.

The study is informed by Foucauldian concepts of biopolitics and governmentality, which explain how modern forms of power operate by shaping conduct, responsibility, and self-regulation rather than relying only on direct coercion.3–6 In digital health contexts, these dynamics are evident in the shift from self-care to continuous self-monitoring. Metrics, activity goals, notifications, and feedback loops encourage users to evaluate their bodies and behaviors according to platform-defined norms of activity, productivity, and health optimization.7,8

Existing research on self-tracking and the quantified self has shown that digital health practices can support self-knowledge, motivation, and health awareness, but can also intensify responsibilization, comparison, pressure, and self-surveillance.1,9,10 Users do not simply accept these systems passively. They may trust, negotiate, ignore, resist, or disengage from platform prompts.8,11 This means that digital health governance should not be understood as a one-directional process imposed by platforms on passive users. Rather, it operates through the interaction between platform discourse, interface design, and everyday user interpretation.

Algorithmic governance research further shows that digital platforms shape behavior through classification, personalization, recommendation, thresholds, defaults, and feedback.12–14 In health contexts, these mechanisms define what counts as normal, desirable, risky, or insufficient. However, consumer health platforms remain underexamined as everyday sites of algorithmic governance, especially in relation to privacy discourse and users’ lived experiences of control. While platform companies frequently present privacy settings and data permissions as evidence of user agency, less is known about how users interpret these claims as they engage with metrics, goals, and notifications in everyday life.

This article addresses this gap by examining Apple Health through a qualitative, triangulated design that combines critical discourse analysis of Apple’s official “Health App and Privacy” text with semi-structured interviews with Apple Watch and Apple Health users. The study does not assume that user control is either fully empowering or simply false. Instead, it conceptualizes control as bounded, relational, and discursively produced within platform environments. This approach allows the analysis to examine both the functional choices available to users and the broader platform conditions within which those choices are made.

The study contributes to digital health and health communication scholarship in three ways. First, it links corporate privacy discourse to users’ lived self-tracking practices within a single digital health ecosystem. Second, it clarifies how platform-defined metrics, goals, notifications, and permissions shape experiences of autonomy, pressure, trust, and dependence. Third, it develops the concept of bounded control to explain how users may be informed and active while still operating within predefined platform architectures.

Accordingly, this study is guided by the following research questions:RQ1. How does Apple Health’s privacy discourse construct user control, privacy, and responsibility in relation to health data?

RQ2. How do Apple Watch and Apple Health users experience platform feedback, metrics, goals, notifications, and permissions in everyday self-tracking practices?

RQ3. How do corporate privacy claims and user experiences converge or diverge in the production of bounded control in digital health?

Methods

Study design

This study used a qualitative, triangulated research design to examine how user control is constructed and experienced in digital health tracking. The design combined critical discourse analysis of Apple’s official privacy communication with semi-structured interviews with Apple Watch and Apple Health users. This approach enabled a comparison between corporate privacy claims and users’ everyday self-tracking practices.

Data sources

The document corpus consisted of Apple’s official “Health App and Privacy” text published on Apple’s website. This text was selected because it is a user-facing legal and communicative document that explains how health data are collected, processed, shared, and protected within the Apple Health ecosystem. The analyzed version was the publicly available English text dated September 15, 2025.

The interview corpus consisted of semi-structured interviews with Apple Watch and Apple Health users. The interviews focused on users’ experiences with metrics, goals, notifications, permissions, privacy settings, health-related feedback, and self-tracking practices.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling. The study population consisted of active Apple Watch users who regularly engaged with Apple Health features. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being aged between 18 and 33, (2) active Apple Watch use, (3) regular engagement with Apple Health features such as steps, activity rings, sleep, heart rate, workouts, or menstrual cycle tracking, and (4) exposure to app-based feedback and notifications. Users with irregular or minimal engagement with tracking features were excluded.

A total of 18 participants were included in the study. Participants were aged between 18 and 33 years; 13 were female, and 5 were male. Their Apple Health use ranged from 8 months to 4 years, and their frequency of use ranged from occasional to daily. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Data collection

Semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were conducted between November 7, 2025, and December 30, 2025. The interviews were conducted by the second author in locations convenient for participants, with no other individuals present. The interview guide was pilot tested with four users and revised accordingly. Each interview lasted approximately 20 minutes, producing approximately 360 minutes of interview data in total. With participants’ informed consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data collection continued until responses began to recur around core themes, indicating thematic saturation.

Data analysis

The document analysis was conducted using critical discourse analysis informed by Van Dijk’s socio-cognitive approach. The analysis focused on how Apple’s privacy discourse constructs user control, privacy, responsibility, data sharing, and health-related self-management. The coding framework included macro-level framing, micro-level meanings, and rhetorical strategies.

The interview data were analyzed using descriptive thematic analysis. The analysis was organized around six analytical themes aligned with the theoretical framework: (1) self-discipline and self-tracking, (2) quantification of the body and the digital body, (3) algorithmic guidance and behavioral control, (4) personalization and normativization, (5) redefinition of privacy, and (6) affective effects such as trust, pressure, and dependence.

To strengthen analytical rigor, the document analysis was conducted by two independent coders trained in critical discourse analysis. Both coders first analyzed the text independently using a shared coding framework. Coding outputs were then compared to identify convergences, divergences, and ambiguous cases. Discrepancies were discussed in iterative review sessions, and final coding outcomes were established through negotiated consensus.

A structured codebook was developed before the analysis and refined during the coding process. The codebook included category definitions, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and anchor examples. Coding decisions and revisions were documented in an audit trail to support transparency and analytical traceability.

Statistical analysis

No statistical analysis was conducted because this study used a qualitative research design based on critical discourse analysis and semi-structured interviews. Participant characteristics are reported descriptively to contextualize the sample.

Ethical considerations

The study followed standard ethical procedures for qualitative research. All participants received an information sheet, provided informed consent, and were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Interviews were audio-recorded only with explicit permission. All data were anonymized during transcription, and participants are referred to by anonymized identifiers such as P01–P18. Data were stored securely.

Istanbul Aydın University Ethics Committee, Istanbul Aydın University, İstanbul, Turkiye, approved this study. 5026854156, dated 1.10.2025.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was supported through methodological transparency, triangulation, systematic coding procedures, and direct textual and interview evidence. The combination of document analysis and interview data strengthened credibility by enabling comparison between institutional discourse and lived experience. Analytical consistency was supported through a shared coding framework, independent coding, negotiated consensus, and an audit trail.

Results

The results are presented in three parts. First, the critical discourse analysis of Apple’s “Health App and Privacy” text is reported. Second, interview findings are presented to show how users experience metrics, goals, notifications, permissions, and privacy settings in everyday self-tracking. Third, the discourse and interview findings are compared to clarify how bounded control is produced through the interaction of privacy discourse, platform affordances, and user practices.

Apple Health’s privacy discourse: Control, protection, and responsibility

Apple’s privacy discourse constructs user control through a recurring vocabulary of choice, permission, sharing, and management. The user is repeatedly positioned as an active subject who can decide which features to use, what data to share, and which third-party applications may access health information. Statements such as “you remain in control” and “you can choose” frame participation on the platform as a matter of individual agency.

At the same time, this discourse situates choice within predefined platform conditions. The available forms of control are organized through settings, permissions, consent flows, and feature-level options. In this sense, control is not absent, but it is operationalized through choices already structured by the platform. Apple’s privacy discourse, therefore, constructs control as functional and user-facing, while also limiting it to the options made available within the platform architecture.

A second pattern concerns trust and risk allocation. Apple presents itself as a protective actor through security-oriented language, while risk is frequently displaced onto third-party applications and onto users’ decisions about sharing. This creates a privacy narrative in which Apple appears as the secure intermediary and the user becomes responsible for managing external data flows.

A third pattern concerns the datafication of health. The discourse presents health as something that can be brought together, tracked, managed, scheduled, and understood through data. Bodily processes are translated into measurable information, making health appear actionable through dashboards, reminders, and personalized feedback. Everyday self-tracking: Informed use and bounded control

Interview findings indicate that users generally did not describe themselves as deceived or unaware. Many participants understood that Apple Health operates through data collection, permissions, notifications, and predefined tracking categories. Several users described their engagement as pragmatic: they used the platform because it was convenient, motivating, or already integrated into their daily routines.

P8’s statement illustrates this informed but pragmatic orientation: “It feels strange to know that every step I take turns into data, but maybe these data contribute to broader health research.”

This finding is important because it shows that user control should not be framed as a simple illusion or as user ignorance. Participants often recognized the platform’s limitations yet chose to use it. However, this informed use remained bounded by the platform’s predefined categories, metrics, and feedback systems.

Participants described control mainly in terms of visible and practical options, such as turning notifications on or off, checking steps, monitoring heart rate, changing goals, or deciding whether to use particular features. These forms of control were meaningful for users, but they did not extend to deeper knowledge of data processing, algorithmic interpretation, or wider institutional uses of health data.

P8 reported that the intensity of notifications generated a sense of pressure and led them to disable notifications, while P10 described selective responsiveness to prompts by stating: “When I’m inactive, I try to move according to the notifications.”

Metrics, goals, and behavioral feedback

Participants consistently described Apple Health as shaping everyday routines through metrics, goals, and feedback. Steps, heart rate, sleep, activity levels, calories, menstrual cycle tracking, and other indicators became reference points for evaluating the body and daily conduct. In many accounts, health was interpreted less in terms of bodily sensation and more in terms of numerical feedback.

P12 describes how tracked metrics are incorporated into everyday decision-making: “I see how many steps I take during the day and adjust the calories I will consume in my diet accordingly.” Similarly, P15 stated: “Every morning, as soon as I wake up, I check Apple Health… if I don’t, I feel uneasy.”

Notifications and reminders played a significant role in micro-level behavioral steering. Some participants reported responding directly to prompts by walking, moving, checking their activity, or adjusting their routines. These responses suggest that platform feedback can organize conduct without coercion. Users remain formally free to ignore prompts, but the prompts nevertheless structure attention, mood, and action.

P15’s remark, “When the watch warns me, I get up and walk,” shows how notifications translate directly into action. P18 similarly explained: “When I see the bedtime reminder, I think I should start getting ready to sleep… when the notification says it’s time to stand up at the office, I get up and drink water.”

Affective responses: Motivation, pressure, guilt, and dependence

The interviews also showed that self-tracking produced ambivalent affective responses. Participants associated Apple Health with motivation, reassurance, and a sense of awareness. For some, the platform made health more visible and helped them maintain routines. However, the same feedback mechanisms also produced pressure, guilt, anxiety, or dependence when goals were not achieved or when data were missing.

Several participants associated tracking with reassurance and motivation. P4 stated that “checking my heartbeats and pulse makes me feel safe,” while P5 described continuous measurement as “feeling psychologically safe.”

P2’s statement, “When I can’t reach it, I see myself as a very useless person,” shows how unmet goals can generate negative self-evaluation. Dependence also appears in P10’s account: “Dependence… when I don’t wear it, it feels like something is missing on my arm.”

This ambivalence is central to the study’s argument. The platform does not simply dominate users, nor do users exercise unrestricted autonomy. Rather, user agency operates within a structured environment in which metrics and feedback shape how users evaluate themselves.

Privacy, trust, and normalization of data sharing

Participants expressed mixed attitudes toward privacy. Some trusted Apple because of its brand reputation, security messaging, or perceived difference from other technology companies. Others reported uncertainty about where their data might go, how it might be used, or whether third parties could access it. In several interviews, privacy concerns were acknowledged but normalized as part of everyday digital participation.

Trust was often expressed through the sense that the application offered reliable oversight. P10 stated, “I feel as if I am being monitored by a doctor,” while P4 explained that “checking my heartbeats and pulse makes me feel safe.”

Participants also articulated uncertainty and concern about future uses of their data. P10 stated, “The fact that it knows me this much… can make me anxious,” while P16 noted, “I foresee that my data could be shared commercially when necessary.”

P16’s statement, “I think there is no escape from this,” makes visible an acceptance-oriented understanding of data sharing as an unavoidable part of digital health participation.

These findings show that privacy is not experienced as a fixed boundary. Instead, it is negotiated through trust, convenience, resignation, and perceived inevitability. Users may be aware of privacy risks without withdrawing from the platform.

Resistance, disengagement, and selective use

Although platform feedback shaped everyday conduct, participants also described moments of resistance or selective disengagement. Some ignored notifications, disabled prompts, checked the app only occasionally, or treated the data as approximate rather than authoritative. These practices show that users are not passive subjects of algorithmic governance.

P8’s account shows that users may also disengage from platform prompts: the intensity of notifications generated a sense of pressure and led them to disable notifications. This indicates that users can resist or selectively manage feedback, even if such resistance remains within the platform’s available settings.

However, resistance generally occurred within the platform’s available options. Users could refuse, mute, ignore, or selectively engage, but they could not redesign the platform’s categories, metrics, or data logic. This supports the concept of bounded control: users are active and informed, but their agency is exercised within a predefined technical and discursive environment.

Convergence and divergence between corporate discourse and lived experience

The comparison between Apple’s privacy discourse and user interviews reveals both convergence and divergence. Corporate discourse frames control through choice, protection, and permission. Users also describe control in practical terms, especially through settings, notifications, and selective use. In this respect, the platform’s control discourse is not entirely detached from user experience.

However, divergence appears in the limits of this control. Apple’s discourse presents control as clear, manageable, and user-centered, whereas interview findings show that users often experience control as partial, routine-based, and shaped by platform prompts. Users may trust the platform and understand its limits, but they still engage with health through categories, thresholds, goals, and feedback loops defined by the system.

Overall, the results suggest that Apple Health produces bounded control. Users are neither simply misled nor fully autonomous. They make informed and pragmatic choices, but these choices are structured by platform-defined forms of visibility, measurement, feedback, and privacy management.

Discussion

This study examined how privacy discourse, platform design, and everyday self-tracking practices intersect to shape user control within Apple Health. The findings show that control in digital health should not be understood as either fully empowering or simply false. Apple Health provides users with functional options, including settings, permissions, notification controls, and selective feature engagement. At the same time, these choices are organized within a predefined platform architecture that shapes what can be seen, measured, compared, shared, and acted upon. The study, therefore, conceptualizes user control as bounded, relational, and discursively produced.

The first contribution of the study is to refine debates on digital health, self-tracking, and governmentality by showing how self-regulation is organized through apparently voluntary practices. Foucauldian accounts of governmentality emphasize that contemporary power often operates by encouraging individuals to regulate themselves according to socially and institutionally defined norms.3–6 Apple Health exemplifies this logic through metrics, goals, notifications, dashboards, and feedback loops that encourage users to evaluate their bodies and conduct through platform-defined indicators. However, the findings also show that users are not passive subjects. They interpret, negotiate, ignore, disable, or selectively follow platform prompts. This supports a more nuanced understanding of digital health governance: it operates not by eliminating agency, but by structuring the conditions under which agency is exercised.

The second contribution concerns the concept of control. The findings do not support a binary distinction between real control and false control. Instead, they show that user control is meaningful at the level of practical interaction but limited at the level of platform architecture and data logic. Users may decide whether to activate features, respond to notifications, change settings, or disengage from specific prompts. Yet these decisions occur within categories, thresholds, and data-processing arrangements defined by the platform. In this sense, control is not absent; it is bounded. This finding addresses the need to distinguish among functional control over settings, perceived control in everyday use, rhetorical control in corporate privacy discourse, and governance control exercised through behavioral steering.

The study also contributes to algorithmic governance research by showing how platform power operates through feedback and affect rather than direct coercion. Existing research has emphasized classification, personalization, recommendation, and modulation as key mechanisms of algorithmic governance.12–14 The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating how these mechanisms are experienced through motivation, reassurance, pressure, guilt, and dependence. Apple Health’s goal-notification-feedback cycle does not force users to behave in a particular way, but it makes certain forms of conduct more visible, desirable, and emotionally consequential. This affective dimension is central to understanding how self-tracking becomes self-regulation. Apple Health can also be understood through the personal informatics model developed by Li, Dey, and Forlizzi, who describe self-tracking systems through five stages: “preparation, collection, integration, reflection, and action”. 15 Apple Health follows this sequence by collecting bodily data, integrating them into dashboards, encouraging reflection through metrics and trends, and prompting action through goals, reminders, and notifications. This model helps clarify why user control is bounded: users may act on the data, but the stages through which data become meaningful are already organized by the platform.

The findings further refine the literature on personalization and normativity in digital health. Apple Health presents feedback as individualized and user-centered, but participants often interpreted goals, metrics, and notifications as benchmarks against which they evaluated themselves. This suggests that personalization and standardization are not opposites. Personalized feedback can become a route through which generalized norms of activity, productivity, and self-optimization are internalized. This supports previous work on the quantified self and datafication, which shows how bodily experience is translated into measurable indicators that can reorganize self-understanding.1,2,11

A further contribution of the study is its connection of consumer health platforms to broader transformations in surveillance governance. Lund-Tønnesen’s concept of digital surveillance governance is useful here because it shows how personal data becomes a governing resource through control, capture, transparency, and data cooperation across organizational settings. 16 Apple Health should not be treated as equivalent to public-sector surveillance systems. It is a consumer platform, and users engage with it through voluntary adoption, convenience, brand trust, and everyday health goals. Nevertheless, the case illustrates how intimate personal data are increasingly normalized as resources for classification, coordination, prediction, and behavioral steering. The importance of Apple Health lies not only in what the platform does internally, but also in how it makes continuous health data capture appear ordinary, useful, and manageable. This normalization can also be read in relation to van Dijck’s argument that “metadata and data have become a regular currency” in digital societies. 17 In the context of Apple Health, users do not necessarily experience data exchange as exceptional or invasive. Instead, the exchange is often framed as the ordinary cost of convenience, personalization, and better self-knowledge. This helps explain why participants could express privacy concerns while continuing to use the platform.

This broader perspective helps clarify the role of privacy discourse. Apple’s privacy communication does not merely conceal datafication; it also legitimizes it by framing participation in terms of protection, choice, and user agency. The discourse constructs Apple as a trustworthy intermediary and places responsibility for sharing decisions on the user, particularly regarding third-party applications. Interview findings show that this discourse partially aligns with user experience: some participants do feel protected, informed, and able to manage visible settings. However, the same participants may also express uncertainty, resignation, or limited knowledge about wider data flows. Privacy is therefore experienced less as a stable boundary than as a negotiated condition shaped by trust, convenience, uncertainty, and normalization. This finding can also be strengthened through Nissenbaum’s theory of contextual integrity. Nissenbaum argues that privacy depends on “appropriate restrictions on information flows”, 18 rather than on secrecy or individual choice alone. This perspective is useful for understanding Apple Health because users can control visible permissions and sharing settings, yet have limited knowledge of how health data move across devices, applications, users, and healthcare contexts. Therefore, the issue is not simply whether users consent, but whether the circulation of intimate health data remains contextually appropriate, transparent, and normatively justified.

The findings also qualify deterministic critiques of platform capitalism. While datafication and behavioral steering are central to Apple Health, users are not simply captured without awareness. Many participants were aware that the platform collects data and that their choices are limited. Some accepted this as a pragmatic exchange; others ignored or disabled notifications; some used the app selectively. These practices matter because they show that bounded control includes both compliance and negotiation. The critical issue is not that users falsely believe they possess complete autonomy, but that the autonomy available to them is organized through platform-defined forms of visibility, choice, and evaluation. This interpretation is consistent with Sharon and Zandbergen’s critique of “data fetishism,” which they describe as a limiting explanation of self-tracking practices. 19 The participants in this study did not treat Apple Health data as unquestionable truth. Rather, they used the platform pragmatically, selectively, and sometimes ambivalently. This supports the study’s central argument that Apple Health does not simply deceive users or fully determine their conduct. Instead, it creates a structured environment in which users negotiate data, convenience, trust, pressure, and partial autonomy.

For health communication scholarship, the study shows that privacy and control claims should be analyzed not only as legal or technical statements, but also as persuasive communication. Apple’s privacy discourse shapes how users understand responsibility, trust, risk, and agency regarding health data. In digital health environments, communication does not merely describe platform practices; it helps make those practices acceptable and actionable. Evaluating digital health platforms, therefore, requires attention to how control is communicated, operationalized, and experienced.

The study has several limitations. First, it focuses on a single platform, Apple Health, and therefore does not claim to represent all digital health platforms or wearable ecosystems. Second, the document corpus is limited to Apple’s official “Health App and Privacy” text; future studies could include interface walkthroughs, app notifications, advertising materials, developer documentation, and policy updates. Third, the interview sample was relatively small and consisted of users aged 18–33, many of whom were students. The findings, therefore, reflect a specific group of relatively young, digitally familiar users rather than the general population of digital health users. Fourth, the study relies on self-reported interview data and does not include longitudinal observation of actual app use. Future research could compare different age groups, health conditions, cultural contexts, and platform ecosystems, and could combine interviews with interface analysis, diary methods, or digital trace data.

Overall, this study shows that Apple Health’s model of control is best understood as bounded rather than illusory in a simple sense. Users are not necessarily deceived; many are informed, pragmatic, and selective. Yet their agency is exercised within a platform environment that defines the available metrics, choices, thresholds, and feedback mechanisms through which health is made visible and actionable. This is the central governance dynamic identified by the study: digital health platforms do not eliminate autonomy, but organize it. 20

Conclusion

This study examined Apple Health as a digital health communication infrastructure in which privacy discourse, platform design, and everyday self-tracking practices intersect. By combining critical discourse analysis of Apple’s “Health App and Privacy” text with interviews with Apple Watch and Apple Health users, the study showed how user control is constructed, communicated, and experienced within a platform-based health environment.

The findings suggest that control in Apple Health is neither entirely absent nor fully autonomous. Users have functional forms of control, including managing settings, permissions, and notifications, as well as selectively engaging with platform features. However, these choices are exercised within a predefined platform architecture that determines which forms of health data are made visible, how they are measured, and how they are translated into goals, prompts, and feedback.

The central contribution of the study is the concept of bounded control. This concept captures the coexistence of user agency and platform constraint. Users may be informed, pragmatic, and selective in their engagement with Apple Health, but their agency remains structured by platform-defined categories, thresholds, and data-processing conditions. In this sense, digital health governance does not eliminate autonomy; rather, it organizes autonomy through specific technical, discursive, and affective mechanisms.

For digital health and health communication scholarship, the study shows that privacy and control claims should be examined not only as legal or technical statements, but also as persuasive forms of communication. Apple’s privacy discourse helps make continuous health data collection appear manageable, secure, and user-centered, while interface features such as metrics, goals, and notifications translate this discourse into everyday self-regulation.

The study also shows that consumer health platforms should be situated within broader debates on datafication, algorithmic governance, and digital surveillance governance. Apple Health is not equivalent to public-sector surveillance systems, but it illustrates how intimate personal data can become normalized as a resource for classification, coordination, and behavioral steering in everyday life.

Overall, the study demonstrates that user control in digital health is best understood as bounded, managed, and discursively legitimized. Future research should examine how this model of control operates across different platforms, age groups, health conditions, and cultural contexts, and should combine interview-based approaches with interface analysis, longitudinal observation, and comparative platform studies.
