ABSTRACT

Pulmonary cryptococcosis (PC) in apparently immunocompetent hosts—now the majority of non‐HIV cases in Asia—cannot be explained by the traditional opportunistic infection paradigm. Three epidemiological observations point toward occult immune susceptibility: familial clustering documented in multiple case reports and small family studies despite shared environmental exposure, normal routine immunological screening, and species‐specific infection patterns between Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii. We propose a three‐layer framework: upstream genetic variants in pattern recognition, Fcγ receptor, complement‐lectin, and macrophage‐regulatory loci (Layer 1) act in concert with intermediate immune phenotypes (Layer 2)—the genetically influenced IL‐17/Th17 axis and IgG2 subclass deficiencies, and acquired anti‐GM‐CSF neutralising autoantibodies—that are proposed to converge on alveolar macrophage dysfunction and defective pulmonary fungal clearance (Layer 3). The three Layer 2 phenotypes differ substantially in evidence strength: anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies have reached clinical validation; IgG2 deficiency remains a mechanistic hypothesis; and IL‐17/Th17 deficiency is supported by a single unreplicated study and should be treated as preliminary. This framework is hypothesis‐generating, derived from candidate‐gene studies, limited immunophenotyping data, and animal models; it has not been validated by genome‐wide association studies or prospective functional research, and its clinical value requires confirmation by higher‐quality evidence.

1 Introduction

Pulmonary cryptococcosis (PC) has long been classified as an opportunistic infection, primarily affecting immunocompromised individuals. Three epidemiological observations challenge this paradigm. First, familial clustering of PC has been reported among first‐degree relatives sharing common environmental exposure, whereas co‐residing non‐relatives remained unaffected—an asymmetry that implicates host genetic susceptibility rather than differential exposure [1, 2]. Systematic immunological survey of asymptomatic relatives in affected families has revealed subclinical immune abnormalities in some family members, suggesting a genetically determined predisposition that is not detectable by routine clinical assessment [3]. Second, systematic immunological evaluation of apparently immunocompetent PC patients—here defined as individuals without known HIV infection, solid organ transplantation, haematological malignancy, or long‐term immunosuppressive therapy—frequently fails to identify defects on standard panels; targeted assays—IL‐17 production capacity or anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibody detection—are absent from routine clinical workups [4, 5], and susceptibility‐associated genetic polymorphisms are not detectable by phenotypic screening [6]. Third, although Cryptococcus neoformans predominantly infects immunocompromised hosts globally, it causes localised pulmonary disease in apparently immunocompetent Han Chinese adults, mirroring the epidemiological pattern of Cryptococcus gattii infection in apparently immunocompetent hosts [7]. This inter‐species epidemiological convergence suggests that host genetic background, rather than pathogen identity, is the primary determinant of susceptibility. Together, these observations indicate that a subset of apparently immunocompetent individuals harbour an occult immune‐susceptibility state (occult immunodeficiency)—undetectable by standard testing and potentially of both genetic and acquired origin—that constitutes the primary predisposing basis for PC in this population.

In mainland China, nearly 70% of PC cases occur in patients without identifiable underlying disease, with HIV accounting for fewer than 4% [7]. The 2026 multicentre cohort by Gu et al. [8] of 395 HIV‐negative patients confirmed that 57.1% were immunocompetent, of whom 93.8% presented with localised pulmonary disease. Similar patterns are observed across East and Southeast Asian populations sharing a high proportion of individuals with East Asian genetic ancestry [9, 10, 11].

This epidemiological profile drives systematic diagnostic delay. The international multicentre MSG‐11 study demonstrated that apparently immunocompetent patients without underlying immunosuppression had a median diagnostic interval of 67 days, compared with 19.7 days for solid organ transplant recipients and 31.3 days for haematological malignancy patients [12]. Pulmonary lesions characteristically present as incidentally detected solitary nodules, frequently misdiagnosed as primary lung malignancy or tuberculosis [13, 14]. The predominant driver of this delay is a clinical cognitive bias—the expectation that PC is an opportunistic infection—rather than the intrinsic rarity of disease, highlighting a fundamental gap in current immune‐status–based risk stratification.

The absence of validated genetic biomarkers to identify high‐risk hosts underlies this diagnostic gap. Precedents from other invasive fungal infections confirm that systematic genetic susceptibility profiling is technically feasible and clinically translatable [15]: biallelic CARD9 deficiency, gain‐of‐function STAT1 mutations and the Dectin‐1 Y238X variant define discrete susceptibility pathways for candidiasis [16, 17, 18], whereas PTX3 haplotype variants predict invasive aspergillosis risk in transplant recipients [19]. These precedents predominantly involve rare monogenic defects or immunocompromised populations, distinguishing them from the polygenic susceptibility context of PC in apparently immunocompetent hosts; nevertheless, they establish the principle that fungal infection susceptibility is genetically tractable.

Preliminary studies in Han Chinese populations have implicated variants in MBL2, FCGR2B, CLEC6A (Dectin‐2) and PTX3 in cryptococcal susceptibility, though the molecular mechanisms linking these variants to downstream immune dysfunction have only recently begun to be characterised. This review examines whether, and through what mechanisms, upstream genetic variants and acquired immune defects might act through intermediate immune phenotypes to impair alveolar macrophage–mediated fungal clearance in apparently immunocompetent hosts. We propose a three‐layer conceptual framework (Figure 1): Layer 1 comprises upstream genetic variants involving pattern recognition, complement‐lectin pathway, Fc receptor, and macrophage‐regulatory loci; Layer 2 encompasses intermediate immune phenotypes—comprising both genetically influenced defects (IL‐17/Th17 axis deficiency and IgG2 subclass deficiency) and an acquired phenocopy of inborn errors of immunity (anti‐GM‐CSF neutralising autoantibodies)—that represent measurable intermediates converging on impaired host defence; Layer 3 represents the common effector endpoint of alveolar macrophage dysfunction and defective pulmonary fungal clearance. This framework is a hypothesis‐generating model derived from candidate‐gene association studies, limited‐scale immunophenotyping data, and animal models, not a prospectively validated causal map; its value lies in providing an integrated interpretive structure for dispersed evidence and identifying priority research gaps.

2 Layer 1: Genetic Predisposition

2.1 Pattern Recognition and Fungal Sensing Genes

2.1.1 Dectin‐2 (CLEC6A)

The most direct human genetic evidence for Layer 1 susceptibility to pulmonary cryptococcosis derives from Hu et al. [20], and—uniquely among the loci reviewed here—comes from an apparently immunocompetent pulmonary cryptococcosis cohort rather than meningitis patients. In 251 cases and 464 controls, they identified a significant depletion of the CLEC6A rs11045418 C/T heterozygous genotype (overdominance model; OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.40–0.87), indicating a protective effect of heterozygosity. The association was organ‐specific, with no equivalent signal in meningitis patients—consistent with the expression pattern of Dectin‐2, which is predominantly found on tissue‐resident macrophages, but is notably absent from brain microglia and meningeal macrophages [21], offering a mechanistic basis for pulmonary but not CNS‐restricted susceptibility.

At the immune phenotype level (Layer 2), Dectin‐2 recognises fungal high‐mannose structures and signals through an FcRγ chain–Syk–CARD9–NF‐κB axis to drive IL‐1β and IL‐23 production and downstream Th17 differentiation—a pathway firmly established in dendritic cell models of Candida infection [22]. In murine infection models, Dectin‐2 deficiency accelerates Th2 polarisation and IL‐4–dependent mucin production following pulmonary C. neoformans challenge [23], whereas CARD9 signalling is required for early accumulation of CD69+ memory and effector T cells and innate‐phase IFN‐γ production in infected lungs [24], positioning this axis as a bridge between pattern recognition and early adaptive immunity.

At the macrophage effector level (Layer 3), Dectin‐2 deficiency impairs dendritic cell phagocytosis of C. neoformans through a CARD9‐dependent mechanism [25], and CARD9 deletion abrogates classical M1 macrophage activation, blocking iNOS/NO production and substantially reducing antifungal killing [26]. Among the candidate loci reviewed here, CLEC6A rs11045418 provides the most internally consistent three‐layer mechanistic chain—from pattern recognition receptor variation through CARD9 signalling impairment to macrophage M1 activation failure—though the complete causal pathway has not been established in primary human alveolar macrophages carrying this variant.

2.2 Fcγ Receptor Signalling (FCGR2B)

The genetic evidence for FCGR2B derives exclusively from cryptococcal meningitis cohorts, not pulmonary disease. Hu et al. identified significant over‐representation of the FCGR2B 232I/I genotype in 117 HIV‐negative Han Chinese patients with cryptococcal meningitis (OR = 1.652, 95% CI: 1.02–2.67), with protective under‐representation of the 232I/T heterozygote (OR = 0.542, 95% CI: 0.33–0.90); both effects held in the subgroup without underlying disease [27]. The population‐specific distribution of FCGR2B and MBL2 polymorphisms in Han Chinese may jointly account for the disproportionate burden of cryptococcosis in HIV‐negative apparently immunocompetent patients in China [7]. Ethnic specificity is further underscored by data from predominantly Caucasian cohorts, in which FCGR2A 131R/R and FCGR3A 158 V/V—rather than FCGR2B 232I/I—are over‐represented among HIV‐negative patients [28], and FCGR3A 158F/V associates with risk in white but not Han Chinese patients [6]. All existing FCGR2B genetic evidence derives from meningitis cohorts; no study has examined FCGR polymorphisms specifically in immunocompetent pulmonary PC, and the extrapolation of meningitis‐derived associations to pulmonary disease—two distinct immunological endpoints—remains methodologically unvalidated.

At the immune phenotype level (Layer 2), FcγRIIb −/− mice infected with C. neoformans exhibit markedly elevated mortality with multi‐organ dissemination; FcγRIIb‐deficient macrophages show increased TNF‐α and IL‐6 with reduced IL‐10, indicating dysregulated inflammatory control [29]. Glucuronoxylomannan (GXM) engages FcγRIIB to activate JNK/p38–c‐Jun signalling, upregulating FasL and inducing T‐cell apoptosis, thereby suppressing adaptive immunity [30]. Both observations carry methodological caveats: the murine data derive from intravenous rather than respiratory infection models, and the GXM signalling data are from the MonoMac6 cell line without primary‐cell validation.

At the macrophage effector level (Layer 3), FcγRIIb −/− macrophages display higher phagocytic indices than wild‐type cells yet equivalent intracellular killing, resulting in increased organ fungal burden—consistent with a Trojan‐horse dissemination mechanism driven by excess phagocytosis without commensurate clearance [29]. The functional balance of the FcγR system, rather than phagocytic quantity per se, is the determinant of macrophage clearance efficiency; whether FCGR2B 232I/I disrupts this balance in primary human alveolar macrophages remains unestablished.

2.3 Toll‐Like Receptor 2 (TLR2)

Like FCGR2B, the genetic evidence for TLR2 originates solely from a cryptococcal meningitis cohort. Jiang et al. reported significant over‐representation of the TLR2 rs3804099 T/T genotype in non‐HIV cryptococcal meningitis patients from Fujian, China (OR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.02–2.11), with the variant associating with greater disease severity—lower GCS scores, higher early mortality risk, and upregulation of 18 pro‐inflammatory cytokines in CSF—rather than incident susceptibility per se [31]. Critically, the study enrolled only meningitis patients; whether TLR2 polymorphisms influence the risk of developing localised pulmonary disease in apparently immunocompetent hosts has not been examined in any published cohort, constituting the primary research gap for this locus. No replication data from other ethnic populations are available.

At the immune phenotype level (Layer 2), TLR2 −/− and MyD88 −/− mice show markedly increased mortality and organ fungal burden following low‐inoculum C. neoformans challenge, with reduced type‐1 cytokine production—an effect absent in TLR4‐deficient animals, confirming pathway specificity [32, 33]. Whether these murine phenotypes translate to human immune dysregulation in TLR2 polymorphism carriers remains unknown.

At the macrophage effector level (Layer 3), TLR2 signalling supports macrophage TNF‐α and IL‐12 production required for fungal containment; its deficiency reduces phagocytic clearance in vivo [32]. The available mechanistic data, however, derive from knockout models and pharmacological stimulation studies rather than from genotype‐stratified human cell experiments.

TLR2 is best positioned within the three‐layer model as a disease‐severity modifier rather than a primary susceptibility determinant, and direct genetic evidence linking rs3804099 to pulmonary PC incidence in immunocompetent hosts is entirely absent.

2.4 Complement‐Lectin Pathway Candidates: MBL2 and PTX3

2.4.1 MBL2

The genetic association for MBL2, like that for FCGR2B and TLR2, derives from a cryptococcal meningitis cohort rather than pulmonary disease. Ou et al. identified significant over‐representation of the homozygous MBL2 coding‐region deficiency genotype (O/O) in Han Chinese patients with HIV‐negative cryptococcal meningitis versus healthy controls (7.8% vs. 1.9%; OR = 4.29), with a stronger effect in the apparently immunocompetent subgroup (OR = 6.65) [34]. This association has not been replicated in non‐Chinese populations: two independent cohorts—Australian HIV‐negative patients [35] and a Thai HIV‐infected natural history cohort [36]—found no significant association between MBL deficiency and cryptococcal disease risk. The genetic effect therefore appears population‐specific, potentially confined to Han Chinese individuals with particular linkage disequilibrium patterns. A further conceptual limitation constrains mechanistic inference: available functional data predominantly describe pathogen‐mediated evasion of MBL recognition—wild‐type C. neoformans actively resists MBL binding, whereas cell‐wall integrity mutants show markedly increased MBL engagement [37]—rather than the immunological consequences of host MBL deficiency. The direct effect of genetically determined MBL insufficiency on opsonophagocytic function in primary human alveolar macrophages has not been established.

2.4.2 PTX3

Zhang et al. [38] identified an association between PTX3 rs2305619 AA genotype and cryptococcosis risk in HIV‐negative Han Chinese patients (OR = 2.579; OR = 4.399 in the apparently immunocompetent subgroup), yet AA carriers paradoxically showed markedly elevated plasma PTX3 protein levels, suggesting the variant affects functional quality rather than expression. Tang et al., examining a partially overlapping locus (rs3816527), found no association of rs2305619 with pulmonary cryptococcosis and identified a protective AA homozygote signal at rs3816527—results directly inconsistent with Zhang et al. [39]. Both studies are limited to small Han Chinese cohorts with no independent replication. PTX3 functional evidence in cryptococcal infection is sparse: pulmonary cryptococcosis patients show PTX3 levels approximating healthy controls, substantially lower than in pulmonary aspergillosis [40], indicating that the well‐characterised PTX3–aspergillosis axis cannot be directly extrapolated to cryptococcal disease.

Both MBL2 and PTX3 are included in the framework on mechanistic plausibility grounds; neither provides a validated causal chain from Layer 1 variant to Layer 3 macrophage dysfunction in primary human cells.

2.4.3 Colony‐Stimulating Factor 1 (CSF1)

The genetic evidence for CSF1 carries the most severe representational limitation of any locus reviewed: it derives exclusively from HIV‐infected patients with cryptococcal meningitis. Kannambath et al. identified six upstream CSF1 loci associated with cryptococcal disease susceptibility at genome‐wide significance (p < 10−6) in HIV‐infected South African patients, with the protective rs1999713 variant replicated in an independent cohort (OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.42–0.66) [41]. This constitutes the strongest statistical genetic signal among the loci reviewed here, yet it carries a fundamental representational limitation: all evidence derives from HIV‐infected patients with cryptococcal meningitis, and no data exist linking CSF1 variants to pulmonary cryptococcosis in apparently immunocompetent hosts. Whether the susceptibility mechanism identified in advanced HIV disease is relevant to the very different immunological context of immunocompetent patients remain entirely speculative. Transcriptomic data from the same cohort suggest that M‐CSF signalling associates with enhanced macrophage antifungal responses [41], though direct functional validation in primary human alveolar macrophages is lacking—Reardon et al. [42] found no significant effect of M‐CSF on cryptococcal killing capacity in human alveolar macrophages, a finding that fundamentally limits extrapolation of the animal model data to the pulmonary context.

At the macrophage effector level (Layer 3), the net effect of CSF1R signalling on pulmonary cryptococcal control is not straightforwardly protective. Mohamed et al. demonstrated that pharmacological CSF1R inhibition with PLX5622 depleted MHCIIhi interstitial lung macrophages and paradoxically reduced pulmonary fungal burden and CNS dissemination [43]. This subpopulation expands through monocyte recruitment during infection rather than local proliferation and appears to function as an intracellular reservoir that sustains infection rather than as a clearance effector. This finding directly challenges a linear model in which stronger CSF1R signalling improves outcome; the net effect depends on which macrophage subpopulation is affected.

CSF1 is therefore unique among the candidate loci reviewed: its genetic evidence comes exclusively from an HIV‐infected meningitis cohort, and its Layer 3 functional data point in the opposite direction to naive expectation, leaving the directionality of its contribution to susceptibility unresolved.

2.5 Cross‐Pathway Integration and Evidence Limitations

The candidate loci do not operate independently. The Dectin‐2/CARD9 and TLR2/MyD88 axes both converge on NF‐κB–dependent pro‐inflammatory cytokine production, and the net immune phenotype produced by a defect in either pathway is likely modulated by the functional state of the other—a form of epistatic buffering that single‐locus association studies cannot capture. The FCGR2B 232I/I gain‐of‐function variant may compound susceptibility when superimposed on reduced Dectin‐2 or TLR2 signalling, as GXM‐driven FcγRIIB immunosuppression would be further amplified in an already recognition‐deficient background. MBL2 deficiency impairs complement‐mediated opsonophagocytosis while FCGR polymorphisms reduce antibody‐driven phagocytic signalling; both converge on the same macrophage recognition‐uptake node, yet no study has systematically assessed the combined genotype effect on alveolar macrophage clearance efficiency. Partial functional redundancy between PTX3 and MBL2 in the lectin‐complement pathway offers a plausible explanation for the population heterogeneity of MBL2 association studies: where PTX3 function is intact, MBL2 deficiency may be phenotypically silent, and vice versa.

A structural asymmetry in the source of evidence pervades the entire Layer 1 dataset and warrants explicit emphasis. Dectin‐2 is the only locus with direct evidence from an apparently immunocompetent pulmonary cryptococcosis cohort, whereas PTX3 has pulmonary‐disease data of conflicting and limited strength. The remaining four—FCGR2B, MBL2, TLR2 and CSF1—derive their genetic evidence exclusively from cryptococcal meningitis cohorts, and in the case of CSF1, solely from HIV‐infected meningitis patients. Cryptococcal meningitis and localised pulmonary disease represent distinct immunological endpoints along the infection trajectory: meningitis reflects failure of containment and CNS dissemination, whereas immunocompetent pulmonary cryptococcosis reflects a more restricted, granuloma‐forming host response. Direct extrapolation of meningitis‐derived genetic associations to pulmonary susceptibility is therefore methodologically unsound, and the relative contribution of each locus to pulmonary versus disseminated disease cannot be resolved from current data. This source asymmetry—rather than the conceptual structure of the three‐layer model—is the principal limitation of the present genetic evidence base, and resolving it requires dedicated genotyping of well‐characterised immunocompetent pulmonary cohorts with species stratification.

3 Layer 2: Intermediate Immune Phenotypes

It is important to frame these phenotypes within current classifications of immune deficiency. Under the global cryptococcosis management framework, the host factors predisposing apparently immunocompetent individuals to cryptococcosis are increasingly recognised as rare primary immunodeficiencies, encompassing single‐nucleotide polymorphisms, inborn errors of immunity (IEI), and anti‐cytokine autoantibodies. Critically, the three Layer 2 phenotypes are not of uniform origin. IL‐17/Th17 axis deficiency and IgG2 subclass deficiency are genetically influenced and, in their monogenic forms, fall within the IEI spectrum. Anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies, by contrast, are now classified by the International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) as phenocopies of inborn errors of immunity: unlike germline IEI, which are typically of inherited origin and manifest in childhood, immunodeficiency due to anti‐cytokine autoantibodies is usually acquired in adulthood and reproduces the clinical phenotype of the cognate genetic defect. Patients with anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies are therefore best understood as adult‐onset immunodeficiency hosts rather than carriers of a germline lesion. We retain all three within the Layer 2 tier because they converge functionally on the same downstream effector, whereas making explicit that they differ fundamentally in origin—genetic versus acquired—and that this distinction, rather than a shared causal genetic pathway, defines the layer.

3.1 Anti‐GM‐CSF Autoantibodies: The Anchor Layer 2 Phenotype

Among the three Layer 2 phenotypes, anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies are supported by the most robust and clinically coherent evidence. As detailed above, they represent an acquired, adult‐onset phenocopy of inborn errors of immunity rather than a downstream consequence of a defined Layer 1 genetic variant; they converge on the same Layer 3 effector failure as the genetically influenced phenotypes—neutralising antibodies block GM‐CSF–STAT5 signalling, impair phagolysosomal maturation, and abolish intracellular fungal killing, the defining features of alveolar macrophage dysfunction [44]. Whether host genetic background influences susceptibility to developing these autoantibodies remains unknown and is not currently supported by direct evidence.

The clinical evidence is internally consistent across independent cohorts. Rosen et al. first identified neutralising anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies in apparently immunocompetent patients with cryptococcal meningitis, establishing that these antibodies confer susceptibility through GM‐CSF signal blockade independently of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis [44]. Kuo et al. [45] subsequently reported autoantibody positivity in 6 of 23 HIV‐negative patients with unusual or disseminated cryptococcosis; all five culture‐positive cases yielded C. gattii VGII, and the clinical phenotype encompassed isolated pulmonary disease in three patients and localised musculoskeletal involvement in one, confirming that this immune phenotype predisposes to pulmonary and extrapulmonary rather than exclusively meningeal disease. Jiang et al. [46] extended these observations in a larger cohort, demonstrating that autoantibody positivity independently predicts one‐year mortality in non‐HIV cryptococcal meningitis.

Mechanistically, patient‐derived antibodies directly suppress STAT5 phosphorylation and MIP‐1α production in macrophages, producing quantifiable phagocytic and killing defects in vitro [45]. In animal models, GM‐CSF deficiency impairs monocyte‐to‐macrophage differentiation and abrogates neutrophil recruitment, substantially worsening pulmonary C. gattii infection [47].

Regarding clinical management, Kuo et al.'s [45] longitudinal follow‐up showed that standard antifungal therapy—amphotericin B with or without flucytosine induction followed by fluconazole consolidation—yields favourable outcomes, with relapse being uncommon after treatment completion despite persistent autoantibody titres. Immunomodulatory interventions targeting the autoantibody itself, including rituximab, GM‐CSF supplementation, and plasmapheresis, have been considered in refractory cases but are rarely required given the generally satisfactory antifungal response. Long‐term surveillance for pulmonary alveolar proteinosis is warranted given the shared pathophysiological mechanism. Anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibody testing is currently the only Layer 2 assessment with a clear clinical rationale in non‐HIV patients with unusual or severe cryptococcosis, and standardisation of testing protocols across centres remains an unmet need. In contrast, polygenic risk scoring, IgG2 subclass profiling, and IL‐17/Th17 functional testing remain investigational; they are proposed here as research priorities and are not recommended for routine clinical use at present.

3.2 IL‐17/Th17 Axis Deficiency

Th17 cells and their signature cytokines IL‐17A/F contribute to mucosal defence against inhaled fungal pathogens, promoting neutrophil recruitment, epithelial barrier integrity, and antimicrobial peptide production at respiratory surfaces. Genetic variants affecting upstream pattern recognition receptors and CARD9 signalling may attenuate Th17 differentiation by reducing IL‐6 and IL‐23 output, though this mechanistic link has been established in candidiasis models and has not been directly demonstrated in individuals carrying cryptococcosis‐associated polymorphisms [26, 48, 49].

The sole direct human evidence for IL‐17/Th17 deficiency as a Layer 2 phenotype in cryptococcosis comes from Townsend et al., who identified reduced CD4+IL‐17+ T‐cell frequencies and lower circulating IL‐17 levels in eight apparently immunocompetent patients with cryptococcal disease [4]. This finding is preliminary: the study enrolled eight patients, lacked a matched control group of sufficient size, and has not been independently replicated; it should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory evidence for this phenotype.

In murine models, IL‐17A enhances classical macrophage activation and IFN‐γ production during pulmonary cryptococcal infection, and its absence promotes alternative macrophage polarisation with impaired fungal clearance [50]. Vaccine studies further associate Th17 induction with multi‐functional CD4+ responses and improved fungal control [51].

IL‐17/Th17 deficiency remains the weakest‐evidenced of the three Layer 2 phenotypes. Establishing its relevance to pulmonary cryptococcosis in immunocompetent hosts will require larger cohort studies with species stratification and functional macrophage readouts alongside immunophenotyping.

3.3 IgG2 Subclass Deficiency

Candidate‐gene studies have linked FCGR2B variants to altered IgG subclass distribution in HIV‐negative patients, and reduced circulating IgG2 has been associated with cryptococcal meningitis in this population [52], implicating a genetically influenced shift in antibody isotype profile as a measurable Layer 2 intermediate. In healthy individuals, IgG2 constitutes the predominant natural antibody subclass reactive against glucuronoxylomannan (GXM), the principal cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide, positioning it as the primary opsonizing isotype for encapsulated cryptococci [53]. Human IgG2—but not IgG1 or IgG3—conferred protection against C. neoformans in a murine infection model through enhanced FcγR‐mediated phagocytosis [54]; however, human IgG2 binds murine FcγRs differently from human FcγRs, so direct extrapolation of this protective effect to human alveolar macrophages requires caution. Direct evidence linking IgG2 subclass deficiency to impaired pulmonary fungal clearance in apparently immunocompetent hosts remains limited to small observational series; larger prospective studies with species stratification and functional macrophage assays are needed to establish this phenotype within the three‐layer framework.

4 Layer 3: Convergence on Alveolar Macrophage Failure

All upstream pathogenic pathways ultimately converge on alveolar macrophage dysfunction as the common effector of impaired pulmonary fungal clearance. Whether the proximate defect lies in pattern recognition receptor variants reducing fungal sensing, anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies blocking macrophage activation, IgG2 insufficiency impairing capsular opsonisation, or CSF1R dysregulation altering macrophage subpopulation composition [43], the structural consequence is the same: alveolar macrophages cannot sustain adequate phagolysosomal maturation, oxidative burst, or classical M1 polarisation.

4.1 M1/M2 Polarisation Imbalance

Davis et al. established that M1 polarisation marks protective responses while M2 polarisation marks non‐protective ones, with polarisation state dynamically shaped by the local cytokine environment [55]. Hardison et al. [56] directly demonstrated that IFN‐γ–driven classical macrophage activation—with attendant iNOS/NO and ROS production—is sufficient to confer protection against pulmonary cryptococcosis. The mechanistic primacy of M2 polarisation as an independent pathological node, rather than a secondary correlate of Th2 immunity, was established by Müller et al. [57], who showed that selective abrogation of IL‐4Rα–dependent alternative macrophage activation is sufficient to confer resistance even in the presence of an ongoing Th2 response.

4.2 GM‐CSF Axis and Macrophage Subpopulation Specificity

GM‐CSF–dependent local differentiation is a prerequisite for effective macrophage activation. Ricafrente et al. [47] demonstrated that GM‐CSF coordinates monocyte M1 polarisation and granulocyte recruitment, with GM‐CSF deficiency producing interstitial macrophage dysfunction and increased pulmonary fungal burden. Hansakon et al. [58] confirmed that intranasal GM‐CSF delivery enhances alveolar macrophage ROS production and M1 polarisation, substantially reducing C. gattii lung burden. However, the net effect of CSF1R signalling is not uniformly protective: Mohamed et al. [43] found that PLX5622‐mediated depletion of MHCIIhi interstitial lung macrophages paradoxically reduced fungal burden, identifying this subpopulation as an intracellular reservoir that sustains infection rather than a clearance effector. Enhancing overall macrophage numbers or activation does not therefore guarantee improved outcome; the functional identity of the affected subpopulation is the critical determinant.

4.3 Innate Lymphocyte Bridging

Before macrophage polarisation is established, NK cells and γδT cells shape the early cytokine environment that determines subsequent effector quality. NK cells are the principal source of IL‐12/IL‐18–driven IFN‐γ during the innate phase [59, 60], whereas γδT cells rapidly produce both IFN‐γ and IL‐17A, bridging immediate innate surveillance with downstream Th1/Th17 polarisation [24, 61]. CARD9 deficiency impairs pulmonary NK and γδT cell recruitment, reducing early IFN‐γ availability and increasing susceptibility in murine models [24, 62]. The contribution of these innate lymphocytes is context‐dependent rather than uniformly protective—γδT cells can suppress Th1 responses in some settings while compensating for neutrophil deficiency through IL‐17A upregulation in others [61]—and their net role in apparently immunocompetent pulmonary disease has not been systematically characterised in human cohorts. These cells are best understood as modulators of the macrophage‐polarising environment rather than as independent Layer 2 phenotypes in the three‐layer framework.

4.4 Fungal Evasion Strategies

Cryptococcus exploits macrophage dysfunction through complementary escape mechanisms. Dragotakes et al. [63] showed that population‐level heterogeneity in phagosomal acidification drives intracellular escape dynamics, with poorly acidified phagosomes serving as preferential sites for intracellular survival and non‐lytic extrusion. Non‐lytic vomocytosis, first established in vitro by Alvarez and Casadevall [64] and subsequently confirmed in vivo by Nicola et al. [65], allows fungal egress without host‐cell lysis, facilitating dissemination while preserving the intracellular niche. Macrophage ARG1 expression further reduces intracellular killing capacity and has been associated with enhanced cryptococcal invasion of brain microvascular endothelium in vitro [66], linking Layer 3 dysfunction to dissemination potential.

4.5 Challenging the Intracellular‐Niche Model

Two recent studies complicate the prevailing emphasis on intracellular killing failure. Zheng et al. [67], using reporter‐strain mice and intravital imaging, found that cryptococci within pulmonary granulomas exist predominantly extracellularly, with ARG1+ monocyte‐derived myeloid cells forming a spatially organised suppressive ring that antagonises IFN‐γ–dependent extracellular killing rather than promoting intracellular persistence. This suggests that granuloma‐associated fungal persistence may reflect extracellular clearance failure as much as intracellular reservoir dependence. Additionally, Dang et al. demonstrated that a secreted cryptococcal virulence effector actively drives alternative macrophage polarisation via TLR4 [68], indicating that M2 skewing is not solely a consequence of host genetic susceptibility but is also actively engineered by the pathogen. The implication of this pathogen‐directed component for the host‐centric framework are addressed explicitly in the Conceptual Integration section below.

4.6 Species‐Specific Transcriptomic Divergence

Comparative transcriptomic profiling by Jeerawattanawart et al. [69] revealed that C. gattii elicits substantially weaker sterol metabolism pathway activation and classical macrophage activation responses in alveolar macrophages than C. neoformans , consistent with C. gattii's preferential exploitation of GM‐CSF axis deficiency. Across both species, the characteristic clinical presentation of granulomatous pulmonary nodules—frequently misdiagnosed as primary lung malignancy—is consistent with localised macrophage containment failure rather than global immunodeficiency, and may represent a convergent Layer 3 phenotype arising from mechanistically distinct upstream pathways.

5 Species‐Specific Expression of Host Susceptibility

The divergent epidemiological profiles of C. neoformans and C. gattii in apparently immunocompetent hosts may reflect a relative, rather than absolute, difference in the host vulnerabilities each species preferentially exploits. It must be emphasised at the outset that the pathway assignments described below are inferred from epidemiological correlation and species‐stratified prevalence data, not from genotype‐stratified human cohorts or direct mechanistic studies. The associations are therefore best understood as predominant tendencies with substantial overlap, not as mutually exclusive species‐specific mechanisms.

Cryptococcus neoformans (serotypes A/D) predominantly infects immunocompromised hosts globally, yet in Han Chinese populations the ST5‐VNI strain is disproportionately represented among immunocompetent patients, suggesting selective utilisation of a particular host genetic background. Epidemiological data from Han Chinese cohorts implicate FCGR2B and CLEC6A polymorphisms as candidate susceptibility determinants [20, 27, 53], pointing toward defects in IgG2/Fc receptor–mediated opsonophagocytosis and pattern recognition as plausibly exploited vulnerabilities. This association is, however, entirely correlative: no study has demonstrated that ST5‐VNI strains specifically target these immune defects, and the same polymorphisms have not been excluded as susceptibility factors in C. gattii infection. Titan cell formation and related dissemination mechanisms contribute to the tendency toward CNS spread rather than pulmonary localisation [70, 71], though a detailed discussion of these virulence mechanisms falls outside the scope of host susceptibility.

Cryptococcus gattii (serotypes B/C) more consistently causes pulmonary disease in apparently immunocompetent individuals [70, 72, 73] and shows a relatively stronger association with GM‐CSF axis dysfunction: anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibody positivity has been reported more frequently in C. gattii–infected immunocompetent patients than in those infected with C. neoformans [44]. This is, however, a relative enrichment rather than a species‐specific marker—anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies have also been documented in C. neoformans infection, and no consistent PRR or FcγR genetic signal has yet been indentified for C. gattii. The antibody‐mediated blockade of GM‐CSF–STAT5 signalling impairs alveolar macrophage activation and constitutes the best‐characterised susceptibility mechanism associated with this species, though it accounts for only a subset of cases. Compared with C. neoformans , C. gattii elicits weaker pulmonary Th1 and Th17 responses in murine models, attributable to attenuated dendritic cell function and reduced chemokine expression [74], and induces less classical macrophage activation as confirmed by comparative transcriptomic profiling [69].

Despite these relative upstream differences—pattern recognition and FcγR pathway defects more often implicated in C. neoformans , GM‐CSF axis dysfunction more often in C. gattii, with documented overlap in both directions—both species converge on the same Layer 3 outcome: alveolar macrophage–mediated pulmonary clearance failure (Table 1). This divergence of upstream routes with convergence at the effector level is the central argument of the three‐layer model and explains why apparently immunocompetent hosts with different genetic backgrounds develop similar pulmonary phenotypes after infection with different species. The species‐pathway associations themselves, however, remain hypotheses awaiting validation in genotype‐stratified, species‐stratified human cohorts.

6 Conceptual Integration: A Convergent Network Rather Than Parallel Causal Chains

The three‐layer model is not a set of parallel independent pathways running from distinct genetic variants to distinct clinical outcomes. It is a convergent network in which multiple Layer 1 inputs, operating through mechanistically distinct Layer 2 intermediates, funnel into a shared Layer 3 effector failure.

Several features of the network architecture deserve emphasis. First, individual loci are not confined to single pathways: FCGR2B simultaneously influences IgG2‐mediated opsonophagocytosis and direct inhibitory macrophage signalling, whereas CSF1 variants may bypass Layer 2 intermediates entirely and act directly on Layer 3 macrophage subpopulation composition. Second, individual Layer 2 phenotypes are not genetically determined by single loci: IL‐17/Th17 deficiency can result from perturbation at Dectin‐2/CLEC6A, TLR2 or CARD9, among others. Third, the three Layer 2 phenotypes are not mutually exclusive—a given host may carry partial defects across multiple pathways simultaneously, reaching the disease threshold through additive impairment rather than complete failure of any single arm. This polygenic, multi‐pathway architecture accounts for the phenotypic heterogeneity observed across apparently immunocompetent cryptococcosis cohorts and explains why no single biomarker reliably identifies all at‐risk individuals.

A fundamental limitation of the three‐layer model must be made explicit: as constructed, it is entirely host‐centric and treats the pathogen as a passive agent whose fate is determined solely by host immune competence. This simplification is increasingly untenable. Cryptococcus actively shapes the very Layer 3 effector state on which the model converges. Dang et al. demonstrated that a secreted fungal virulence effector drives alternative (M2) macrophage polarisation through TLR4, showing that the M2 skewing central to Layer 3 dysfunction is not solely a downstream consequence of host genetic susceptibility but can be actively engineered by the pathogen itself [68]. Comparable pathogen‐driven mechanisms—capsular glucuronoxylomannan shedding that suppresses opsonophagocytosis, titan‐cell formation that exceeds phagocytic capacity, and strain‐dependent differences in the induction of classical macrophage activation—each modify the Layer 3 outcome independently of the host's Layer 1–2 status. The implication is that the macrophage dysfunction at Layer 3 is the product of a bidirectional host–pathogen interaction, not a unidirectional cascade. Host genetic susceptibility and pathogen virulence programmes converge on the same effector cell and may act additively or synergistically; a host with only modest Layer 1–2 vulnerability may still develop disease when infected with a train that aggressively reprogrammes macrophage polarisation, and conversely. Incorporating this pathogen‐directed axis as a formal fourth dimension—rather than treating it as an external caveat—is a necessary direction for future refinement of the framework, and would require experimental design that vary host genotype and fungal strain factorially rather than studying either in isolation.

The practical implication is that risk stratification in this population will likely require composite immunophenotyping—assessing anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies, IgG subclass profiles, and Th17 functional capacity in parallel—rather than sequential single‐marker testing.

7 Critical Appraisal, Knowledge Gaps and Research Priorities

7.1 Evidence Quality

The existing evidence base meets OCEBM levels 3–4, with GRADE quality rated as low to moderate across all identified signals. No genome‐wide association study, whole‐exome sequencing analysis, or polygenic risk score model has been applied to apparently immunocompetent pulmonary cryptococcosis. The causal pathways linking specific SNPs to downstream macrophage dysfunction have not been established in primary human alveolar macrophages; the mechanistic inferences drawn throughout this review rest on candidate‐gene associations, animal models, and limited‐scale immunophenotyping data. A subset of patients remains unexplained even after targeted immune assessment, indicating that additional mechanisms—epigenetic regulation, lung microbiome composition, or stochastic host–pathogen interaction—probably contribute to susceptibility. The three‐layer model should be treated as a hypothesis‐generating framework, not as a validated causal map.

7.2 Basic Research Gaps

7.2.1 Genome‐Wide Genetic Architecture

The field has relied almost exclusively on small candidate‐gene studies, which constrain the discovery of novel susceptibility loci, the estimation of heritability, and the development of predictive risk tools [70]. A well‐powered, multicentre GWAS in East Asian apparently immunocompetent PC patients—with rigorous pulmonary phenotyping, species stratification ( C. neoformans vs. C. gattii), and complementary whole‐exome sequencing to capture large‐effect rare variants—is the most pressing basic research priority. Gene–environment and gene–pathogen interaction analyses should be embedded within this design from the outset, as these are not independent research questions but integral analytical components of a properly constructed GWAS.

7.2.2 Layer 1‐to‐Layer 3 Causal Chain

The complete mechanistic pathway from upstream genetic variant to alveolar macrophage dysfunction has not been established in any human study. Bridging this gap requires systematic integration of host genotyping with anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibody measurement, IL‐17/Th17 functional assays, and IgG subclass profiling, combined with multi‐omic analysis of primary human alveolar macrophages from genotype‐stratified donors. Epigenetic regulation at CSF1 and CSF2R loci—including promoter methylation and histone modification states—represents an underexplored mechanistic dimension that may account for susceptibility not captured by sequence variation alone.

7.3 Translational Gaps

7.3.1 Validated Genetic Risk Scores

The candidate variants reviewed here carry individually modest effect sizes (OR 1.6–6.6) and lack predictive power as single markers. A pilot polygenic risk score (PRS) is a near‐term actionable priority, and its construction can be specified concretely. Variant selection: the initial model should prioritise the two loci with the strongest pulmonary‐specific or replicated evidence—CLEC6A rs11045418 (the only variant studied directly in an apparently immunocompetent pulmonary cohort) and FCGR2B 232/I (replicated, with a consistent effect in the underlying‐disease–free subgroup)—supplemented by MBL2 O/O and PTX3 rs2305619 as secondary, population‐restricted terms. CSF1 rs1999713 should be excluded from the initial pulmonary model given its HIV‐meningitis–only provenance and unresolved directionality. Weighting: in the absence of a pulmonary‐specific GWAS, effect sizes should be derived from a dedicated case–control discovery cohort rather than borrowed from the heterogeneous meningitis literature; a log‐additive model weighting each allele by its locally estimated 1n (OR) is the appropriate starting structure, with explicit modelling of FCGR2B × CLEC6A and MBL2 × PTX3 interactions suggested by their pathway convergence. Priority validation population: Han Chinese adults without HIV infection, solid organ transplantation, haematological malignancy or immunosuppressive therapy, recruited from high‐incidence regions of southern and southeastern China, represent the optimal initial validation cohort, given both the disease burden and the population in which the constituent variants were originally identified. Generalizability to other East Asian and non‐Asian populations would require separate ancestry‐specific recalibration.

7.3.2 Standardised Anti‐GM‐CSF Autoantibody Testing

Anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies are the only Layer 2 phenotype with established clinical utility, yet their measurement is not standardised, and the framework below is proposed rather than settled. Two observations constrain assay design. First, ELISA (quantifies total binding) and functional neutralisation assays (measuring blocking of GM‐CSF–induced STAT5 phosphorylation) do not correlate linearly—binding affinity does not predict neutralising capacity [75, 76]. Second, low‐titre binding antibodies of uncertain pathogenicity occur in healthy individuals, as also documented for anti–IFN‐γ antibodies [77]. We therefore suggest that neutralising capacity, not binding signal, define a clinically significant result, with ELISA reserved for screening—mirroring the validated approach to anti–IFN‐γ autoantibody testing [77]. Notably, although absolute antibody levels vary with disease activity and treatment, neutralising activity remains relatively stable, persisting despite antifungal therapy [45, 78]; this further supports anchoring any threshold to neutralising activity. No study has yet defined such a threshold—ideally the neutralising titre above which alveolar macrophage GM‐CSF responsiveness is measurably impaired—and establishing one is a research priority. An international consensus on assay type, neutralising‐activity threshold, and sampling timing (testing at diagnosis for case definition; serial measurement for surveillance, including for delayed‐onset pulmonary alveolar proteinosis), particularly involving high‐burden East Asian centres, is a prerequisite for clinical adoption.

7.3.3 Imaging–Immunophenotype Correlation

No study has systematically examined whether specific genetic or immune subtypes associate with distinct radiological patterns (nodule size, distribution, margin characteristics, cavitation rate). Identifying such correlations would provide radiologists with host‐based differential diagnosis support and directly address the misdiagnosis‐as‐malignancy problem that drives diagnostic delay.

7.3.4 Asymptomatic High‐Risk Carriers

Whether pre‐infection monitoring—such as serial cryptococcal antigen screening—is feasible, cost‐effective, or clinically meaningful in individuals carrying high‐risk genetic profiles has not been examined in any study. This question must be addressed before the genetic risk framework can be translated into preventive strategies.

7.3.5 Modifiable Delay Nodes

The 67‐day diagnostic interval documented in MSG‐11 reflects identifiable decision‐making failures—referral patterns, radiological reporting conventions, threshold for laboratory testing—that have not been prospectively mapped or quantified as intervention targets.

Taken together, these gaps point toward a clear progression: population‐scale genetics to establish the susceptibility architecture, integrative mechanistic studies to validate the causal chain, and translational research to convert genetic and immune biomarkers into practical tools for early identification and risk stratification in apparently immunocompetent hosts.

8 Conclusion

Pulmonary cryptococcosis in apparently immunocompetent hosts may represent, in a subset of patients, an occult immune‐susceptibility state rather than a disease of truly normal immunity, with predisposition that is genetically and, in some cases, acquiredly determined. The three‐layer framework proposed here—upstream genetic variants in Dectin‐2/CLEC6A, MBL2, FCGR2B and CSF1 proposed to converge through distinct intermediate immune phenotypes onto a shared effector failure of alveolar macrophage–mediated pulmonary clearance—offers a mechanistically coherent candidate explanation for three otherwise unexplained observations: familial clustering despite shared environmental exposure, normal results on routine immunological screening, and the epidemiological convergence of C. neoformans and C. gattii infection patterns in this population.

The three Layer 2 phenotypes differ substantially in their evidentiary standing. Anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies have reached clinical validation and represent the only immediately actionable diagnostic and therapeutic target; IgG2 subclass deficiency remains a mechanistic hypothesis; and IL‐17/Th17 axis deficiency is supported by a single unreplicated study and should be treated as preliminary. Immunomodulatory intervention is currently justifiable only for the anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibody pathway, and even here systematic efficacy data are lacking. The framework should be understood as hypothesis‐generating: its clinical value will be determined by whether the mechanistic inferences it organises can be validated at the level of primary human cells and prospective cohorts. Moreover, as a host‐centric construct, the framework does not yet formally incorporate the active role of cryptococcal virulence factors in reprogramming macrophage polarisation; integrating this bidirectional host‐pathogen dimension is a priority for future development of the model.
