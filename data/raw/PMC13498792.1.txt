Aim

To analyse referrals and admissions to Malta’s sole inpatient rehabilitation facility, identifying emerging trends and challenges over recent years.

Design

A retrospective, population-based analysis was conducted on all in-patient rehabilitation referrals in Malta between July 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023. Referral outcomes, acceptance rates, and primary admission diagnoses were examined. Results were compared to a similar study in 2018. A follow-up questionnaire was designed to look at Rehabilitation outcomes in the 01.07.2022 to 31.12.2023 cohort at 2 years post discharge home.

Objectives

1) To identify key shifts in admission process, length of stay and rehabilitation outcomes, thereby providing evidence to enhance in-patient rehabilitation selection and guide international rehabilitation strategies 2) To assess rehabilitation functional outcomes of an inpatient rehabilitation program and 2 years post discharge.

Results

407 referrals were placed. Of these, 357 were new referrals, 73.1% (n=261) were accepted—a notable rise from 47% in 2018. Non-acceptance was recorded in 96 cases, while 50 patients were discharged or deceased before review. Both the mean (59 → 92) and median (22 → 59) length of stay increased over the 6-year period, reflecting an increase in both outliers due to social issues but also a shift towards longer length of stays in general. Despite this shift, the number of patients discharged to their own home fell from 90% to 79% with 12.3% being re-admitted to the acute hospital, 8.2% being discharged to a long-term care facility and 1 individual passing away.

Conclusions

As a population-based analysis of national in-patient rehabilitation data, this study provides insights into shortcomings in the efficiency and effectiveness of inpatient rehabilitation services. Analysis into the variables that might have contributed to these findings may serve as a framework for rehabilitation policies worldwide. Future research should investigate barriers influencing discharge and long-term rehabilitation success.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO) Rehabilitation is “a set of interventions designed to optimize functioning and reduce disability in individuals with health conditions in interaction with their environment”. 1 In-patient rehabilitation (IPR) is a healthcare service carried out within a dedicated rehabilitation facility that aims to aid patients recovering from a physical impairment, regain function through intensive and specialised multidisciplinary treatment.

The increasing global demand for rehabilitation is reflected in an estimated 2.4 billion people whose functioning is impaired by medical conditions. Worldwide, the need for rehabilitation is vastly unmet 2 despite the fact that it is an essential part of universal health coverage and a key strategy for the WHO’s Sustainable Development Goal 3 and that ‘building comprehensive rehabilitation service delivery models to progressively achieve equitable access to quality services’ is deemed as a crucial target in Rehab2030. 3 This deficiency is due to multiple factors including lack of funding, resources, plans at a national level as well as under-utilised referral pathways to rehabilitation.

In recent years, Malta’s population has grown steadily, currently estimated at approximately 545,000, further intensifying the pressure on the healthcare system. Ensuring good quality rehabilitation interventions and systems can lead to significant cost benefits in the long run both at an individual and societal level, by reducing length of stay, avoiding preventable hospitalisation and increasing the possibility of employment and independence therefore minimising the need for carer and financial help.

Inpatient rehabilitation in Malta remains centred around one public hospital. Since the conduction of a similar snapshot study in 2018 4 the rehabilitation hospital has doubled the specialised in-patient rehabilitation wards, splitting the cohort into a neurorehabilitation ward and an ortho-trauma rehabilitation ward, each comprising 30 beds. Despite this, given the rising population, an aging demographic, and the growing prevalence of chronic diseases, demand for rehabilitation services still exceeds available resources. 5

This population-based study analyses inpatient rehabilitation referrals to the rehabilitation hospital, comparing new 2022–2023 data with the 2018 findings of Agius Anastasi et al, to identify trends and challenges in achieving effective rehabilitation outcomes. By examining admission criteria, referral patterns, and changes in patient profiles, the study aims to inform improvements in inpatient rehabilitation selection frameworks and contribute to international rehabilitation strategies. It also aims at assessing the long-term sustained benefits of an inpatient rehabilitation program.

Outcomes such as functional independence (measured by the modified Barthel Index), discharge destination and employment at 2 years were assessed on the discharged cohort via telephone questionnaire. This gives an indication to the importance and cost effectiveness of an inpatient rehabilitation program.

Methods

Data from 1st July 2022 to December 31st 2023 was collected and analysed retrospectively. Software applications used by the local hospital systems for digital record keeping Isoft and Electronic Case Summary, as well as Microsoft Excel, were used for data collection.

Demographic data such as age, gender, admission and discharge dates, of patients referred for inpatient rehabilitation at the rehabilitation hospital from the acute general hospital was collated. Patients were classified according to their reason for referral in the following diagnostic group subsets: spinal cord injury, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), peripheral vascular disease (PVD), orthopaedic pathologies, post-intensive care syndrome, post-cardiology intervention, post-oncological treatment, traumatic brain injury, acquired brain injury (excluding CVA) and other pathologies.

Other factors taken into consideration were: whether the patient was accepted for in-patient rehabilitation (IPR) or not, and if accepted, whether they were planned for transfer to the neurorehabilitation or the ortho-trauma rehabilitation unit. The length of stay at the rehabilitation hospital, final outcome, discharge route and whether any patients had passed away during their IPR stay was also taken into account. This data was then compared to statistics from the same hospital back in 2018. 4

A follow-up questionnaire was developed for patients discharged from in-patient rehabilitation in 2023-2024. These patients were contacted in September 2025, representing a follow-up period of 2-3 years post-discharge. The main objective of the questionnaire was functional status and ADL independence inferred by the Modified Barthel Index Score. Other objectives included living arrangements which included the following groups: long-term care, home or home with carer/relative support. Finally, their current employment status was recorded and categorised as: part-time, full-time employment or unemployed/pensioner.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the online software ‘JASP’ (University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic, referral, admission, discharge and follow-up variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) where appropriate according to data distribution, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

The 2022–2023 cohort was compared with the previously published 2018 population-based audit for predefined service-level outcomes. Differences in referral acceptance rates between cohorts were assessed using a two-sample z-test for independent proportions, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals for the absolute difference in proportions.

Changes in modified Barthel Index (MBI) scores between discharge and follow-up were summarised using descriptive statistics. The relationship between change in MBI score and time since discharge was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient owing to the non-normal distribution of follow-up intervals.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

As this was a retrospective population-based service evaluation, no formal sample size calculation was performed. All eligible referrals during the study period were included.

Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis for each outcome. Patients who did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria for follow-up (including those without a documented discharge MBI, those who died during the index admission, those discharged against medical advice, or those readmitted to acute care during inpatient rehabilitation) were excluded from the follow-up analysis. Non-responders to the telephone follow-up questionnaire were reported separately.

Functional outcome was assessed using the Modified Barthel Index (MBI), recorded at admission to inpatient rehabilitation, discharge, and at long-term follow-up. MBI scores were summarised using means with standard deviations (SD), with medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) reported where appropriate based on data distribution. Functional change was calculated as the difference between MBI scores at discharge and admission, and between follow-up and admission.

The clinical relevance of observed functional changes was interpreted with reference to published estimates of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the Barthel Index/Modified Barthel Index. Given variability in reported MCID thresholds according to diagnosis, baseline disability and rehabilitation setting, MCID values were considered as contextual indicators rather than absolute thresholds applicable to all rehabilitation populations.

Ethical Approval

Approval by the research and ethics committee of the rehabilitation hospital was acquired prior to data collection and interpretation. Separate ethical approval was obtained to contact patients over the phone at 2-3 years follow up and inquire about salient variables post-discharge. All data collected was anonymised and utilised for this study only.

Results

407 patients were referred from the acute hospital for inpatient rehabilitation (IPR) between 1st July 2022 and 31st December 2023. Fifty referred patients were not included in the study as they were duplicate referrals or were not assessed by the PRM team due to discharge or death prior to evaluation. Following the initial assessment of the new referrals (n = 357) by the rehabilitation team, 261 patients were accepted for inpatient rehabilitation. Of these, 169 were allocated to the ortho-trauma ward, while 92 were assigned to the neurorehabilitation ward (Figure 1).Figure 1. Study flow diagram showing the selection of the study population and reasons for exclusion

Of the 261 patients initially accepted, 219 were eventually transferred to IPR. 28 patients were not transferred as they refused or were discharged or deceased prior to the transfer date. 10 patients were not transferred as they had either improved significantly and no longer required intervention or had deteriorated medically and were considered unfit for an intensive rehabilitation programme. 3 patients were refused for IPR upon second review and instead referred to outpatient-based rehabilitation while 1 patient opted to be admitted to a private rehabilitation care facility rather than the public IPR hospital even though they had been accepted by the rehabilitation team. The remaining 96 patients were not accepted for IPR. Reasons for refusal upon first review included: IPR not indicated (n=85), medically unstable requiring further acute care (n=7), to be re-reviewed (n=4) (Figure 2).Figure 2. Chart depicting reasons for refusal of IPR referrals upon first review

The average age of all 407 patients referred was 58.46 years while the average age for the 261 individuals initially accepted was 58.26 years. Furthermore, the majority of referrals were male (n=265 male and n=142 female).

The average time taken for patients to be transferred from the acute hospital to the rehabilitation hospital was 11.3 days. This value excludes readmissions, admissions with no value for ‘date seen’ inputted, as well as 3 prolonged acute hospital admissions due to severe, acute illness. The mean LOS at the rehabilitation hospital was 91.7 days, with a median LOS of 58.5 days.

A close look at the length of stay issues shows a mean of 92 days and a median of 59 days. While 75% of the cohort had a LOS below 117 days, the top 10% of patients with the longest LOS combined accounted for 41% of all days spent in inpatient rehabilitation by all patients between 2022 and 2023 combined. In particular, the ten patients with the longest LOS ranged from 321 to 913 days, together making up 5521 days (Figure 3).Figure 3. Skewed distribution of in-patient rehabilitation (IPR) length of stay (LOS)

Of the 219 patients accepted and transferred to IPR: 27 (12.3%) were re-admitted to the acute hospital because of an acute event, 173 (79%) were successfully discharged home after rehabilitation, 18 (8.2%) were transferred to a long-term care facility and 1 patient passed away during IPR (0.5%) (Figure 4).Figure 4. Chart depicting patient discharge destination post-IPR programme

The most common pathology for referral was cerebrovascular accident at a percentage of 22.36% (n = 91), closely followed by orthopaedic cases at 21.87% (n = 89) and ‘other’ pathologies at 21.38% (n = 87). Spinal cord injuries carried a weight of 16.95% of referrals (n = 69) while peripheral vascular disease/amputees contributed to 4.18% of referrals (n = 17). Furthermore, 15 patients (3.69%) were referred in view of traumatic brain injury, which 17 patients (4.18%) were referred in view of acquired brain injury (such as resection of space-occupying lesions). 2.46% were post-ICU pathologies (n = 10); 1.71% were post-cardiology intervention (n = 7) and 1.22% (n = 5) were post-oncological treatment (Figure 5).Figure 5. Pathology on referral to IPR

At the 2-3 year follow up post-discharge from IPR, additional variables were explored for the same cohort of patients. Out of the 219 patients admitted to IPR, 64 individuals were excluded from follow-up analysis due to missing data (no documentation of modified Barthel index upon discharge), discharge against medical advice from IPR, readmission to the acute hospital during IPR or death during IPR admission. The remaining 155 patients with a modified Barthel Index documented upon discharge were called in an attempt to carry out the follow up questionnaire. 71 patients were successfully contacted and willing to participate whilst the remaining 83 patients did not respond while 1 patient refused to participate in the follow-up questionnaire.

For the 71 patients who were successfully contacted, Barthel Scores were measured using the modified Barthel Index (20) over telephone consultation. Modified Barthel scores recorded on admission, discharge and at 2-year follow up were analysed. Results showed that out of 71 patients contacted, 54 individuals (76.1%) had a Barthel score that increased, 10 (14.1%) kept the same score whilst only 7 (9.9%) reported a regression in Barthel Score. The mean Barthel Score at admission was 8.17 which improved to 15.61 at discharge from inpatient rehabilitation program and continued to show gains at 2 years with a mean Barthel Score of 17.38. Overall, an average gain of 9.21 Barthel Points was noted from admission to 2-3 year follow up.

Out of the 71 successfully contacted, 52.1 % were living with relatives or carers, 42.3% were living independently and only 5.6% were living in a long-term institution. 49% of all respondents were retired by the end of 2025, while 33% of the respondents returned back to work either as part-time 8% or full time 25%. Only 18 % of respondents were unemployed at the 2-3 year follow up mark (Figure 6).Figure 6. Charts depicting Barthel change, employment status and living situation at 2-3 year follow up

Demographic data for the 71 respondents was as follows: 48 were male, 23 were female with a mean age (on referral to IPR) of 60 years and a median of 61 years. Length of stay at the inpatient rehabilitation hospital was a mean of 98 days and a median of 63 days. Mean re-admission rate post-discharge from IPR was 1.1 and median was 1. Diagnosis upon referral was as follows: spinal cord injury (n = 8), traumatic brain injury (n = 2), cerebrovascular accident (n = 18), orthopaedic pathology (n = 24), peripheral vascular disease/amputees (n = 6), acquired brain injury other than TBI/CVA (n = 6), post-cardiological intervention (n = 2), post-oncological treatment (n = 3), post-ICU syndrome (n = 1) and other pathologies (n = 1).

Of the 84 not included in the respondent analysis, 1 patient had been repatriated, 10 patients had passed away since discharge (M:F 7:3), 1 individual declined participation while the remaining 72 patients did not answer when called. Average age (upon referral to IPR) for patients who passed away was 70 years whilst the mean age at time of referral for those who did not respond was 60 years (median of 61.5 years old). The mean length of IPR stay for the 72 non-respondents was 91 days with a median of 39 days. While the average modified Barthel on discharge was 13 and median was 15. Mean and median re-admission rates for this cohort (n=84) were 1.3 and 1 respectively.

Pathology upon initial referral for the 10 patients who had passed away within 2-3 years post-discharge from IPR were as follows: 3 CVA patients, 3 individuals with complex fractures/orthopaedic pathology; 2 individuals with post-ICU syndrome, 1 patient with spinal cord injury and 1 patient with vascular pathology. The inpatient rehabilitation acceptance rate increased significantly from 47.4% (55/116) in the 2018 cohort to 71.5% (261/365) in the 2022–2023 cohort, representing an absolute increase of 24.1 percentage points (95% confidence interval 13.7% to 33.9%). Mean MBI score increased from 8.17 ± 4.57 at admission to 15.61 ± 4.36 at discharge and 17.38 ± 4.84 at follow-up. The mean improvement was 7.39 ± 4.80 points from admission to discharge and 9.21 ± 5.58 points from admission to follow-up. The additional improvement between discharge and follow-up was 1.77 ± 3.57 points.

The observed improvement from admission to follow-up exceeded commonly reported MCID thresholds for the Barthel Index. A previous study in stroke rehabilitation reported that an improvement of approximately 2 points on the 20-point Modified Barthel Index represented a clinically meaningful change. 6 Although this threshold was derived from a specific diagnostic population and may not be directly transferable to a heterogeneous inpatient rehabilitation cohort, the magnitude of improvement observed in this study suggests clinically meaningful functional gains in addition to statistical improvement.

Discussion

Comparison of the data cohorts over a 6-year period, from the same inpatient rehabilitation service in Malta demonstrates a marked trend towards reduced efficiency and bed turnover. While other key performance indicators such as sustained Barthel Index and return to work are crucial in assessing the effectiveness and quality of rehabilitation service, the overall increase in delays and LOS is still worth rigorous analysis, considering costs and infrastructural restrictions that these bring with them.

Compared to the 2018 national audit, 4 this recent data highlights an increase in acceptance rate from 47% to 73.1% which was accompanied by a significant increase in mean and median LOS and an almost three-fold increase in the time-to-transfer (average time to transfer of 4.84 days in 2018 compared to 11.3 days in this study) from the acute hospital to IPR. This has profound implications on the rehabilitation offered to service users, putting patients at risk of delayed rehabilitation, jeopardising recovery potential in crucial periods of heightened neuroplasticity and increasing risks of complications prior to transfer.

This represents a major healthcare concern which needs to be emphasised in order to ensure rehabilitation services are optimised to reach more individuals in a shorter amount of time. While optimising patient selection remains the principal focus of this study, alternative healthcare strategies and parallel pathways for individuals with extreme long-stay needs must become an urgent national priority in order to offload a rehabilitation service that is still strained for resources. Without effective and efficient rehabilitation pathways, the societal and economic costs of persistent disability and prolonged time away from work will be substantial, further justifying the need for governments to prioritise the efficiency of rehabilitation services.

If one removes the longest-staying 4.9% of patients, the mean LOS is noted to reduce by more than 20 days, highlighting the disproportionate impact of extremely prolonged stays on overall bed utilisation. Nevertheless, it is still worth noting that this will have little impact on the median LOS, which thus reflects a broader systemic increase affecting most patients undergoing rehabilitation, rather than isolated outliers.

This increase is likely multifactorial, ranging from changes in rehabilitation intensity and service efficiency to a more cautious multidisciplinary approach that prioritises comprehensive discharge planning, equipment provision and social support - often stemming from a heightened culture of medicolegal concerns. Furthermore, literature on average LOSs in rehabilitation facilities is sparse and varies significantly based on pathology being addressed, infrastructure and even funding methods. It is therefore difficult to base success and failure of a rehabilitation service based purely on LOS. For this reason, changes in Barthel from admission to discharge and 2 years after, as well as discharge destination and readmission rates during those two years of follow up, have been analysed in this study to have a better understanding of the impact of rehabilitation services on patients’ general outcome.

The sustained improvement in MBI scores observed at 2–3-year follow-up suggests that functional benefits following inpatient rehabilitation extended beyond discharge. The mean improvement of 9.21 points from admission to follow-up exceeded published estimates of the minimal clinically important difference for the Barthel Index. However, MCID thresholds vary according to diagnosis, baseline functional impairment and rehabilitation setting, and the available thresholds have predominantly been established in specific populations such as stroke. Therefore, while the magnitude of improvement observed supports clinically meaningful functional recovery, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design, heterogeneous diagnostic cohort and absence of follow-up data for non-responders.

Notwithstanding, the proportion of individuals discharged from the rehabilitation hospital to a long-term care (LTC) facility has increased from 3% in the 2018 cohort to 8.2% in the current study. This is also an important key performance indicator to be accounted for, with the increase in institutionalisation giving a more negative outlook to the progress made.

In the 2 year follow up questionnaire it was evident that the vast majority of patients who responded were living in the community 94.4% either with relatives/carers or independently. With a mean Barthel score of 17.38. This shows the success rate of an inpatient rehabilitation program as the vast majority were living in the community with good functional outcomes and an average improvement of Barthel of 9.21 from admission to 2 year follow up. This complements our findings and further emphasis that careful patient selection and early rehabilitation leads to significant improvement in functional outcomes and independence.

Apart from underscoring the need to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of rehabilitation strategies within the IPR wards, these findings also highlight the need to reassess current admission criteria. Review of the ten patients with the longest LOS shows that 80% had already been flagged, prior to acceptance to IPR, as requiring additional considerations, including social work input. Furthermore, 40% were foreign nationals, of whom only one was successfully repatriated, after 638 days in the neurorehabilitation unit, following an ADAM-variant Guillain–Barré syndrome.

Notably, 90% of these exceptionally long-stay patients in IPR were younger than 60 years, all falling within the 35–59-year age bracket. This pattern largely reflects a persistent gap in Malta’s healthcare system, namely the limited availability of long-term care facilities willing to accept individuals under the age of 60. Among the ten longest-staying patients in IPR, only half were discharged home. The remaining patients were transferred to long-term care facilities, a private hospital (n=1), repatriated (n=1), or discharged against medical advice (n=1).

While patients aged over 60 can often be cohorted and transferred under the care of geriatric medicine services while awaiting placement in long-term care, this pathway is generally unavailable to younger patients. Consequently, at any given time, one or two beds in each of the two high-intensity wards in the rehabilitation hospital are occupied by patients who are clinically ready for discharge but remain in hospital solely because of delays in accessing long-term care.

This situation has important implications for ward morale and image. Younger patients may compare their own progress with that of these highly complex or atypical cases and, in turn, develop unrealistic expectations regarding their own length of stay, despite such cases representing only a small proportion of the overall inpatient population. The authors nevertheless believe that these negative comparisons exert a cumulative effect on the median length of stay across the service.

Analysing admission criteria enables the optimization of the recruitment process, directly influencing patient length of stay and turnover rates by anticipating potential obstacles and selecting appropriate rehabilitation candidates. Notwithstanding, even when red flags are identified and even raised to social workers prior to acceptance for inpatient rehabilitation, it can be exceedingly difficult to refuse patients who require intensive and complex rehabilitation based on the possibility of unfavourable length of stay or discharge destination at the end of their rehabilitation journey.

Access to rehabilitation should be determined by eligibility, rather than demand, to prioritize individuals with a genuine need for IPR7,8 and as stated by Zhang B et al, 2023 “specialized intensive inpatient rehabilitation is crucial and time-sensitive for functional recovery”. 9 Therefore, it is the challenging role of the Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine (PRM) Specialist and team leader’s to select complex patients who would benefit from inpatient rehabilitation while still minimising unnecessary delays with both pre-admission preparation and post-admission strategies and lobbying for policy change. A study by Durand et al (2020) demonstrated that benchmarking rehabilitation length of stay effectively reduced bed occupancy and system costs without negatively impacting patients’ functional or sensorimotor outcomes. 10 Integrating this approach during the admission phase of inpatient rehabilitation (IPR) may help reduce length of stay by providing both patients and staff with a clear target to guide goal-setting and treatment planning. Unnecessarily-prolonged hospitalisation can potentially lead to adverse outcomes such as negative emotions, iatrogenic complications (such as hospital-acquired infections and medication errors) and in a study by New P.W. et al 2013, the risk of the occurrence of adverse events has been documented to increase by 6% daily. 11

The increase in referral acceptance since 2018 may be attributed to a more lenient approach in response to external pressures to alleviate strain on acute hospitals, as well as the expanded availability of beds at the inpatient rehabilitation facility under the care of PRM specialists. It is important to note that the data analysed in this study was collected from referrals submitted between July 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023, a period encompassing both the peri- and post-COVID-19 pandemic phases. The World Health Organization (WHO) officially declared the end of the pandemic on May 5, 2023, a factor that may have influenced referral patterns to IPR. During medical crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, acute care hospitals frequently experience bed shortages, often leading to increased patient transfers to rehabilitation facilities. As the study period spans both peri- and post-pandemic phases, the impact of this medical crisis may have contributed to the observed findings.

In comparison, data published by Hakkennes et al (2013) indicated an acceptance rate of 81.3%, with favourable factors for IPR admission including satisfactory pre-morbid cognitive function, mobility, and communication. 12 Similarly, Lim et al (2013) reported a 74.9% acceptance rate, noting that patients with high levels of independence in personal activities of daily living prior to a sudden increase in dependence were more likely to be accepted by the IPR team. 13 One potential explanation for the lower acceptance rate observed in this study compared to previous literature is a lack of awareness regarding the concept of rehabilitation and the services available locally in the country.

Some of the patients referred and accepted for IPR did refuse admission themselves once the transfer date was offered. This highlights a pitfall very often encountered during reviews, where referring clinicians fail to ask about their patients’ wishes. In these cases, an in-depth discussion should be initiated between the reviewing physiatrist and patient to ensure that their consent is adequately informed. The ultimate decision to refuse lies within the patient’s remit as long as they are deemed to have mental capacity to do so. Non-compliance was chosen as a reason not to admit a patient to rehabilitation by over 90% of participants in a survey carried out by Friedman A. et al 2024. 14 An observational cohort study by Anders DM et al (2022) found that participants’ level of effort (LoE) positively correlated with improved rehabilitation outcomes which further highlights this. 15 Reasons for refusal may include patients wishing to return directly to their own home or stigma surrounding admission to an in-patient rehabilitation hospital. The complexity of this issue increases when individuals with cognitive impairment are involved, as their ability to refuse treatment may be uncertain. Additionally, their limited willingness to engage in a rehabilitation program should be interpreted with greater consideration when assessing their eligibility for admission.

A satisfactory pre-morbid cognitive state compatible with independence in ADLs but a newly-acquired cognitive impairment (such as after acquired brain injury) favours acceptance to an IPR. The new cognitive impairment must not interfere significantly with patient participation in the IPR process and must also be expected to be improved by it, with the aim of an overall improved functional state. In this case, a patient who may not be willing to be admitted during the initial phases, may, through cognitive rehab, become aware of the importance of this same admission. Patients with a poor pre-morbid cognitive state or chronic cognitive impairment such as severe dementia are less likely to improve from a functional point of view.16-18

New PW et al, 2013 noted that patients under 65-years-old, non-weightbearing patients after lower limb fractures, males and those with acquired brain injury were more likely to have a barrier to discharge from in-patient rehabilitation. 11 This is mirrored in our study as the average age of the 17 patients with a failed discharge route was 50.82 years and 82.4% were male. A plausible reason for this is because older adults (>65-year-olds) are eligible to a wider selection of community services such as long-term care homes and carer at home schemes. 12

The potential for functional improvement and a comprehensive list of goals at the start of a patient’s rehabilitation admission is key to correct management.19,20 The rise in the re-admission rate to acute medical hospitals (12.3% compared to 7% in 2018) underscores the importance of ensuring medical stability as a key transfer criterion. Patients admitted to an IPR still require daily physician oversight and 24-hour rehabilitation nursing care. They must also be able to participate in an intense rehabilitation programme.18,21,22

Patients must exhibit a need for continuous, intensive interdisciplinary therapies that encompass both a diagnostic and therapeutic approach. This usually requires a daily minimum of three hours of services delivered by the rehabilitation team including at least two of physiotherapy, speech therapy and occupational therapy, known as the ‘3-hour rule’. The case history must indicate that these services cannot be provided in an alternative setting, such as an outpatient clinic.20,23

Based on these findings and with the objective of facilitating the complex decision-making process undertaken by physiatrists globally in selecting patients for inpatient rehabilitation (IPR), the admission criteria proposed in 2022 (Table 1) have been revised as follows:1. The patient has demonstrated improvement when exposed to daily therapy in the acute setting, a positive indicator of potential for significant functional gains through intensive rehabilitation moving forward.

2. The patient is willing to be admitted to the IPR programme (in patients with significant and new cognitive impairment lacking mental capacity, a decision must be taken in their best interest and a reasonable chance of recovery leading to likely compliance with the IPR programme needs to be anticipated).

3. The patient requires a minimum of 3 hours of comprehensive (multi-disciplinary) rehabilitation services/treatments daily for no less than 5 days a week.

4. The patient is medically stable and able to participate in an intensive IPR programme.

5. The patient has a clear diagnosis and management plan from the acute setting, with no pending urgent investigations or treatment.

6. The patient must have a new functional loss, or recent exacerbation of disability, leading to a significant reduction in physical and/or cognitive function.

7. The patient and their relatives must be made aware of the initial rehabilitation goals, estimated duration of IPR stay and agree to follow the proposed treatment plan.

8. The patient requires 24-hour daily rehabilitation nursing care/supervision.

9. The patient’s pre-morbid cognition, mobility and communication were of a satisfactory level and allowed for a high-level of independence in personal activities of daily living. 21 23 12 13

Conclusions

As a population-based analysis of national in-patient rehabilitation data, this study provides insights into shortcomings to efficiency and effectiveness of inpatient rehabilitation services. Analysis into the variables that might have contributed to these findings may serve as a framework for rehabilitation policies worldwide. Future research should investigate barriers influencing discharge and long-term rehabilitation success.

Study Limitations

While data from all referrals and inpatients from the rehabilitation hospital remains complete, the study design remains a retrospective one, making it prone to errors. Similarly, more detailed analysis of variables which could be related to either issues with admission or discharge could not be gathered or retrieved. Since the time of data collection, a new tool has been integrated into the admission documentation templates which should enhance the variables related to patient (the Rehabilitation Complexity Scale), which should make future studies more robust and guide future rehabilitation services more effectively. Another limitation of note is that as a score, the modified Barthel Index has a ceiling effect and may not accurately represent improvement in certain cases.

Potential sources of bias include selection bias and response bias. Selection bias was minimised because the study included all referrals to Malta’s only national inpatient rehabilitation hospital during the study period. Referral assessments were undertaken using the same consultant-led admission criteria applied in both the 2018 and 2022–2023 cohorts. Nevertheless, changes in rehabilitation service capacity during the study period may have influenced admission practices and should be considered when interpreting comparisons between cohorts.

Selection bias may have arisen because only patients referred to the rehabilitation service from the acute hospital were captured; patients who may have benefited from inpatient rehabilitation but were not referred were not included. In addition, the follow-up questionnaire was limited to patients with available discharge Barthel scores who could be contacted and agreed to participate. Non-responders may have differed systematically from responders in functional status, mortality risk, social support or likelihood of readmission. To mitigate this, available demographic, diagnostic, length-of-stay and readmission data for non-responders were described separately. However, residual bias cannot be excluded.
