Background

The limited availability of donor hearts has led to an increased utilization of organs from donors with pre-existing coronary artery disease (CAD). However, the impact of donor-transmitted CAD (DTCAD) on post-transplant outcomes stratified by donor age remains insufficiently characterized.

Methods

Using the United Network for Organ Sharing database, adults undergoing orthotopic heart transplantation (2000 to 2025) were analyzed. Donors were stratified by age and CAD status on pre-procurement angiography. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Effect modification by donor age and allocation era was assessed using multivariable Cox regression.

Results

Among 21,805 recipients (median follow-up 3.94 years), 1932 (8.9%) received hearts with CAD. DTCAD was not associated with mortality [hazard ratio (HR) 1.04; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.95-1.13; p = 0.38]. The CAD × donor age interaction met the prespecified threshold (p = 0.094), with increasing risk among older donors (HR 1.27 at age 60; p = 0.02). Significant effect modification by allocation era was observed (interaction p = 0.018): DTCAD was not associated with higher mortality pre-2018 (HR 0.97) but was associated with a 24% increase in mortality post-2018 (HR 1.24; 95% CI 1.07-1.43; p = 0.005). Secondary outcomes, including graft failure, treated rejection, stroke, dialysis, and pacemaker implantation, did not differ between groups.

Conclusions

DTCAD is not consistently associated with adverse outcomes. However, in the current era characterized by higher-acuity recipients, DTCAD, especially from older donors, is linked to increased mortality. These findings support re-evaluating donor CAD selection criteria in contemporary practice.

Heart transplantation remains the definitive therapy for advanced heart failure, with median post-transplant survival exceeding 12 years.1, 2, 3 Despite this, the ongoing disparity between donor heart availability and transplant demand has led to increased utilization of extended-criteria donors, including older individuals and those with cardiovascular risk factors such as pre-existing coronary artery disease (CAD).5 The prevalence of donor CAD rises with age, and recent data indicate a growing reliance on older donors, accompanied by considerable geographic variability in acceptance practices.4, 5, 6, 7 Historically, donor CAD has resulted in higher organ refusal rates due to concerns regarding early graft dysfunction, accelerated cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV), and unfavorable long-term outcomes.4, 8

The prognostic significance of donor-transmitted CAD (DTCAD) remains unclear. Some studies have associated donor atherosclerosis with major adverse cardiovascular events, accelerated CAV progression, and increased plaque burden on serial intravascular ultrasound. In contrast, other investigations suggest that carefully selected donors with non-obstructive CAD may be utilized without compromising recipient outcomes.9, 10, 11, 12 The current body of evidence is constrained by heterogeneous study designs, small sample sizes, and inconsistent evaluation of donor age as an effect modifier. The 2018 U.S. heart allocation policy introduced substantial changes to transplant practice by prioritizing medical urgency, expanding geographic sharing, and increasing the use of temporary mechanical circulatory support.13, 14, 15 It remains uncertain whether donor CAD has different prognostic implications in this higher-acuity era.

Although both DTCAD and advanced donor age have been associated with adverse outcomes, the interaction between these factors has not been thoroughly characterized. This distinction holds clinical significance because CAD in younger donors may indicate limited focal disease, while CAD in older donors may reflect more diffuse atherosclerosis, impaired coronary reserve, and reduced vascular adaptability.10, 16, 17 Previous studies, including the DONOR-CAD analysis, have reported increasing DTCAD prevalence with donor age but have not assessed whether donor age modifies the clinical impact of DTCAD.4 Accordingly, we sought to evaluate the impact of DTCAD on post-transplant outcomes stratified by donor age in a contemporary national cohort. We hypothesized that the prognostic impact of DTCAD is modified by donor age, with a disproportionately greater adverse effect in older donors.

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) Standard Transplant Analysis and Research files. Adult recipients (≥18 years) undergoing primary isolated orthotopic heart transplantation between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2025, were included if the donor underwent pre-procurement coronary angiography. Inclusion was restricted to donors with angiographic data because coronary angiography is typically performed in older donors or those with cardiovascular risk factors, and this restriction ensures accurate ascertainment of CAD status. Patients undergoing repeat transplantation, multi-organ transplantation, or heterotopic transplantation were excluded. This study used deidentified data and was deemed exempt by the University of Miami Institutional Review Board (20260205).

Study design and definitions

The primary exposure was donor-transmitted coronary artery disease (DTCAD), defined as any angiographic coronary abnormality identified during donor evaluation. For secondary analyses, CAD severity was categorized as non-significant (<50% stenosis) or significant (≥50% stenosis in ≥1 major epicardial vessel).

Prespecified donor variables included age, sex, race or ethnicity, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, left ventricular ejection fraction, and cause of death. Recipient variables included demographics, heart failure etiology, renal function, pulmonary vascular resistance, hospitalization status, mechanical circulatory support, and transplant waitlist characteristics. Transplant-related variables included ischemic time and predicted heart mass ratio.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality during follow-up, assessed at 30 days, 1 year, 5 years, and 10 years. Secondary outcomes included graft failure (defined as re-transplantation or need for durable mechanical circulatory support [MCS]); treated acute rejection; stroke; dialysis; permanent pacemaker implantation; and primary graft dysfunction. Follow-up was available through January 28, 2026.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] and compared using Student's t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher exact tests.

Survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate associations between DTCAD and mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with no major violations observed. All prespecified covariates were retained in multivariable models regardless of statistical significance. Additional clinically relevant covariates, such as female donor-to-male recipient sex mismatch, pre-transplant dialysis, and mechanical ventilation at listing, were assessed and included when data were available. Because donor age and donor CAD may be biologically related, potential collinearity between these variables was assessed using variance inflation factors prior to model construction.

Donor age and allocation era were evaluated as prespecified effect modifiers using multiplicative interaction terms. Hazard ratios (HRs) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic splines with 4 knots, placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of donor age, were used to assess non-linear associations between donor age and outcomes. Fine–Gray competing-risk models were applied to analyze graft failure, with death without prior graft failure considered as a competing event. Cumulative incidence functions were calculated to estimate graft failure while accounting for competing events, and these estimates were compared descriptively across groups. Prespecified sensitivity analyses included a landmark analysis excluding deaths within 90 days, restriction to the post-2018 allocation era, and analyses stratified by donor CAD severity. A two-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, except for interaction analyses, for which a p value less than 0.10 was prespecified. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

A total of 21,805 recipients met eligibility criteria, of whom 1932 (8.9%) received donor hearts with CAD on pre-procurement angiography and 19,873 (91.1%) received donor hearts without CAD. Median follow-up was 3.94 years (IQR: 1.03-7.95), representing 112,310 patient-years; 6951 deaths occurred during follow-up.

At 1 year, mortality was slightly higher in the CAD group (11.5% vs 10.0%; p = 0.038); however, this difference diminished over time, with no significant differences observed at 5 or 10 years (Figure 1). In multivariable Cox regression, donor CAD was not independently associated with all-cause mortality in the overall cohort (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.95-1.13; p = 0.377). Severity of donor CAD

Among donors with available stenosis severity data, CAD was classified as non-obstructive in 1261 recipients and obstructive (≥50% stenosis) in 100 recipients; severity data were unavailable in 571 recipients. To evaluate potential selection bias in donor acceptance practices, donor and recipient characteristics were compared across CAD severity levels (Table S5). Among donors with available severity data, 1261 (92.7%) exhibited non-obstructive CAD, whereas 100 (7.3%) had obstructive CAD. Recipient acuity was generally similar across severity groups. Rates of ECMO support at listing (2.3%, 2.1%, and 2.0%), IABP support (9.6%, 10.0%, and 8.0%), and mechanical ventilation (1.8%, 1.4%, and 3.0%) were comparable among recipients receiving donor hearts without CAD, with non-obstructive CAD, and with obstructive CAD, respectively. Donors with obstructive CAD were older than donors without CAD (median age 47 years [IQR 41-52] vs 42 years [IQR 36-48]).

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated modest separation of survival curves beginning approximately 3 to 4 years after transplantation, with higher cumulative mortality observed among recipients of donor hearts with multivessel CAD. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.105; Figure S1). Compared to recipients without CAD, non-obstructive CAD was not associated with increased mortality (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.91-1.14; p = 0.72). Single-vessel obstructive CAD (HR 1.27, 95% CI 0.89-1.81; p = 0.19) and multivessel obstructive CAD (HR 1.44, 95% CI 0.94-2.21; p = 0.09) were associated with progressively higher, though statistically non-significant, point estimates for mortality.

Secondary outcomes were similar between groups, including perioperative stroke, dialysis, permanent pacemaker implantation, early acute rejection, and treated rejection within the first year (Table 3). Subgroup Analyses

Era of Transplantation

Era-stratified analyses were conducted to assess whether the 2018 revision of the United States heart allocation system altered the association between DTCAD and post-transplant mortality. Compared with the pre-2018 era, the post-2018 cohort exhibited higher recipient acuity, as indicated by increased use of IABP (21.5% vs 6.6%) and ECMO (5.3% vs 0.6%). Additionally, this cohort experienced shorter waitlist times (34 vs 97 days), higher rates of diabetes (32.2% vs 28.1%), younger donor age, longer ischemic times, and lower prevalence of donor smoking and hypertension (Table S2). The proportion of donor hearts with CAD increased from 7.7% in the pre-2018 era to 9.8% in the post-2018 era (Table S3). The median follow-up duration was 3148 days (IQR 1628-4383) for the pre-2018 cohort and 737 days (IQR 335-1464) for the post-2018 cohort.

Each era-stratified Cox model was adjusted using the same prespecified recipient, donor, and donor-recipient matching covariates as those included in the primary multivariable analysis. In the pre-2018 era (n = 9483; 5213 deaths), DTCAD was not associated with mortality (adjusted HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87-1.08; p = 0.539). In the post-2018 era (n = 11,924; 1867 deaths), DTCAD was independently associated with a 24% increased risk of mortality (adjusted HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.07-1.43; p = 0.005). A formal interaction model demonstrated significant effect modification by allocation era (interaction HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.04-1.49; p = 0.018), indicating that the prognostic impact of donor CAD differed substantially between allocation periods.

Kaplan–Meier analyses corroborated these results. In the pre-2018 era, survival rates were similar between recipients of donor hearts with and without CAD. Specifically, 30-day, 1-year, and 5-year survival rates were 95.6%, 87.9%, and 76.4% in the CAD group, compared to 95.1%, 88.0%, and 75.0% in the non-CAD group (log-rank p = 0.81). In the post-2018 era, recipients of donor hearts with CAD experienced significantly lower survival at all time points: 96.1%, 88.7%, and 74.2% at 30 days, 1 year, and 5 years, respectively, versus 97.0%, 91.1%, and 77.2% in the non-CAD group (log-rank p = 0.006). The absolute survival difference increased over time in the post-2018 era, rising from 1.1% at 30 days to 2.8% at 1 year and 2.9% at 5 years. Donor type

Of 21,805 heart transplant recipients, 21,189 (97.2%) received organs from donation after brain death (DBD) donors and 616 (2.8%) from donation after circulatory death (DCD) donors. DTCAD was identified in 1865 DBD donors (8.8%) and 67 DCD donors (10.9%), with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.074). The association between DTCAD and mortality or graft failure was not influenced by donor type (interaction p = 0.751 for mortality; interaction p = 0.444 for graft failure) (Table S4).

Discussion

This contemporary analysis of 21,805 adult heart transplant recipients demonstrates that DTCAD is not a uniform risk factor but instead acts as an age- and era-dependent determinant of post-transplant outcomes. In the overall cohort, DTCAD was not independently associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.95-1.13; p = 0.38). Significant effect modification by allocation era was observed (interaction p = 0.018): DTCAD was not associated with mortality in the pre-2018 era (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87-1.08; p = 0.54) but was associated with a 24% increased risk of death in the post-2018 era (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.07-1.43; p = 0.005). Additionally, the interaction between DTCAD and donor age met the prespecified significance threshold (interaction p = 0.094), with the HR increasing from 0.82 at donor age 20 years to 0.99 at age 40 years, 1.08 at age 50 years, and 1.19 at age 60 years, suggesting a progressive increase in risk across the donor age spectrum. Collectively, these findings indicate that DTCAD should be considered within the broader context of donor age and recipient acuity, rather than as an absolute contraindication to organ acceptance.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that non-obstructive donor CAD does not uniformly impair post-transplant survival. Lechiancole et al. reported that moderate CAD (≤50% stenosis) does not adversely affect survival or accelerate CAV.9 Similarly, the DONOR–CAD study found that DTCAD was not associated with overall mortality, although significant CAD (≥50% stenosis) was linked to cardiovascular events.4 Our severity analysis supports this pattern, demonstrating no excess risk with non-obstructive CAD and a graded increase in risk with multivessel disease, although statistical significance was limited by sample size in obstructive CAD subgroups rather than absence of a biological effect.

We observed early separation of survival curves, with higher 1-year mortality among recipients of CAD donor hearts, followed by convergence at longer follow-up. This pattern suggests that risk is concentrated in the early post-transplant period. A plausible mechanism is increased susceptibility of CAD-affected grafts to ischemia-reperfusion injury and inflammatory stress.18 Older donor hearts with CAD likely harbor diffuse atherosclerosis and reduced coronary reserve, limiting tolerance to ischemic stress. Supporting this, prior studies have shown that prolonged ischemic time (>3 h) disproportionately affects outcomes in older donor hearts.19 In addition, IVUS studies demonstrate that donor-transmitted atherosclerosis is associated with accelerated CAV progression, which may contribute to early graft dysfunction.12 Importantly, the mechanism appears to involve impaired compensatory remodeling rather than accelerated intimal hyperplasia. Sites with donor lesions do not show compensatory vessel enlargement as intimal thickening develops, suggesting pre-existing atherosclerosis impedes expansive remodeling. The 50% stenosis threshold is reached sooner in arteries with donor lesions not because they progress more rapidly, but because they start at a greater percentage of stenosis. 10 Recipients surviving beyond the first post-transplant year demonstrated comparable long-term outcomes, consistent with prior data showing that the association between older donor age and DTCAD development is confined to the first year.20 A contemporary IVUS analysis similarly found that donor-transmitted atherosclerosis was not associated with medium- to long-term adverse outcomes (coronary intervention, CAV stage 2-3, or cardiovascular death) over a median follow-up of 6.1 years.21

Although the interaction between donor CAD and donor age did not reach conventional statistical significance, the observed gradient across age groups and strong biological plausibility support an age-dependent effect. A recent United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) analysis (2015 to 2023) found that donors ≥45 years were associated with significantly higher 1- and 3-year mortality and graft failure rates compared to younger donors, with spline analysis suggesting the mortality risk inflection point occurs around age 40.7 Importantly, the hazard from donor age appears time-dependent: in the ISHLT analysis of 64,354 transplants, the HR for mortality per 10-year increase in donor age was 1.19 at 30 days and 1.16 in the first year, attenuating to 1.07 by years 5 to 10.22 A significant effect modification by allocation era was observed. DTCAD was not associated with graft failure in the pre-2018 cohort but was linked to significantly lower graft survival in the post-2018 cohort. The 2018 allocation policy revision shifted transplantation toward higher-acuity recipients who were bridged with temporary MCS. 23 In this context, donor CAD may constitute an additional physiological insult that disproportionately impacts outcomes in recipients with greater illness severity. Further investigation is required to determine whether this interaction reflects a true biological effect or residual confounding from unmeasured changes in donor selection and recipient management. For example, short-term MCS is associated with 2.47-fold increased 1-year mortality (95% CI 1.04-5.87) in a recent meta-analysis.24 The pre-2018 era included more stable recipients (often on durable LVADs or inotropes alone) who may have tolerated donor CAD without adverse consequences. The post-2018 era, characterized by higher proportions of ECMO- and temporary MCS-bridged recipients with limited physiologic reserve, creates conditions in which DTCAD may exceed the threshold for graft tolerance, particularly during the vulnerable early post-transplant period.

Non-obstructive CAD represented 92.7% of donor hearts with available severity data, whereas obstructive CAD was rare. Recipient acuity remained consistent across severity groups, indicating that the observed trends cannot be explained solely by preferential allocation of obstructive CAD grafts to the most critically ill recipients (Table S5).

These findings may have direct implications for donor selection. Donor CAD should not be considered a uniform contraindication but rather an age- and context-dependent variable. Hearts from younger donors with mild-to-moderate CAD can be safely utilized, potentially expanding the donor pool. In contrast, donor CAD warrants greater scrutiny in older donors and in recipients requiring mechanical circulatory support. Importantly, recipients who survive the early post-transplant period demonstrate comparable long-term outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Selection bias is inherent, as donors with severe CAD are unlikely to be accepted. Angiographic assessment is subject to inter-observer variability, and the registry lacks lesion-level and functional data. The database does not capture detailed post-transplant management, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains possible. Follow-up was shorter in the post-2018 cohort, limiting assessment of long-term outcomes. Direct evaluation of CAV-specific outcomes was not feasible because the OPTN registry does not capture a dedicated CAV variable.

Conclusions

Donor coronary artery disease is not a uniform predictor of adverse outcomes following heart transplantation but rather an age- and era-dependent risk factor. In the post-2018 era, especially among older donors, DTCAD was linked to increased mortality. These results indicate that DTCAD should be evaluated considering donor age, disease severity, and recipient clinical status. These data support implementing a risk-stratified donor selection strategy to optimize outcomes and maintain opportunities for safe expansion of the donor pool.
