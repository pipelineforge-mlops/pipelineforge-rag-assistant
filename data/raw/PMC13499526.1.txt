Aging and Alzheimer's disease are complex processes marked by continuous neuronal loss, disrupted neural networks, and cognitive decline. The past decade has seen advances in genomics, proteomics, and single-cell RNA sequencing, enabling the discovery of cellular markers with distinct gene expression patterns. This review aims to compile recent markers of aging and Alzheimer’s disease in immune, glial, and neuronal cells, which are increasingly vital for enhancing diagnostic precision and monitoring disease progression. Markers such as Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) and Cluster of differentiation 33 (CD33) facilitate immune responses and function as indicators of neuroinflammation and amyloid-beta clearance; Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and aquaporin-4 serve as indicators of gliosis and impaired interstitial fluid drainage; Postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95), Synaptosomal-associated protein 25 (SNAP25), and aberrant tau phosphorylation signify synaptic degradation and cytoskeletal instability characteristic of Alzheimer's pathology. The breakdown of the blood-brain barrier is associated with endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1). The markers were identified using cutting-edge technologies that unveiled variations in gene expression across cell types, brain regions, and disease stages. This cellular heterogeneity improves understanding of Alzheimer's disease (AD) progression and brain aging, clarifies molecular pathways, and may be used for prognostic and diagnostic purposes after thorough validation. Novel cellular markers with altered expression profiles are still being identified through ongoing research, which could be crucial for improving our understanding of and ability to treat neurodegenerative diseases.

1 Introduction

The distinction between normal brain aging and the onset of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's has long posed a challenge to the neuroscience community (Boccardi et al., 2024, Dai et al., 2024a; Hayat et al., 2025). Although it is widely acknowledged that Alzheimer's disease has age as a fundamental risk factor, the underlying mechanisms that distinguish brain aging from disease development remain unclear (Dunn et al., 2023, Heuer et al., 2024, Hong et al., 2024). The latest breakthroughs in single-cell analysis and multi-omics research have identified cellular markers and cell-type-specific differences, providing crucial insights into disease mechanisms and the development of novel diagnostics (Huang et al., 2022, Mesecar et al., 2023, Muralidharan et al., 2025, Niu et al., 2023, Stanley et al., 2025). The complex relationships between cellular and molecular differences of the aging brain must be clarified. The brain undergoes morphological, anatomical, physiological, and chemical changes during aging. These changes affect the brain's physiological and functional capabilities (Hayat et al., 2025). Despite their significance, their exaggeration could potentially trigger neurodegenerative diseases (Hayat et al., 2025). The link between cortical thickness and the volitional loss of memory in older individuals has been identified, revealing the structural foundation of the normal cognitive impairment associated with increasing brain aging (Eich et al., 2021). It is essential, however, to distinguish regular age-related changes from those indicative of active disease, such as the accelerated brain aging seen in neurodegenerative disorders (Dai et al., 2024a; Doering et al., 2025). Machine learning algorithms have also been applied in neuroimaging studies to classify expected patterns of brain aging from Alzheimer's disease-specific changes by identifying unique spatial atrophy patterns (Doering et al., 2025, Habuza et al., 2022).

Research identifying new biomarkers, particularly those specific to certain cell types and their roles in aging and Alzheimer's disease, is a crucial area of study. Microglia act as the resident cells of the brain's immune system, and their roles are imperative in both normal aging and Alzheimer's disease (Kenkhuis et al., 2022, Malvaso et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023). Their functional plasticity with age and disease makes them ideal subjects for single-cell studies (Stanley et al., 2025). Microglial senescence and activation are key features not only of normal brain aging but also of Alzheimer's disease, underscoring their value as sentinels of endogenous and exogenous damage (Malvaso et al., 2023). Single-cell RNA sequencing studies have revealed major biological features of aging in microglia, including a wide array of transcriptomic changes across significant brain cell populations, such as the induction of pro-inflammatory responses in the aging brain (Zhou & Glass, 2025). Single-cell transcriptomes of the cortex in comparative analyses of neurodegenerative diseases identified both common and disease-specific alterations in cellular communication, underscoring the importance of cell-type-specific processes in neurodegenerative progression (Muralidharan et al., 2025, Stanley et al., 2025). The possibilities for identifying biomarkers using extracellular vesicles obtained from brain tissue have shown encouraging results, and studies have found the abundance of key proteins and cell-type-specific proteins to be dysregulated in Alzheimer’s disease-associated vesicles (Huang et al., 2022, You et al., 2022). Blood biomarkers offer a more appealing option owing to their non-invasive nature and utility as early predictors of the disease (Sekimori et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e). Abnormal immune-related phagocytosis pathways identified using gene expression profiling of blood cells have been suggested to contribute to the development of Alzheimer’s disease (Park et al., 2020). Moreover, the presence of neuronal pentraxin−2 has been proposed as a predictive marker of defects in cerebrospinal fluid synaptic integrity in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (Haberman et al., 2025). Cellular senescence, or the cessation of cellular growth, has increasingly been recognized as a crucial determinant of neurodegeneration (DElla Torre et al., 2024, DiBattista et al., 2020, Sahu et al., 2022) (Fig. 1). Despite initial findings linking them to peripheral tissue degeneration, recent investigations have highlighted their role in distinguishing between normal brain aging and neurodegeneration (Azam et al., 2021, DiBattista et al., 2020). A variety of programmed cell death programs, such as parthanatos, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, have been found to contribute to aging and age-related brain disorders, thus establishing the complex nature of the cellular and molecular processes of Alzheimer’s disease (DElla Torre et al., 2024).Fig. 1 The circular graphic illustrates various regulated cell death pathways and their associations with neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting the interconnected nature of these mechanisms in age-related neuropathology (DElla Torre et al., 2024).

Moreover, another key aspect of the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease is neuroinflammation, which includes chronic inflammatory cytokine production, breakdown of the blood-brain barrier, and production of proinflammatory cytokines (Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e). Peripheral immune cells, specifically CD8 + T cells, have been linked to neuroinflammation and pathological inflammation in the Alzheimer’s disease brain, with both resident and peripheral macrophages exhibiting reduced phagocytic activity toward amyloid-beta and enhanced inflammation (Krishnan et al., 2020, Yamakawa and Rexach, 2024); see Fig. 2.Fig. 2 The graphic illustrates the complex interaction of amyloid pathology, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, immune cell activation, and inflammatory signaling pathways, all of which contribute to neuronal degeneration in Alzheimer's disease (Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e).

2 Methodology

The existing literature was reviewed to identify studies on novel cellular markers whose expression is altered in brain aging and Alzheimer's disease, using databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar, ResearchGate, OpenGrey, and the Cochrane Library. The search used keywords like “brain aging and Alzheimer’s Disease, OR Aging brain and AD” “Alzheimer’s disease Biomarkers,” “cellular markers,” “Novel biomarkers,” “gene expression,” “TREM2,” “CD33”, “aquaporin−4,” “PSD−95”, “SNAP25”, “tau phosphorylation”, “AD Biomarkers neuroinflammation,” “neuronal markers”, “glial markers”, and “immune cell markers”, “AD and single-cell”, AD and Proteineomics” combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR). Filters based on publication date and article type ensured relevant literature was included. Only peer-reviewed, reputable studies were considered, with a focus on recent publications from the past 10–116 years that provide current insights into cellular markers associated with brain aging and Alzheimer's disease, unless older studies remain relevant. Special emphasis was placed on research presenting direct experimental evidence of altered cellular marker expression and their roles in neurodegeneration, cognitive decline, and disease progression. The review included literature on immune cells, glial cells, neuronal cells, and vascular-associated cells to cover their full functional spectrum, examining both in vivo (animal models) and in vitro (cell culture) studies, particularly those utilizing advanced imaging, genomic, proteomic, transcriptomic, or genetic techniques.

3 Techniques for identifying AD markers

As one of the most challenging neurodegenerative disorders, AD is characterized by a progressive and inevitable cognitive deficit, accompanied by synaptic disruption, the formation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques, and hyperphosphorylated tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) (Kamatham et al., 2024, Safiri et al., 2024). Despite the successful understanding of AD using traditional technologies, high-resolution and multi-omics technologies are changing the research paradigm (Hossain and Hussain, 2025). High-resolution and multi-omics tools, including scRNA-seq, proteomics, and spatial transcriptomics, are enabling a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving AD through systems-level biology (Lee et al., 2025).

A significant paradigm shift in AD biology, particularly regarding the discovery of AD at the cellular level, has come from the application of scRNA-seq (Tang et al., 2009, Cen et al., 2026). Profiling of the genome expressed across all individual brain cells reveals profound, distinct profiles of cellular heterogeneity throughout normal and pathological brains. Through scRNA-seq of single nuclei from AD patients and controls, multiple cell types are uniquely dysregulated in AD, including diverse subtypes of microglia, astrocytes, and neurons (Rayaprolu et al., 2021). Disease-associated microglia (DAM), a specific microglial subpopulation highly enriched in AD brain tissues, has been identified as the main contributor to both Aβ clearance and neuroinflammation, and the astrocytic state plays a role in maintaining normal synaptic function. The identified cell types and their specific transcriptomic changes provide the underlying mechanisms by which genetic risk factors associated with AD (e.g., APOE and TREM2) influence the progression of the disorder (Wolfe et al., 2018, Luquez et al., 2022). Overall, cell-type-specific expression atlases facilitate further investigations on AD biology by paving the path toward precision medicine (Tang et al., 2009).

With a focus on the functional machinery of cells, the proteins themselves, omics complement transcriptomic data (Hossain and Hussain, 2025, Hemme et al., 2026). AD research has uncovered distinct microglial proteomic profiles using omics studies (Kamatham et al., 2024). Moreover, these studies have identified various post-translational modifications of proteins involved in NFT formation, such as phosphorylation of tau (Lee et al., 2025). In addition to revealing biomarkers useful for the diagnosis and prognosis of AD (e.g., Amyloid-beta fragments, phosphorylated tau), omics analyses of post-mortem AD brain and CSF identified specific biomarker candidates that could improve diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic capabilities for AD (Liu et al., 2025).

Omics bridge the transcriptional level to that of their corresponding protein products, confirming transcriptional changes and revealing functional pathways implicated in the disease (Hossain and Hussain, 2025). Omics, for example, can confirm transcripts that exist only transiently and provide evidence for pathways that would not be observed by transcriptomic analysis alone (Liu et al., 2025).

4 Genetic and Epigenetic regulation

New information is now shedding light on the importance of genetic and epigenetic control in the etiology of Alzheimer's disease (AD) (Pishva et al., 2020). DNA methylation changes that occur in the AD brain have been correlated with neuropsychiatric findings (Lang et al., 2022), with downregulation of synaptic gene expression in early stages separating AD from normal aging (Dunn et al., 2023), and alternative splicing patterns in cell types also correlate with neurodegenerative complications associated with aging (Farhadieh and Ghaedi, 2023a). The discovery of new cellular markers not only seeks to improve our current understanding of brain aging and the cause of Alzheimer's but will also aid in the development of practical diagnostic tools for earlier treatment. It is important to treat earlier, as abnormal protein buildup begins before the symptoms of clinical staging, potentially affecting the effectiveness of any therapeutic intervention (Sekimori et al., 2024). New therapeutic approaches are being sought, including stem-cell-based therapies, and the use of extracellular vesicles shows promise for controlling neuroinflammation and promoting neuronal regeneration (Ahmad et al., 2024, Bhatt et al., 2024, Rather et al., 2023).

In brief, it is a crucial area of study to identify cell-type-specific biomarkers that distinguish between normal brain aging and Alzheimer’s disease. Scientists have identified specific molecular biomarkers, dysfunctions in cell interactions, and predictive signs of pathological processes using cutting-edge techniques such as single-cell transcriptomics, multi-omics analysis, and advanced imaging. Such breakthroughs have immense potential to enhance the capacity for early diagnosis, thereby advancing the prevention and treatment of Alzheimer’s disease.

5 Markers of Alzheimer’s disease

5.1 Established markers

Although the presence of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, due to hyperphosphorylation of the tau protein, is a major pathological hallmark of Alzheimer's disease, their diagnostic and predictive power is not significant (Ahmad et al., 2024). This leads us to look for more specific and dynamic markers. The presence of amyloid plaques in people with normal cognition prompts us to seek more robust markers to differentiate pathological from physiological aging (Dai et al., 2024, Dunn et al., 2023). Conventional markers such as Aβ42 and tau-related pathological hallmarks play a crucial role in AD. In AD, Aβ42 peptides aggregate to form plaques, whereas abnormally phosphorylated tau proteins accumulate to form neurofibrillary tangles (Zhang et al., 2021). Both plaques and tangles are closely associated with neuronal injury and loss, as well as reduced cognition (Zhang et al., 2021). There are certain drawbacks in using these markers for diagnostic purposes. There may be a considerable gap between protein accumulation and disease manifestation, with a long preclinical phase in which diagnosis is not straightforward, but treatment could be effective (Egiguren-Ortiz et al., 2024). Moreover, distinguishing normal aging from AD is further complicated by similarities between the two (Dai et al., 2024, Dunn et al., 2023). Alongside proteinopathies, AD pathophysiology also includes neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and various forms of regulated cell death (Hayat et al., 2025).

The search for new biomarkers goes beyond these constraints by being attuned to living processes and changes in cell type. It should be noted that Alzheimer’s is marked by neuroinflammation, which includes the activation of microglia and astrocytes (Xu et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2021). Microglia undergo dramatic phenotypic and functional alterations, shifting from a homeostatic state to an inflammatory state. (Woodburn et al., 2021). Iba1, TMEM119, or P2RY12 is usually employed in the characterization of microglia, while the expression levels of these genes help indicate microglial response during disease progression (Kenkhuis et al., 2022, Walker, 2020). Microglial dysfunction in Aβ clearance leads to increased plaque deposition. Advances in spatial transcriptomics offer a greater perspective regarding microglial circuitry and communication among different cells (Piwecka et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e). Other immune cells, such as peripheral blood-derived macrophages and CD8 + T cells, have emerged as involved in neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's disease (Fig. 3). Additionally, aberrant immune phagocytic processes have been identified for peripheral blood cells to reveal Alzheimer's disease biomarkers potentially. Impairment of blood-brain barrier integrity allows greater invasion by neuroinflammatory and peripheral immune cells.Fig. 3 shows healthy and diseased neurons in Alzheimer's, highlighting mechanisms beyond amyloid-beta and tau proteins. It emphasizes interactions among microglia, astrocytes, and T cells that lead to inflammation and neuronal death. Extracellular vesicles from brain tissue are potential non-invasive biomarkers with cell-specific proteins essential for understanding Alzheimer's. Blood biomarkers are readily accessible and promising for early diagnosis. Cellular senescence, where cells stop dividing, is also important. Senescent cells in aging and Alzheimer's brains release inflammatory factors that drive neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Understanding the differences between healthy and diseased senescence is key to developing treatments.

Recently emerging evidence supports the important role of both genetic and epigenetic control in Alzheimer's disease (Migliore and Coppedè, 2022). The role of reduced synaptic gene expression in the initial stages as a demarcating feature between Alzheimer's disease and aging has been revealed, indicating an early molecular divergence. Alternative splicing, a cell-type-specific regulatory process that increases protein diversity, has increasingly been linked to age-related neurodegenerative diseases, opening promising avenues for understanding the pathology of Alzheimer's disease (Nik and Bowman, 2019). Thus, the limitations of traditional biomarkers underscore the urgent need to establish a broad spectrum of markers capable of adequately capturing the complexity of disease progression over time. Biomarkers should include neuroinflammation, immune responses, synaptic disturbances, and cellular senescence, to be noninvasive and potentially useful for early-stage identification. Moving towards a more accurate and dynamic model of identification will play a pivotal role in the effective management of Alzheimer's disease.

5.2 Cell-type-specific markers

In neuroscience, and more specifically in brain aging and neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease, there is a need for cell-type-specific markers due to the diversity of brain cells and each cell type's unique functions and roles (Duran et al., 2022; Farhadieh and Ghaedi, 2023; Patel et al., 2021). General brain markers often overlook cell-type-specific details crucial for understanding the onset and progression of diseases (Duran et al., 2022). The brain's complexity is represented through its cells: neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes (Duran et al., 2022). All these cells have unique functions. Neurons carry and process electrical and chemical impulses necessary for brain functions such as behavior and cognition. Astrocytes have functions that include metabolism and structural maintenance. Additionally, they modulate synapse functions and maintain the blood-brain barrier (Kenkhuis et al., 2022, Niu et al., 2023). Microglia are part of the brain's immune system and participate in its surveillance, phagocytosis, and inflammatory processes (Etebar et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2023). Oligodendrocytes have functions that include creating a myelin sheath around axons. Such enclosures enable efficient signal transmission (Duran et al., 2022). These cell types respond variably to aging, injury, or disease (Farhadieh and Ghaedi, 2023, Niu et al., 2023). In AD, neurons are directly affected by amyloid-beta plaques and tau protein, leading to synaptic impairment and neuronal cell death (Huang et al., 2022). Microglia are activated in response to damaging cues, converting from a resting to an activated phenotype, typically pro-inflammatory, that can be either beneficial for debris removal or damaging by inducing neuroinflammation (Kenkhuis et al., 2022, Xu et al., 2023). Iba1, TMEM119, or P2RY12 are markers that help determine the stages and role of microglia in the development of AD (Kenkhuis et al., 2022).

In Alzheimer's disease, astrogliosis is the reactive response of astrocytes to changes in gene expression, cellular proliferation, and involvement in inflammatory reactions and synaptic changes (Duran et al., 2022). Oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells are damaged by demyelination and impaired myelin repair, which are associated with white matter pathology in neurodegenerative disorders (Niu et al., 2023). The use of general biomarkers that track average gene expression across cells may mask initial cell-specific changes at a stage when there are no apparent symptoms of the disease. Transcriptional changes at the level of individual cell types can distinguish Alzheimer's patients from individuals who are aging normally, indicating that initial cell-specific biomarkers can be identified before severe neurodegenerative pathology develops using single-cell RNA sequencing approaches for studying such complexities to gain a comprehensive understanding of cell diversity, states, and perturbations associated with disorders (Duran et al., 2022).

The identification of cell-specific markers is essential for reasons, such as that detection may be achieved by recognizing cell-type-specific modifications that occur even before the onset of symptoms (Niu et al., 2023); cell-specific markers improve our understanding of mechanisms by showing how interactions between cells affected by diseases may influence their progression (Duran et al., 2022), and playing a significant role in developing personalized therapeutic approaches to modulate detrimental cell states and maintain beneficial ones (Huang et al., 2022, Palma, 2024). Finally, they help to clearly differentiate between healthy aging and neurodegeneration because, in disease conditions, neurodegeneration may be pathological. Extracellular vesicles, specifically of brain origin, appear to be a promising group of biomarkers because they carry a set of proteins and nucleic acids that could inform about cell-specific pathological processes (Huang et al., 2022). Cell-specific markers help distinguish between physiological and pathological processes in aging and neurodegeneration, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy (Huang et al., 2022). Extracellular vesicles, especially those of brain tissue origin, are another promising biomarker type, as they contain proteins and nucleic acids that provide information about their cell of origin (Farhadieh & Ghaedi, 2023)

5.3 Novel Markers in AD

Novel biomarkers in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) define pathologically distinct states in microglia, astrocytes, and neurons that are markedly different from those of healthy aged individuals (Abukuri, 2024, Shineman et al., 2013). Markers such as phospho-tau, neurofilament light, and glial activation antigens support the finding that Alzheimer’s disease is more than accelerated aging, indicating it is a distinct form of neurodegenerative disease (Perrin et al., 2011). Dementia occurs more frequently in AD in the overall population, presenting progressively with declining cognition, amyloid-β (Aβ) deposits, and tau-positive neurofibrillary tangles in the central nervous system, which are characterized by abnormally high levels of phospho-tau (Karikari et al., 2022, Lewczuk et al., 2004). Over the last few years, molecular fingerprinting techniques, such as biomarker identification, have enabled the search for new markers for diagnosing these diseases. These markers demonstrated end-predicative capacity not only for predicting diagnoses but also for elucidating the pathomechanisms of the targeted diseases (Korologou-Linden et al., 2024, Öhrfelt et al., 2016, Walczak et al., 2024). More importantly, these markers segregate AD from the biological processes underlying healthy aging, highlighting the disease's unique pathological reprogramming (Nativio et al., 2018).

One of the most important findings in biomarkers to date has been the detection of phosphorylated tau proteins (p-tau181, p-tau217, or p-tau231) in blood plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Fu et al., 2025, Milà-Alomà et al., 2022). These biomarkers are strongly associated with tau pathology in the brain and exhibit greater diagnostic accuracy than conventional total tau measurement (Ashton et al., 2021). Likewise, the protein neurofilament light chain (NfL) has been recognized as a marker of neuronal damage and neurodegenerative changes at all stages of AD (Benedet et al., 2021). These neuronal biomarkers illustrate the rapid progression of neurodegenerative pathology characteristic of AD, as opposed to the slow progression of pathology associated with normal aging (Alawode et al., 2021). In the context of neuronal biomarkers, the role of glial activation patterns has come to the forefront in AD research (Athanasaki et al., 2025, Jamal et al., 2025). Microglia, the resident immune cells within the brain, become disease-associated microglia (DAM) upon Aβ accumulation. DAM expression involves the induction and upregulation of genes, including TREM2, APOE, CST7, and LPL, which are generally associated with phagocytosis and immunology (Yang et al., 2025). It should be noted that this condition serves as an attempt at homeostasis and protection, as well as an environmental factor that concurrently drives chronic biologically induced inflammation in the brain. In healthy brains, microglial homeostasis, in contrast, maintains the expression of genes such as P2RY12 and CX3CR1 (Lin et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025).

Astrocytes also undergo a profound change in AD, trending toward reactive astrogliosis, characterized by hypertrophy and increased expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), complement proteins (C3), and inflammatory mediators (Botella Lucena & Heneka, 2024). CHI3L1, also called YKL−40, and SERPINA3 are novel astrocytic markers implicated in disease progression and cognitive decline (Ahmad et al., 2024, Althammer et al., 2023). Astrocytes in healthy aging show only a mild metabolic decline compared with those in AD, which adopt a distinctly pro-inflammatory phenotype that contributes to synaptic dysfunction and the breakdown of the blood-brain barrier (Liu et al., 2024). 5.4 Other biomarkers

Biomarkers that show different aspects of AD pathogenesis, such as aquaporin 4 (AQP4), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD 95), synaptosomal-associated protein 25 (SNAP25), and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM 1), have recently been identified by researchers (An et al., 2021, Silva et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e).

AQP4, a water channel that concentrates in astrocytic end-feet adjacent to cerebral vessels, facilitates the drainage of cerebral metabolites, such as the accumulation of the amyloid beta protein, via the glymphatic pathway, and an appropriate AQP4 localization is thus associated with successful glymphatic flux (Subramanian et al., 2020). It has been established that mislocalization of AQP4 away from the end-feet reduces fluid drainage through the glymphatics and can also increase amyloid-beta levels (Silva et al., 2021). Hence, AQP4 can be considered a marker of astrocytic functions that contribute to AD's neurovascular impairments; it represents a potential therapeutic target to enhance amyloid-beta clearance (Silva et al., 2021). PSD 95 is a postsynaptic density scaffolding protein fundamental for the proper functioning and plasticity of synaptic connections. Loss of synaptic connections is well documented in AD patients and is considered one of the strongest pathological correlates of AD patients’ cognitive decline. Its levels are increased in CSF as a result of such synaptic pathology (Subramanian et al., 2020). It has also been noted that PSD−95 levels correlate with other forms of pathology (e.g., Down's Syndrome), and its correlations with amyloid and tau pathology, as well as clinical features, make it a useful biomarker of synaptic involvement in AD (Yook et al., 2024).

SNAP25 presynaptically drives neurotransmitter release by the SNARE complex. Levels of SNAP25 in AD cerebrospinal fluid are increased in preclinical AD, and the levels are positively associated with amyloid load, tau burden, and cortical thinning (Wolner et al., 2025). SNAP25 is therefore a powerful biomarker for synaptic dysfunction preceding significant neurodegeneration and predicting transition to AD dementia (Wolner et al., 2025). Endothelial nitric oxide synthase, which regulates vascular tone and cerebral blood flow, regulates the release of nitric oxide (NO). Reduced eNOS activity in AD contributes to endothelial dysfunction, reduced CBF, and impaired cerebrovascular coupling (Ahmed et al., 2022). NO imbalance potentiates oxidative stress and enhances amyloid formation (An et al., 2021).

VCAM−1 is an endothelial cell-surface protein that binds leukocytes and plays a role in inflammatory signalling. BBB damage can increase BBB permeability, contributing to white matter disease in AD and facilitating leukocyte entry into the brain, thereby perpetuating inflammation (Haarmann et al., 2015). VCAM−1 as a potential peripheral biomarker of vascular inflammation associated with AD, in addition to central markers of synaptic and neuronal dysfunction (Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e). Postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD−95), a structural component of the postsynaptic density, is implicated in the maintenance of glutamatergic function. PSD−95 loss in AD postsynaptic terminals is correlated with decreased spine density, dendritic damage, and is considered another marker of synaptic loss and AD progression (Chen et al., 2023).

6 Aging versus Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

Aging is one of the most important risk factors for the development of AD; however, the two conditions do not show the same signs (Moonen et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2025). Aging entails a progressive series of changes in the body’s functionality. These changes include reduced mental processing speed, reduced synaptic plasticity, and alterations in brain structure (Jack et al., 2018, Pereira et al., 2021). AD, on the other hand, is a pathological process in which the progressive changes observed in AD are accelerated, accompanied by specific molecular characteristics (Jack et al., 2018, Pereira et al., 2021). A comparison between aging and AD is interesting for understanding how specific biological processes that are harmless in aging become toxic in AD (Oh et al., 2023). 7 Vital Discovery in relation to microglia, astrocytes, and neurons

Microglia and astrocytes initiate neuroinflammatory networks that influence the neuronal susceptibility factor in the context of AD (Althammer et al., 2023). Following amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, microglia enter reactive states that alternate between plaque clearance and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, which primarily exacerbate synaptic damage, while astrocytes become reactive in functions related to glutamate metabolism and the blood-brain barrier in AD (Cruz et al., 2023). Neuronal cells downstream of glial signaling undergo synaptic damage, tau hyperphosphorylation, and network disruption, which are exacerbated by cytokine release from reactive glia and complement activation in AD (Jiang & Bhaskar, 2017). These cellular associations render neuroinflammation both a consequence and an inducer within AD, thereby highlighting glial states as essential determinants of disease prognosis, from Aβ formation to massive neuronal cell death in AD patients (Farfara et al., 2008, Goel et al., 2022).

Single-cell analyses now reveal that specific microglial phenotypes, including but not limited to disease-associated microglia, along with reactive astrocytes, differentially affect Aβ plaque compaction, synaptic pruning, and metabolic robustness with greater resolution than bulk analyses (Wood et al., 2025). These cell-type-specific details enable mechanistic definition at higher resolution, with particular attention to microglial TREM2-dependent signaling pathways, Aβ handling, and astrocyte lipid metabolism. This improves connections between pathology and dementia (Lin et al., 2024, Yin et al., 2024). Importantly, cell-type resolution enables stratification of patients based on specific pro-inflammatory or metabolic programs and informs therapeutic strategy in early and prodromal patients (Olatokun et al., 2025).

8 Future research direction

These will involve validation, assessments of neuronal injury, longitudinal validation of glia-derived biomarkers, and validation of their use for prediction (Fagan and Perrin, 2012, Thal et al., 2006). Reproducibility assessments, thresholds for clinical use, and use of multimodal endpoints will be important for validation (McCarthy et al., 2023, Ye et al., 2022). Intervention trials will need to use a framework involving a glia-derived biomarker ensemble, for instance, based on microglial activation, to assess pharmacodynamics (Devanath et al., 2024, Lu and Koziol, 2025). Harmonized frameworks for assay, operational, and cross-calibrated methods will be critical for implementation (Devanath et al., 2024). Where the Goldberg model is particularly valuable is in moving beyond blood and cerebrospinal fluid assessments and unlocking new ways to leverage the role of glia, like soluble forms of TREM2, GFAP, and inflammatory cytokines, along with known neuronal injury markers NfL and tau (Roveta et al., 2024, Syk et al., 2024). The cerebrospinal fluid is the closest matrix available, reflecting modifications in the central nervous system regarding the signal-to-noise ratio for amyloid and tau (KOrecka and Shaw, 2021, Patil et al., 2026).

Plasma assays, on the other hand, are amenable to large-scale testing and biomarker monitoring, even on ultrasensitive platforms (Pais et al., 2023). Multimarker models incorporating measures of astrocytic GFAP, indices of microglial activation, and assessments of neuronal p-tau/NfL could improve diagnostic specificity between AD and other neurodegenerative diseases (Agnello et al., 2025, El Abiad et al., 2024, Tolar et al., 2024). Cell type-specific biomarkers enable earlier diagnosis by targeting prodromal changes, such as microglial metabolic alterations and astrocyte reactivity, that occur before actual neuronal damage (Lu and Koziol, 2025, Volonté et al., 2026). At the therapeutic level, modulation of microglial receptors (e.g., TREM2), the complement pathway, and astrocyte lipid and glutamate metabolism may represent an upstream strategy to alter the drivers of the disease, even before tau spread (Dias and Socodato, 2025, Li et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang et al., 2024c, Zhang et al., 2024d, Zhang et al., 2024e). Combining these treatments with appropriate biomarkers allows for an adaptive therapeutic strategy, setting the optimal timing and dose to target the disease process before the advent of irreparable network changes (Bougea and Gourzis, 2024, Hampel et al., 2017, Hampel et al., 2019).

9 Conclusions

Novel biomarkers like phosphorylated tau, NfL, DAM genes, and astrocytic proteins have revolutionized our understanding of Alzheimer’s Disease, improved diagnosis, and identified disease-specific cell states. Research indicates the potential to use multiple biomarkers clinically for better monitoring, earlier detection, and mechanism-targeting therapies. Incorporating glial, astrocytic, or neuronal biomarkers can reveal processes such as inflammation or metabolic stress that led to neuronal injury; glial modulators, astrocytic functions, or complement activation are promising targets. Fluid biomarkers will help track disease progression, enabling biomarker-guided trials of multitarget therapies that restore glial function and synaptic stability, thereby improving AD management.
