Background

Celiac disease (CD) is a chronic, immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by gluten that affects about 1% of the population and may present with extraintestinal manifestations. Pulmonary complications are considered rare and are poorly characterized. Lane-Hamilton syndrome is the best described lung manifestation, but data suggests a broader spectrum of respiratory involvement.

Methods

We performed a narrative review with structured search strategy of PubMed and Embase (January 1, 1970–December 1, 2025) using the terms celiac/coeliac and lung, pulmonary, respiratory, hemoptysis, hemosiderosis, alveolar hemorrhage, interstitial lung disease, alveolitis, bronchiectasis, bronchiolitis, asthma, pneumonia, pneumococcal, sarcoidosis and histiocytosis. We analyzed all available data describing patients with CD and respiratory disorders.

Results

Pulmonary involvement in CD appears to represent one of its extraintestinal manifestations, alongside hematologic, dermatologic, neurologic, hepatic, and skeletal. LHS is well defined: several systematic reviews identified dozens of pediatric and adult cases. Increased risk of pneumonia (especially pneumococcal) is demonstrated in observational studies and described in systematic reviews. Moreover, CD has been associated with interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic cough.

Conclusions

Pulmonary involvement in CD is uncommon but clinically important. For hospital internists, a bidirectional approach is suggested: to screen for CD in selected pulmonary presentations, and to systematically assess respiratory symptoms in patients with CD.

1. Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is a chronic, immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten in genetically susceptible individuals, characterized by atrophy of intestinal villi, crypt hyperplasia and intraepithelial lymphocytosis.1–5 It affects approximately 1% of Western population2–4 and is increasingly recognized worldwide across all age groups.2–7 Current evidence underscores that CD is a systemic autoimmune disorder rather than a condition confined solely to the gastrointestinal tract.1–3,6,7.

Extraintestinal manifestations are common. They include iron deficiency anemia (IDA), osteoporosis, infertility, dermatitis herpetiformis, neurologic syndromes and liver disease.8–11 Pulmonary involvement has traditionally received little attention and is usually mentioned only briefly in reviews of extraintestinal manifestations.8–11 The most well-characterized association is Lane– Hamilton syndrome (LHS), defined by the coexistence of CD and idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (IPH).12–23 However, over recent decades, numerous studies have described other pulmonary manifestations, including interstitial lung disease (ILD), bronchiectasis, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), increased susceptibility to pneumonia, and chronic cough.

To date, no dedicated review has synthesized the full spectrum of pulmonary manifestations associated with CD, as existing reviews have largely focused on isolated entities - most notably LHS - or on extraintestinal manifestations in general. The present review is therefore unique in providing a comprehensive overview spanning parenchymal, airway, vascular, and infectious respiratory involvement in CD.

2. Methods

We conducted a narrative review with a structured search strategy. PubMed and Embase were searched from January 1, 1970 to December 1, 2025 using combinations of the terms: celiac/coeliac and lung, pulmonary, respiratory, hemoptysis, hemosiderosis, alveolar hemorrhage, interstitial lung disease, alveolitis, bronchiectasis, bronchiolitis, asthma, pneumonia, pneumococcal, sarcoidosis and histiocytosis. We also screened reference lists of key guidelines and reviews on CD and IPH.

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori. We included peer-reviewed publications (January 1, 1970–December 1, 2025) reporting human data or systematic syntheses on pulmonary involvement in CD, including case reports, case series, cross-sectional studies, cohort/register studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. CD was considered present when diagnosed with biopsy and positive serology tests (IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (IgA-TG2) or endomysial (IgA-EMA) antibodies, or IgG-based assays in IgA deficiency). Pulmonary outcomes of interest comprised all reported CD-associated pulmonary manifestations, without restriction by respiratory phenotype. We excluded non-human (including in-vitro) studies, conference abstracts without an accessible full text, duplicate publications, editorials or opinion pieces without original patient-level data, and reports, in which CD could not be reasonably confirmed. Searches were restricted to English-language texts; non-English articles with an English abstract were screened and included only if sufficient clinical and diagnostic details could be reliably extracted. After de-duplication, records were screened by title and abstract for relevance, full texts were reviewed when eligibility was uncertain, and additional articles were identified via backward citation tracking. Data extraction was performed using a template capturing study design, population, criteria used to diagnose CD, pulmonary phenotype, treatment and reported outcomes. When multiple publications appeared to describe overlapping patients, the most complete report was retained.

2.1. Evidence base and grading

The evidence base for lung involvement in CD is heterogeneous and predominantly low level. Among the studies included in this review, the majority were case reports or small case series (25 reports in total; references 12–20,24–39). Observational data consisted of 10 cohort or register-based studies and 6 cross-sectional physiologic or screening studies (references 40–53). Higher-level syntheses included four systematic/scoping reviews and one meta-analysis (references 21,22,54,55). Additional narrative reviews, one mechanistic immunology study, a Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis and a quality-improvement audit provided supporting biological and implementation insights (references 23,56–62).

Because the literature was dominated by case-based reports and observational designs, we did not perform a formal GRADE assessment. Instead, we synthesized findings using a pragmatic evidence-tier framework (Tiers 1–5) based on study design, replication, and ability to estimate comparative risk. However, this tiering is only intended to aid interpretability and should not be interpreted as a formal GRADE rating. Evidence tiers are as follows:

- Tier 1: Systematic/scoping review, meta-analysis of observational studies

- Tier 2: At least two population-based cohort/register studies

- Tier 3: Single population-based cohort/register study, cross-sectional studies or MR studies

- Tier 4: Systematic review of case reports/series or multiple independent case reports/series

- Tier 5: Isolated case reports only

3. Results

Reported CD-associated pulmonary manifestations fall into four groups: immune-mediated, interstitial, airway, and infectious. These phenotypes likely arise from immune dysregulation, which is described below.

3.1. Pathophysiologic links between intestine and lung in CD

Pulmonary involvement in CD reflects a convergence of autoimmunity and altered host defense. In the intestine, gluten exposure in genetically susceptible individuals initiates a T-cell–mediated inflammatory response.1–5,63,64 Because the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts share key features of mucosa-associated immunity, this immune response may extend beyond the gut through cell trafficking and mucosal homing, allowing activated lymphocytes and inflammatory mediators to recirculate and potentially contribute to airway or parenchymal inflammation in susceptible patients.64,65 Moreover, it is important to point out that CD-related autoantibodies (IgA-TG2 and IgA-EMA) are not restricted to the intestinal mucosa but can also be detected in extraintestinal tissues, including lungs. 1–3 These findings suggest that autoimmunity plays an important role in pulmonary injury in CD. However, exact mechanisms remain unclear, although several hypotheses have been proposed, including immune complex deposition in LHS.12–23,56.

A parallel pathway involves impaired antimicrobial defense, which may be particularly relevant for infectious and airway complications. Untreated CD is associated with functional hyposplenism, reducing clearance of encapsulated bacteria.24,40 Consistent with this, population-based observational studies demonstrate increased risk of pneumonia and IPD in CD.41–43,54,57 In addition, over-represented in CD immunodeficiency disorders - especially IgA deficiency - predispose to recurrent sinopulmonary infections,1–3,8–11 which may act as a downstream driver of airway injury and bronchiectasis.25–30.

3.2. Immune-mediated pulmonary involvement – Lane-Hamilton syndrome

LHS was first described in 1971 in two adults with malabsorption and IPH in whom gluten-free diet (GFD) led to improvement in both gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms.12 Since then, numerous pediatric and adult cases have been reported.13–20.

A 2022 scoping review (20 manuscripts spanning 50 years) identified 23 children with LHS - hemoptysis was present at diagnosis in only about a half, whereas IDA and pulmonary infiltrates were near universal.21 A parallel adult-focused systematic review compared LHS with “isolated” IPH and found that patients with LHS were more likely to have anemia and gastrointestinal symptoms, but acute mortality was lower.22 Another review collated 80 LHS cases (44 children, 36 adults) reported between 1971 and 2020 and estimated acute mortality at approximately 14%.23 In these cohorts, CD and IPH were often diagnosed concurrently; in a substantial minority the CD diagnosis lagged by months to years, frequently after repeated hemorrhagic episodes.14–16,21–23.

Recent IPH state-of-the-art reviews and contemporary case reports continue to describe new LHS cases, including adults presenting with life threatening hemoptysis.31,56,58

Clinically, LHS presents with hemoptysis, dyspnea, fatigue and IDA, often severe.13–23 Children may lack overt hemoptysis and instead present with unexplained anemia and recurrent pulmonary infiltrates, often resembling recurrent bacterial pneumonia.21,23

Computed tomography (CT) commonly shows bilateral ground glass opacities and patchy consolidations in the acute setting as well as reticular interstitial changes and traction bronchiectasis developing in chronic or recurrent disease.21–23 Lung biopsy reveals diffuse intra-alveolar hemorrhage, abundant hemosiderin-laden macrophages, and varying degrees of interstitial fibrosis, in the absence of histologic evidence of vasculitis.13–20,56 When lung biopsy cannot be performed, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) may be sufficient to confirm pulmonary hemosiderosis, when hemosiderin-laden macrophages are present.21–23.

CD in LHS may be symptomless. In the pediatric scoping review, only a minority of children had overt gastrointestinal symptoms at the time of IPH diagnosis; CD was often discovered by serologic screening prompted by refractory IDA.21,22.

The mechanism linking CD and IPH remains uncertain. Proposed explanations include immune complex–mediated pulmonary capillaritis triggered by gluten-related antigens, cross-reactive antibodies targeting basement membrane structures, shared HLA susceptibility and vitamin K deficiency in severe malabsorption.12–23,56.

Regardless of mechanism, multiple case reports and both pediatric and adult reviews highlight the therapeutic impact of strict GFD. Initiation of GFD, usually alongside systemic corticosteroids, is associated with reduced frequency and severity of hemorrhagic episodes as well as anemia resolution.13–23,31.

Recurrent hemoptysis and radiographic relapses are often associated with poor adherence or inadvertent gluten exposure.13–18,21–23.

3.3. Interstitial lung diseases

Early reports from the 1970s described diffuse ILD, fibrosing alveolitis, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis-like syndromes in association with CD.32–34 In one case, a young adult with CD had histopathologic findings of severe interstitial fibrosis,32 while another report described a teenager with CD and allergic alveolitis.34 However, the first controlled study, which compared 18 biopsy-proven CD patients with matched controls, found no clear excess of ILD, and differences in the diffusion capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) were not statistically significant.66 Similarly, a subsequent physiologic study of lung function in CD failed to demonstrate consistent signs of ILD.67

More recently, ILD in CD has been reported mainly as isolated cases. Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) has been described in a middle-aged woman with CD and autoimmune polyglandular syndrome,44 and nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) has been reported in a 55-year-old man in whom CD was uncovered during work-up for hemoptysis.45

Case reports and small series have also documented co-occurrence of sarcoidosis and CD.35,36,46,47 A systematic review and meta-analysis reported an increased risk of sarcoidosis in CD,55 and a Mendelian randomization (MR) study suggested a modest causal influence.59 Finally, two reports describe pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH) in young adults with CD, including one nonsmoker.37,38.

To sum up, all the data suggest that ILD may occur in patients with CD - but is rare and not clearly a primary pulmonary manifestation.

3.4. Airway diseases

3.4.1. Small airway disease

Tarlo et al. observed that patients with CD had higher prevalence of chronic cough and objective airflow limitation compared with matched controls, despite similar smoking history.66 Subsequent physiologic study confirmed subtle abnormalities in spirometry in CD cohorts, though most values remained within reference ranges, and clinical implications were uncertain.45,67

Several case reports suggest that CD may present with chronic cough and lymphocytic bronchoalveolitis that improves with GFD. Brightling et al. described a 53-year-old woman with persistent cough, lymphocytic bronchoalveolitis in BAL and CD; symptoms improved substantially after starting GFD.26 Hayat et al. reported an 8-year-old child whose only presenting complaint was chronic cough; evaluation revealed CD and symptoms resolved after GFD.27 Kalpana et al. described similar pediatric cases with chronic respiratory symptoms as atypical manifestations of CD.28

3.4.2. Asthma and COPD

Population-based studies indicate a modest association between CD and asthma. In a Finnish register study, children with CD had an increased risk of asthma compared with matched controls.48 Subsequent Swedish nationwide cohort showed a 1.6-fold increased risk of asthma in individuals with CD.49 Other pediatric studies have reported higher asthma prevalence among children with CD and explored perinatal and dietary factors that may contribute.50–52.

Conversely, patients with COPD may have an increased prevalence of undiagnosed CD, although causality is uncertain and confounded by smoking and chronic inflammation.35–38,44–53,55,59.

3.4.3. Bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis is one of the most striking airway manifestations reported in CD. Mahadeva et al. described a 48-year-old woman with extensive CT-proven bronchiectasis and newly diagnosed CD,25 while the case-based review “The Missing Link” highlighted a nonsmoking woman with idiopathic bronchiectasis and ultimately recognized CD.39 More recently, Ohanian et al. reported chronic bronchiectasis in an adult with CD, emphasizing the importance of systematic evaluation for underlying causes, including CD, in unexplained bronchiectasis. 29 Ghassa et al. described bronchiectasis combined with chronic sinusitis in a patient with CD, suggesting that impaired mucosal immunity and recurrent infections could link the disorders.30

Mechanistically, recurrent respiratory infections in the setting of hyposplenism, IgA deficiency, and nutritional deficits could predispose patients with CD to bronchiectasis.24–30,39–43,54,57.

3.4.4. Respiratory infections

Impaired antimicrobial defense in CD increases susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria, particularly Streptococcus pneumoniae.24,40 Several large cohort studies and systematic reviews support an increased risk of pneumonia and invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in CD. Zingone et al. demonstrated a higher risk of community-acquired pneumonia among adults with CD compared with matched controls.41 Tjernberg et al. found increased rates of IPD in Swedish patients with CD42 as well as demonstrated altered complement activation in response to S. pneumoniae, providing a potential mechanistic link.57 Simons et al. confirmed an elevated risk of pneumococcal infection in CD, recommending pneumococcal vaccination for adults and children with CD.54 Canova et al. showed increased risk of bacterial pneumonia and pneumococcal infections in young adults with CD.43

Despite this, multiple audits indicate that adults with CD are frequently undervaccinated against S. pneumoniae.60–62 Expert reviews now recommend that adults with CD be considered a high-risk group for pneumococcal disease and receive vaccination accordingly.54,60–62.

4. Summary

Table 1 provides a summary of the reported pulmonary manifestations in CD and the corresponding evidence (the evidence-tier framework is described in the Methods section). The limitations of evidence are evaluated in Table 2.

4.1. Knowledge gaps and future directions

Despite numerous case reports and several systematic reviews, important gaps remain:

The true prevalence and spectrum of pulmonary involvement in unselected CD populations are unknown. Most data derive from referral centers and may overrepresent severe cases.21–23, 32–34,39,45,66,67.

Mechanistic studies directly examining lung tissue, immune cell profiles and autoantibody deposition in CD-related lung disease are lacking.

Prospective data on the impact of GFD on non-hemorrhagic respiratory outcomes are minimal.25–30,39,44,45,48–53,66,67.

Implementation science around vaccination strategies in CD is still evolving, despite clear evidence of increased pneumococcal risk and persistent undervaccination.41–43,54,57,60–62.

5. Conclusions

Pulmonary involvement in CD is uncommon but clinically significant. The most firmly established association is LHS. Another well-documented association is the increased risk of pneumococcal pneumonia, underscoring the importance of appropriate vaccination strategies in this population. Beyond these associations, CD has been linked to a range of pulmonary conditions, including chronic cough, asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis, and ILD. However, the strength of evidence supporting these associations remains limited, as it is largely derived from case reports and small observational studies.

6. Practical implications

A bidirectional, clinically oriented approach is warranted. Pulmonary presentations in which CD screening should be considered:

- IPH or otherwise unexplained diffuse alveolar hemorrhage,

- bronchiectasis of unclear etiology,

- chronic cough or airway symptoms that are persistent or atypical, particularly when standard evaluation is unrevealing and systemic features suggest an extra-pulmonary disease,

- selected ILD presentations (UIP, NSIP, sarcoidosis, PLCH) when accompanied by features suggestive of gastrointestinal disease, recognizing that the current evidence for a direct ILD-CD relationship is limited,

- recurrent pneumococcal pneumonia.

Patient populations with CD, in whom additional surveillance should be emphasized:

- patients not vaccinated against S. pneumoniae,

- patients with features of impaired host defense (hyposplenism, IgA deficiency, recurrent sinopulmonary infections),

- patients with poor nutritional status, delayed diagnosis, or ongoing active disease.

For these groups, clinicians should ensure that pneumococcal vaccination is up to date (according to local recommendations) as well as maintain a low threshold to evaluate red-flag symptoms (hemoptysis, unexplained dyspnea, persistent radiographic abnormalities, recurrent infections).

Adopting this “from villi to alveoli” perspective can facilitate earlier recognition of rare but treatable pulmonary presentations while strengthening preventive care - particularly vaccination and infection- risk mitigation - for the broader population of patients with CD.
