Purpose

Resistance to adjuvant chemotherapy following enucleation continues to drive recurrence and metastasis in high-risk advanced retinoblastoma (RB), posing a major clinical challenge. To identify candidate genes, a previous transcriptomic analysis revealed markedly reduced expression of Rho GTPase-activating protein 9 (ARHGAP9) in the etoposide-resistant Y79/EDR cell line. This study aims to further investigate the effect of ARHGAP9 on chemoresistance in high-risk advanced RB.

Methods

Whole-exome sequencing and reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing profiles of eight peripheral blood samples from patients with high-risk advanced RB were analyzed to identify genes with germline genetic or epigenetic alterations associated with RB prognosis. In vitro and in vivo studies on drug responsiveness and molecular mechanisms were conducted in stable ARHGAP9-silenced and ARHGAP9-overexpressing cell lines.

Results

ARHGAP9 was identified through peripheral blood–based genomic and epigenomic analyses and further validated via functional study. Functional assays demonstrated that ARHGAP9 knockdown considerably decreased sensitivity to carboplatin and etoposide, whereas its overexpression restored drug responsiveness in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic analysis indicated that ARHGAP9 downregulation markedly enhanced Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1) activity as its catalytic substrate, promoted Rac1–β-catenin interaction, and facilitated their nuclear accumulation. This process induced the formation of lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (LEF1)–β-catenin complex and increased expression of downstream proteins, such as c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2, which contribute to chemotherapy resistance in RB.

Conclusions

These findings provide mechanistic insights into how ARHGAP9 modulates drug responses via the Rac1/β-catenin/LEF1 signaling axis in RB and suggest that ARHGAP9 warrants further investigation as a candidate prognostic indicator or therapeutic target for advanced high-risk RB.

Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most common malignant intraocular tumor in infants and children, accounting for approximately 3% of pediatric tumors, with a global incidence of 1 per 15,000 to 20,000 live births.1 Chemotherapy constitutes the cornerstone of RB treatment and can be delivered via multiple routes. These include systemic intravenous chemotherapy (IVC) as well as regional/local approaches such as intra-arterial, periocular, and intrathecal chemotherapy.2 IVC remains the primary approach of chemoreduction, with the standard regimen being the “triple-drug regimen” consisting of vincristine, etoposide, and carboplatin (VEC).3 However, drug resistance arising from prolonged systemic chemotherapy remains the leading cause of treatment failure and recurrence in RB.4,5 Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying chemoresistance is essential for identifying novel therapeutic targets and improving clinical outcomes in patients with RB.

Several studies have demonstrated that Rho GTPase activating protein 9 (ARHGAP9) is strongly associated with tumor development, progression, and prognosis.6 As a critical member of the Rho-GTPase–activating protein (RhoGAP) family, it negatively regulates the intrinsic GTPase activity of Rho GTPases by hydrolyzing guanosine triphosphate (GTP) into guanosine diphosphate (GDP), thereby transforming Rho GTPases to their inactive states.7,8 Rho GTPases, as small GTP-binding proteins, contribute to tumor progression and dissemination by regulating diverse cellular processes, including cytoskeleton dynamics, cell motility, cell morphology and polarity, cell cycle progression, cell growth, survival, and differentiation.9 Nevertheless, limited research has investigated the role of ARHGAP9 in RB progression and prognosis.

ARHGAP9 contains four domains: a Src homology 3 domain, a WW domain, a pleckstrin homology region, and a RhoGAP domain.10,11 The RhoGAP domain of ARHGAP9 catalyzes the hydrolysis of GTP bound to Rho GTPases, thereby converting them to the inactive GDP-bound state.12 As a prominent member of the Rho GTPase family, ARHGAP9 exhibits GTPase-activating protein (GAP) activity toward Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1), inactivating it by hydrolyzing bound GTP to GDP and thereby regulating Rac1-dependent cellular processes, including cell morphology, cytoskeletal reorganization, and cell-matrix adhesion.13,14 Rac1 also forms a complex with β-catenin, activating it and promoting its dissociation from the cell membrane to the nucleus, thereby enhancing nuclear β-catenin accumulation in breast cancer cells.15 The binding of β-catenin to lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (LEF1) represents a crucial event for the initiation of downstream signaling pathways.16 To date, the involvement of ARHGAP9 and these proteins in RB chemotherapy remains unexplored. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing profiles of eight peripheral blood samples from patients with high-risk advanced RB were analyzed to identify genes with germline genetic or epigenetic alterations associated with RB prognosis. Based on these omics datasets, a germline single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) variation (rs11544238, 1108T>G missense variant) of ARHGAP9 was identified through WES analysis and validated by PCR-based genotyping.17 Moreover, ARHGAP9 expression was considerably reduced in etoposide-resistant Y79/EDR cells compared with parental Y79 cells. This RB subline, derived from Y79 cells through sustained drug exposure to etoposide, exhibited cross-resistance to carboplatin and vincristine,18 providing a valuable model for subsequent characterization in the present study.

To corroborate the association between this germline variation and RB prognosis, this study genotyped additional peripheral blood samples from 67 patients with advanced RB and similar treatments. The genotype was significantly more prevalent in remission cases than in recurrence cases, suggesting that ARHGAP9 confers prognostic advantages in advanced RB. Functional assays demonstrated that ARHGAP9 downregulation or upregulation modulates proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and susceptibility in Y79 or Y79/EDR cell lines, confirming its critical role in RB chemoresistance. Mechanistic investigations further elucidated the molecular mechanism by which ARHGAP9 regulates RB chemoresistance, providing insights into its role in prognosis prediction and the development of targeted therapies for advanced, high-risk RB in the future.

Materials and Methods

Association of ARHGAP9 With Prognosis in Patients With RB

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 67 patients with RB (44 with remission and 23 with recurrence) at Beijing Children's Hospital affiliated with Capital Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all recruited patients, and the study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Beijing Children's Hospital (Approval No. 2020-k291, IEC-C-008-A08-V.05.1) and the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Medicinal Biotechnology (IRB No. 2014-quick review of scientific research-007). All peripheral blood samples were obtained from patients histopathologically diagnosed with advanced high-risk RB (group D or E) according to the International Intraocular Retinoblastoma Classification. Patients received postoperative systemic chemotherapy with either VEC or carboplatin, cyclosporine A, teniposide, and vincristine. According to the standard treatment protocol, patients with RB typically receive a total of six cycles of systemic chemotherapy. In cases where the initial response is insufficient or if there is disease progression, treatment may be extended to a maximum of eight cycles before considering enucleation. Prognostic outcomes were determined after a follow-up period of more than 2 years. Among the 23 patients who experienced recurrence, the recurrence patterns included both orbital disease and metastatic disease. Specifically, orbital recurrence presented with features such as tumor regrowth at the primary site, neovascularization of the iris, complete retinal detachment, and, in severe cases, intracranial invasion through the skull. Distant metastasis was confirmed, with features including choroidal involvement in the contralateral eye, detection of tumor cells in the cerebrospinal fluid, and disease-related mortality.

Four cases from patients with remission and recurrence were subjected to WES by Shenzhen Huada Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) using the Illumina HiSeq platform. Candidate genes and variants were subsequently screened according to established criteria. For genotype identification, PCR resequencing was performed in all 67 patients by Beijing Liuhe Huada Genetic Science Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) The primers for rs11544238 in ARHGAP9 are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Based on the allele model, the association between the target allele (G allele, exposure factor) and the clinical outcome of disease recurrence (outcome event) was assessed. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated directly from a 2 × 2 contingency table of allele counts via the standard formula (OR = ad/bc). The 95% confidence interval was derived via the Wald method (log-transformation).

Analysis of ARHGAP9 Expression in Pan-Cancer and Its Association With Pathological Stage, Prognosis, and Drug Sensitivity

ARHGAP9 mRNA expression in tumor and normal samples was assessed using the Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource (TIMER, https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/). Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon test, a nonparametric method suitable for expression data that do not assume normality. The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) database, specifically the stage plot function in the expression DIY module, was applied to examine the association between ARHGAP9 expression and pan-cancer pathologic stage. The pan-cancer prognostic value of ARHGAP9 was evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/), which includes over 30,000 samples from 21 cancer types, along with univariate Cox regression analysis. Patients were divided into high and low expression groups based on the median transcript level of ARHGAP9. The gene set cancer analysis (GSCA, https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/drug) was employed to investigate the correlation between gene expression and drug sensitivity using two databases: Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP).

Cell Culture

The human RB cell line Y79 was obtained from the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). Y79/EDR cells, previously established to be resistant to etoposide, carboplatin, and vincristine,18 were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 (RPMI-1640) medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide.

Chemicals

Puromycin dihydrochloride (ST551) was sourced from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Carboplatin (HY-17393), etoposide (HY-13629), NSC23766 (HY-15723), MSAB (HY-120697), and SKL2001 (HY-101085) were obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

Stable Cell Line Construction

Stable ARHGAP9 overexpression and knockdown cell lines were generated via lentiviral infection. Lentiviruses were obtained from Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). ARHGAP9 overexpression constructs were generated using the commercial lentiviral vector GV492. The expression cassette consisted of a Ubi promoter driving the gene of interest, followed by a 3× FLAG tag. A separate CBh promoter controlled the expression of a gcGFP–IRES–puromycin reporter/selection cassette. The full-length coding sequence of human ARHGAP9 was cloned into the vector using BamHI and AgeI restriction sites. An empty vector (CON335) lacking the ARHGAP9 insert served as the control. ARHGAP9 knockdown constructs were prepared using the GV493 commercial lentiviral vector. The vector features an hU6 promoter driving the expression of a short hairpin RNA (shRNA), followed by a separate CBh promoter controlling a gcGFP–IRES–puromycin reporter and selection cassette. A shRNA specifically targeting human ARHGAP9 was designed and inserted into the multiple cloning site. A nontargeting control shRNA vector (CON313), containing a scrambled insert (5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′), was used as the control. Cells were infected with lentivirus according to the manufacturer's protocol. A multiplicity of infection of 100 was applied using HiTransG P, an infection-enhancing reagent. After 72 hours, cells were cultured in fresh medium containing 1 µg/mL puromycin. Successful overexpression and silencing were confirmed by real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) and Western blot analysis.

Cell Proliferation Assays

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. At 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CK04, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) solution was added and incubated at 37°C for 4 hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Colony Formation Assays

MethoCult H4034 Optimum (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) was used to assess the clonogenic capacity of cells. Cells were resuspended in a mixture of fresh medium and H4034 (v/v, 1:9) and seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well. Cultures were incubated for 10 to 15 days and terminated when each colony contained more than 50 cells. Colonies were counted and photographed. The colony formation rate was calculated using the following formula: Colony formation rate (%) = Number of colonies / Number of seeded cells × 100%.

Real-Time RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (version 2; RC112-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription was performed with 1 µg total RNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (R323; Vazyme). Quantification was conducted using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (RR820B; TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.19 Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Drug Susceptibility Analysis

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells in 180 µL per well. Subsequently, 20 µL carboplatin or etoposide at varying concentrations was added, with PBS serving as the control. After 72 hours, CCK-8 solution was added to the wells and incubated for 4 hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek). Drug concentration–cell viability curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 5; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). IC50 values were calculated using SigmaPlot (version 10.0; San Jose, CA, USA) with a four-parameter logistic curve-fitting model. Each concentration was tested in triplicate, and experiments were repeated with three independent biological replicates. No data points were excluded as outliers.

Flow Cytometry

A total of 1 × 106 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with carboplatin (10 µM) or etoposide (250 nM) for 48 hours. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V–phycoerythrin/7-aminoactinomycin D (Annexin V-PE/7-AAD) double staining apoptosis detection kit (KGA1104; KeyGene BioTECH, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Staining buffer, propidium iodide (PI), and RNase A (C6031; US Everbright, Beijing, China) were used to evaluate cell cycle distribution. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Cell cycle distribution was modeled using ModFit LT with the default debris elimination and aggregate modeling (AutoDebris/AutoAggregate) enabled. The coefficient of variation of the G0/G1 peak was <8% for all samples. Apoptosis was analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA). The gating strategy included (1) exclusion of debris via FSC-A/SSC-A, (2) singlet gate using FSC-H/FSC-A, and (3) annexin V/PI quadrant gates set based on unstained and single-stained controls. Compensation was automatically calculated using FlowJo's compensation matrix and verified manually.

In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy

The RB xenograft model was established by subcutaneous injection of 1 × 107 cells suspended in PBS and Matrigel (354248; Corning, Corning, NY, USA) into the right flank of NTG mice. NTG is a severe combined immunodeficiency mouse strain generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of the IL2rg (interleukin 2 receptor subunit gamma) gene in NOD-SCID mice carrying a Prkdc (protein kinase, DNA-activated, catalytic polypeptide) gene mutation. Five-week-old female NTG mice were purchased from SiPeiFu (Beijing, China). The mice were divided into four groups (n = 3 per group) according to the type of cells injected: the Y79-shNC group, the Y79-shARHGAP9 group, the Y79/EDR-EV group, and the Y79/EDR-ARHGAP9 group. When tumor volumes reached 400 to 500 mm3, the mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxiation, and the tumors were excised, cut into 1 × 2 × 3–mm fragments, and transplanted subcutaneously into 5-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (n = 15 per group). Mice were randomly assigned to groups after tumor fragment transplantation. Tumor volume measurements were performed by an investigator blinded to the group allocation. Mice were excluded pretreatment if no palpable tumor formed after 2 weeks or if the initial tumor volume was an outlier (>2 SD from the group mean). Mice received intraperitoneal injections of carboplatin (50 mg/kg) or etoposide (20 mg/kg) once per week (n = 5 per group). The Y79-shNC group and Y79/EDR-EV group served as controls for the Y79-shARHGAP9 and Y79/EDR-ARHGAP9 groups, respectively. Tumor volume and body weight were measured twice weekly. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: V = (Length × Width²) / 2, where length is the longest diameter and width is the perpendicular shorter diameter. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Growth curves were generated using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Four weeks after treatment, mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxiation, and tumors were harvested, weighed, photographed, and either fixed in paraformaldehyde or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All animal experiments were approved by the Committee of Laboratory Animal Center of Zhengzhou University (Ethics approval No. ZZU-LAC20231229[15]) and conducted in compliance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Immunohistochemistry Analysis

Paraffin-embedded tumor tissue samples were sectioned into 4-µm slices and mounted on glass slides (n = 3 per group). Sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using citric acid. Sections were incubated with a mouse anti–Ki-67 monoclonal antibody (1:400 diluted, GB121141; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 4°C overnight. The secondary antibody was HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (GB23301; Servicebio). It was incubated with the sections for 50 minutes at room temperature. Staining was visualized under an inverted microscope using a chromogenic reagent. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) quantification of Ki-67, whole-section images of each tumor sample were captured and analyzed. Instead of manual field selection, the Aipathwell digital pathology software (Servicebio) was used for automated tissue detection, which identified and delineated the entire tumor region on each slide to ensure comprehensive and unbiased coverage. The software, based on deep learning principles, performed automated color segmentation using the hue, saturation, and intensity color model, classifying staining intensity as weak (yellow, 1+), moderate (brownish-yellow, 2+), or strong (brown, 3+). Positive cells at each intensity level, total cell count, and tissue area were automatically calculated. The positive cell rate (number of positive cells / total number of cells) was used to quantify Ki-67 expression. All scoring and quantification were performed in a completely blinded manner; the pathologist and software operator were masked to sample identification and experimental group assignments throughout the analysis. Slides were coded, and data were collected before the codes were broken for statistical analysis.

Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction

Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions were separated using the Minute Cytoplasmic and Nuclear Fractionation Kit (SC-003; Invent Biotechnology, MN, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and rinsed once with cold PBS. Subsequently, cytoplasmic extraction buffer was added to the cell pellets, followed by vigorous vortexing for 15 seconds and incubation on ice for 5 minutes. Cytosolic fractions were obtained by centrifugation at maximum speed for 5 minutes in a microcentrifuge. Nuclear proteins were subsequently extracted using nuclear extraction buffer, followed by vortexing, incubation on ice, and centrifugation.

Rac1 Activity Assessment

Rac1 activity was quantified using the Active Rac1 Detection Kit (8815; Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The GTPγS and GDP treatments were indeed performed as the positive control and negative control to validate the specificity of the pull-down assay. Activated GTP-bound Rac1 was captured by the GST-PAK1-PBD fusion protein and subsequently immunoprecipitated with glutathione resin. These treated lysates were then subjected to the same affinity precipitation and Western blot analysis as the experimental samples.

Co-Immunoprecipitation Assay

Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed using the BeaverBeads Protein AG Immunoprecipitation Kit (22202; BEAVER, Suzhou, China). Cells were washed with PBS and lysed in IP binding buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. β-Catenin antibody (8480; Cell Signaling Technology) or normal mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) control (sc-2025; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) at a concentration of 20 µg/mL was conjugated to BeaverBeads. Cell lysate supernatants were incubated with BeaverBeads at 4°C overnight on a rotary mixer. Eluates were subsequently analyzed by Western blot.

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were harvested via centrifugation, washed with PBS, and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013C; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and protein concentrations were quantified using the bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime Biotechnology). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 4% to 12% gradient gel (ET10412Gel; ACE Biotechnology, Changzhou, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). PVDF membranes were then immunoblotted with primary antibodies against β-actin (AB0035; Abways, Shanghai, China), ARHGAP9 (ab101361; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), E-cadherin (CY1155; Abways), N-cadherin (13116; Cell Signaling Technology), vimentin (CY5134; Abways), Bcl-2 (CY6717; Abways), cyclin D1 (55506 T; Cell Signaling Technology), c-Myc (CY5150; Abways), β-catenin (CY3523; Abways), DKK2 (YN2130; Immunoway, San Jose, CA, USA), Lamin A/C (CY5222; Abways), Rac1 (ab155938; Abcam), and LEF1 (2230; Cell Signaling Technology). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Millipore) and quantified with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance for comparisons between two groups was assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test. For tumor volume and nude mouse body weight data, repeated-measures ANOVA was performed, with group as a between-subjects factor and time (10 time points) as a within-subjects factor. Mauchly's test of sphericity was applied; if violated (P < 0.05), the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used. Main effects of group and time, as well as their interaction, were examined. When a significant interaction was found, simple effects analysis with Bonferroni correction was conducted. Statistical significance was set at two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Favorable Prognosis Associated With ARHGAP9 Germline Variant (SNP rs11544238) in Patients With Advanced High-Risk RB

Previous omics analysis indicated that patients harboring the ARHGAP9 SNP (rs11544238) exhibited improved clinical outcomes.17 To validate the association between this variant and chemotherapeutic response in RB, 67 peripheral blood samples from patients with advanced RB were genotyped and analyzed. Genotyping of this locus in 67 patients (44 with remission and 23 with recurrence) revealed the variant in 25 patients achieving remission (including homozygous and heterozygous variants), representing 56.8% of this group, whereas only 21.7% of patients with recurrence (5 of 23) carried the variant. Conversely, 78.3% of patients lacking this homozygous variant (18 of 23) experienced relapse. As detailed in Figure 1A and the Table, the SNP exhibited a statistically significant association with remission (P = 0.0177), suggesting that the ARHGAP9 germline variant may enhance RB sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents, thereby contributing to a favorable prognosis.

ARHGAP9 Expression and Its Association With Prognosis and Drug Sensitivity in Patients With Pan-Cancer: Bioinformatic Analysis Using Public Databases

The association between ARHGAP9 expression and multiple tumors was examined using the TIMER database. ARHGAP9 was significantly upregulated in six tumors, including cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) (P < 0.05). In contrast, ARHGAP9 was significantly downregulated in bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) (P < 0.05). Furthermore, ARHGAP9 expression was higher in human papillomavirus (HPV)–positive HNSC tumors compared with HPV-negative HNSC tumors, while in skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), primary tumors exhibited lower expression than metastatic lesions (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Analysis of the association between ARHGAP9 expression and tumor progression across distinct cancer types revealed that ARHGAP9 expression exhibited stage-dependent patterns in KIRC, SKCM, STAD, breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and thyroid carcinoma (THCA) (Fig. 1C). The prognostic significance of ARHGAP9 expression in pan-cancer was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier plotter database and univariate Cox regression. In Kaplan–Meier analysis, patients with higher ARHGAP9 expression in BRCA, cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), HNSC, liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), LUAD, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), READ, sarcoma (SARC), thymoma (THYM), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) exhibited markedly longer overall survival (OS) than those with lower expression. In contrast, lower ARHGAP9 expression in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, KIRC, and KIRP was associated with significantly longer OS (Fig. 1D). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified ARHGAP9 as a critical risk factor for OS in KIRC, acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), brain lower-grade glioma (LGG), and uveal melanoma (UVM) and as a protective factor in CESC, HNSC, LUAD, SKCM, and UCEC (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Table S3).

In addition, ARHGAP9 expression correlated with sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic agents for RB, including etoposide, teniposide, and vincristine, as determined by GSCA analysis of the CTRP drug sensitivity and expression correlation (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Table S4). These findings suggest that ARHGAP9 expression is correlated with not only tumor prognosis but also drug responsiveness.

ARHGAP9 Influences Cell Proliferation and Chemosensitivity in RB as Determined by Knockdown in Y79 and Overexpression in Y79/EDR Cells

Based on bioinformatic and association analyses using both our RB datasets and public resources, ARHGAP9 was hypothesized to play a key role in chemotherapy responses in RB. To investigate its function in RB progression and chemosensitivity, stable cell lines were generated, including Y79 cells with shARHGAP9 knockdown and Y79/EDR cells with ARHGAP9 overexpression, and validated by real-time RT-qPCR and Western blot assays (Figs. 2A, 2B). CCK-8 and colony formation assays indicated that ARHGAP9 knockdown in Y79 cells enhanced cell proliferation and colony formation, whereas ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells markedly suppressed cellular proliferative capacity (Figs. 2C, 2D). ARHGAP9 knockdown decreased the sensitivity of Y79 cells to carboplatin and etoposide, as reflected by a significant increase in IC50 values. In contrast, ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells increased cell sensitivity to carboplatin and etoposide (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, ARHGAP9 knockdown in Y79 cells significantly reduced the proportion of cells in S phase and inhibited carboplatin- and etoposide-induced apoptosis. Conversely, ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells notably promoted S phase arrest and enhanced apoptosis (Figs. 2F, 2G; Supplementary Fig. S1).

Downregulation or Upregulation of ARHGAP9 Alters the Antitumor Effects of Carboplatin and Etoposide In Vivo

In the in vivo model, mice bearing Y79-shARHGAP9 or control tumors and mice bearing Y79/EDR-ARHGAP9 and Y79/EDR-EV tumors were treated four times with carboplatin (50 mg/kg) or etoposide (20 mg/kg) once weekly. Under carboplatin or etoposide treatment, tumor growth rates in the Y79-shARHGAP9 group were markedly higher than those in the Y79-shNC group, whereas growth of tumors in the Y79/EDR-ARHGAP9 group was slower than in the Y79/EDR-EV group (Fig. 3A). Similarly, the average weight of subcutaneous tumors was considerably greater in the Y79-shARHGAP9 group compared with the Y79-shNC group, while the average weight of subcutaneous tumors in the Y79/EDR-ARHGAP9 group was lower than that in the Y79/EDR-EV group (Figs. 3B, 3C). The body weights of the mice did not differ significantly between the beginning and end of drug administration, indicating that carboplatin and etoposide doses were well tolerated in mice (Fig. 3D). Consistent with the tumor growth rate, IHC staining for Ki-67 with semiquantification demonstrated that ARHGAP9 knockdown attenuated the antiproliferative effect of carboplatin and etoposide on tumor cells, whereas ARHGAP9 overexpression enhanced their antiproliferative efficacy (Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that ARHGAP9 knockdown diminishes, while ARHGAP9 overexpression enhances, the antitumor effects of carboplatin and etoposide in vivo.

ARHGAP9 Modulates RB Susceptibility via Rac1/β-Catenin/LEF1 Signaling

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ARHGAP9-mediated RB susceptibility, the expression of β-catenin and its target genes, including c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2, was assessed by real-time RT-qPCR and Western blot assays. These targets were significantly upregulated in ARHGAP9 knockdown Y79 cells, whereas ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells produced the opposite effect (Figs. 4A, 4B). Given the critical role of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in migration and invasion,20 protein levels of E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and vimentin were quantified as valuable biomarkers of cell migration and invasion. ARHGAP9 silencing in Y79 cells apparently reduced E-cadherin and N-cadherin expression while increasing vimentin levels. Conversely, ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells considerably suppressed these protein levels compared with the negative control EV in Y79/EDR cells (Fig. 4C).

ARHGAP9 exhibits GAP activity by converting active GTP-Rac1 to its inactive GDP-bound form. To assess the effect of ARHGAP9, the activity of Rac1 GTPase was measured using the Active Rac1 Detection Kit, with GTPγS and GDP serving as positive and negative controls, respectively. Rac1 activity was markedly increased following ARHGAP9 knockdown in Y79 cells, whereas it was significantly reduced upon ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells (Figs. 4D, 4E). Concurrently, ARHGAP9 knockdown considerably enhanced nuclear accumulation of Rac1 and β-catenin, whereas ARHGAP9 overexpression decreased their nuclear accumulation (Figs. 4F, 4G). Additionally, co-IP assays verified the interaction between β-catenin and Rac1 or LEF1. Endogenous Rac1 and LEF1 were co-immunoprecipitated from cell lysates with a β-catenin antibody. The interactions of β-catenin with either Rac1 or LEF1 increased after ARHGAP9 knockdown (Fig. 4H) and were inhibited following ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells (Fig. 4I).

To evaluate the effect of ARHGAP9 on chemotherapy response, Rac1 activity and the expression of β-catenin and its target genes, including c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2, were quantified in cells treated with carboplatin or etoposide. ARHGAP9 knockdown combined with those drugs enhanced Rac1 activity and increased protein levels of β-catenin, c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2 in Y79 cells, whereas ARHGAP9 overexpression in Y79/EDR cells significantly inhibited Rac1 activity and β-catenin signaling (Fig. 4J).

ARHGAP9-Mediated Rac1/β-Catenin/LEF1 Axis Modulates RB Susceptibility via Rac1 and β-Catenin Inhibition or Activation

To confirm that ARHGAP9 knockdown induces RB chemoresistance by enhancing Rac1 activity and to evaluate its effect on cellular susceptibility, the Rac1 inhibitor NSC23766 was administered in combination with chemotherapeutic agents. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that NSC23766 enhanced cellular sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs in ARHGAP9-knockdown Y79 cells, as indicated by a significant reduction in IC50 (Fig. 5A). NSC23766 also reversed the elevated expression of β-catenin and its downstream signaling proteins induced by ARHGAP9 knockdown (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that ARHGAP9 modulates RB susceptibility by regulating Rac1 activity and the downstream β-catenin pathway.

Furthermore, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway inhibitor MSAB and activator SKL2001 were employed to confirm that ARHGAP9 knockdown reduced RB chemosensitivity by activating Rac1 and the downstream β-catenin pathway (Figs. 5C–F). MSAB reversed the reduced chemosensitivity of ARHGAP9-knockdown RB cells, as validated by Western blot results exhibiting reversal of β-catenin downstream pathway activation and decreased expression of downstream proteins, including β-catenin, c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2 (Figs. 5C, 5D). Conversely, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling activator SKL2001 significantly reversed the enhanced chemosensitivity of RB cells with ARHGAP9 overexpression (Fig. 5E). As illustrated in Figure 5F, SKL2001 restored activation of the β-catenin downstream pathway induced by ARHGAP9 overexpression, leading to increased expression of β-catenin downstream proteins. These results indicate that ARHGAP9 modulates RB susceptibility by regulating Rac1 activity and the downstream β-catenin pathway, as confirmed by the effects of NSC23766, MSAB, and SKL2001.

Discussion

Chemoresistance remains the critical therapeutic barrier in advanced high-risk RB.21 Previous RNA sequencing data demonstrated a marked reduction of ARHGAP9 expression in Y79/EDR cells. Furthermore, the SNP (rs11544238) in ARHGAP9 exhibited a strong association with a favorable prognosis in advanced high-risk RB,17 a finding corroborated by clinical replication in this study (Fig. 1A; Table). Bioinformatic analyses also revealed that ARHGAP9 was correlated with prognosis across pan-cancer cohorts and with drug sensitivity in public databases (Figs. 1B–F). To investigate the role of ARHGAP9 in RB, stable cell lines with ARHGAP9 knockdown or overexpression were established. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that ARHGAP9 knockdown reduced chemotherapeutic sensitivity in RB, whereas ARHGAP9 overexpression enhanced responsiveness (Figs. 2, 3).

ARHGAP9 plays dual roles in cancers, functioning as a tumor suppressor in certain tumors22–25 and as an oncogene in other tumors.6,26,27 In studies examining its drug-related functions, only two agents, vitexin and ginsenoside Rg3, have been reported to induce dysregulation of ARHGAP9 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma.28 ARHGAP9 possesses GAP activity toward Rac1.29 Reduced Rac1 expression has been implicated in RB progression in both invasive and noninvasive tumor specimens.30 Activated Rac1 promotes dissociation of the Rac1/β-catenin complex from the membrane to the nucleus, thereby increasing β-catenin nuclear accumulation. Additionally, the Rac1/β-catenin signaling axis drives tumor invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance.31–33 Consequently, it was hypothesized that ARHGAP9 may act as a tumor suppressor gene through its GAP activity toward Rac1 in RB, thereby increasing chemotherapy sensitivity. Consistent with this hypothesis, ARHGAP9 knockdown promoted RB chemoresistance by enhancing Rac1 activity, facilitating the Rac1–β-catenin interaction, driving the translocation of the Rac1/β-catenin complex from the membrane to the nucleus, elevating β-catenin nuclear accumulation, and promoting LEF1–β-catenin complex formation, which ultimately activated the downstream β-catenin signaling pathway (Fig. 4). Conversely, ARHGAP9 overexpression produced the opposite effects. These findings suggest that ARHGAP9 functions as a tumor suppressor in RB. However, it is noteworthy that ARHGAP9 appears to exert oncogenic effects in other cancers. In gastric cancer, ARHGAP9 knockdown inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, accompanied by downregulation of p-AKT and p-p38, suggesting a potential oncogenic role.34 In acute myeloid leukemia, elevated ARHGAP9 expression predicts poor overall survival, indicating a pro-tumorigenic role.26 In breast cancer, ARHGAP9 is upregulated in tumor tissues and promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion.35 Collectively, these discrepancies highlight the importance of investigating ARHGAP9 in a cancer-type-specific manner and suggest that its function may be influenced by the molecular landscape of each tumor.

NSC23766 is a specific inhibitor of Rac1, MSAB is an inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and SKL2001 is an activator of this pathway. Previous studies demonstrated that ARHGAP9 knockdown promoted chemoresistance by enhancing Rac1 activity, thereby activating downstream β-catenin signaling. To further investigate this mechanism, RB cells were treated with chemotherapeutic agents in combination with NSC23766, MSAB, or SKL2001. Notably, co-treatment with these agents reversed ARHGAP9-mediated alterations in both chemotherapeutic sensitivity and the activity of the β-catenin downstream pathway (Fig. 5).

This study has some limitations. First, validation in RB tumor tissues (e.g., ARHGAP9 expression or β-catenin nuclear localization in enucleated specimens) was not performed in this study, as postchemotherapy tumor samples were often too small and poorly formed to allow reliable molecular analysis. Furthermore, while we performed IHC for Ki-67 and β-catenin in our xenograft samples, we did not directly perform IHC for ARHGAP9 in these tissues because all paraffin blocks were exhausted after the planned histopathologic analyses. ARHGAP9 knockdown and overexpression in the xenografts were inferred from the well-validated stable cell lines (Figs. 2A, 2B) and consistent functional outcomes, but direct protein visualization in tumor tissue remains absent. This limits the translational relevance of our findings, and future studies with appropriate tumor specimens are warranted to further corroborate the conclusions. Second, the mechanism by which the germline variant (rs11544238) in ARHGAP9 influenced chemotherapeutic sensitivity and Rac1 activation, together with downstream β-catenin/LEF1 signaling, remains unclear and requires further investigation. Although our patent demonstrated no effect of rs11544238 on ARHGAP9 protein expression,36 its direct impact on protein stability, GAP activity, or Rac1-GTP regulation has not yet been experimentally determined. Importantly, a causal relationship has not yet been established; our findings regarding rs11544238 are associative and do not imply direct causation. Further studies are required to elucidate the functional mechanism of this SNP. Third, additional ARHGAP9-mediated cell signaling pathways may have been involved but were not examined. For instance, other regulators of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, such as GSK-3, AXIN, CK1, and DVL1, may participate through crosstalk with ARHGAP9.37 Fourth, previous research has demonstrated that ARHGAP9 knockdown promotes lung cancer metastasis by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway via DKK2 suppression38; however, in this study, ARHGAP9 knockdown or overexpression did not affect DKK2 protein levels (Fig. 4B). Fifth, while the Y79 line offers clear advantages given its well-characterized features and relevance to advanced retinoblastoma, it is important to acknowledge that its use as the sole cell model for functional validation was a compromise shaped by time constraints and experimental efficiency.39,40 Therefore, the findings reported in this study should be interpreted with caution, as they may not directly generalize to other retinoblastoma cell types, patient-derived tumor cells, or clinical settings. The exclusive reliance on the Y79/Y79-EDR system represents a major limitation of the present work. Moving forward, incorporating additional or next-generation cellular systems will be essential to test the specificity and translational scope of our conclusions. Additionally, although we have provided multiple lines of evidence supporting ARHGAP9-mediated regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, including β-catenin nuclear accumulation, enhanced Rac1–β-catenin interaction, increased LEF1 binding, and upregulation of canonical target genes (c-Myc, cyclin D1, Bcl-2), a direct functional readout of Wnt activity, such as a luciferase reporter assay, was not performed in this study. This represents a limitation that should be addressed in future investigations to further consolidate the mechanistic conclusions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that ARHGAP9 knockdown promoted chemoresistance in RB by enhancing Rac1 activity, facilitating the interaction between Rac1 and β-catenin, and promoting the translocation of the Rac1/β-catenin complex from the membrane to the nucleus. This nuclear translocation increased LEF1 and β-catenin binding, activating downstream target genes, including c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2, as illustrated in Figure 6. These findings support the hypothesis that ARHGAP9 modulates RB susceptibility through Rac1/β-catenin/LEF1 signaling and elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying its regulation of drug responses in RB (Fig. 6). Moreover, the results highlight the potential of ARHGAP9 as a candidate for further evaluation in the context of prognosis and targeted therapy for advanced high-risk RB, although this requires validation in RB tumor tissues.

ARHGAP9 knockdown enhanced RB chemoresistance by increasing Rac1 activity, promoting Rac1/β-catenin binding (1) and nuclear translocation (2), which subsequently facilitated LEF1 and β-catenin binding (3) and activated downstream target genes, including c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Bcl-2.
