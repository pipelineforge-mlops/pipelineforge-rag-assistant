Background

Metastatic burden is a key determinant of prognosis in advanced solid tumors, yet current RECIST 1.1-based assessment does not fully capture total or site-specific disease extent. Emerging evidence suggests that quantitative imaging-derived metrics may improve prognostic stratification beyond binary metastatic classification.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed 52 patients with advanced non-oncogene-addicted lung cancer. Baseline clinical variables and imaging-derived metastatic lesions were assessed across multiple organ sites (lung, lymph nodes, bone, brain, liver and adrenal glands). Lesion area was calculated using an elliptical approximation and summed per organ and globally. Overall survival was evaluated using multivariable Cox regression models with restricted cubic splines to account for non-linear effects. Analyses included: binary metastatic status, continuous site-specific tumor burden, and relative contribution of each site to total metastatic load.

Results

Median age was 67 years, and bone (53%) and brain (27%) were frequent metastatic sites. Across all analytical levels, bone metastases consistently showed the strongest prognostic impact. Bone involvement was associated with worse overall survival both as a binary variable (HR 2.93, p = 0.020) and as continuous tumor burden (HR 2.82, p = 0.014). A higher relative contribution of bone disease to total tumor burden was also significantly associated with poorer survival (HR 3.01, p = 0.016). Other metastatic sites did not demonstrate consistent statistically significant associations with survival in this exploratory cohort, although these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of prognostic relevance. Histological subtype and age remained independent prognostic factors across models.

Conclusion

Quantitative assessment of metastatic burden identified skeletal disease as a consistent prognostic signal in this exploratory cohort, supporting further investigation of imaging-derived measures of metastatic burden and spatial disease distribution.

Highlights

• Quantitative imaging assessment of metastatic burden provides prognostic stratification beyond RECIST 1.1 in advanced NSCLC.

• Skeletal metastases represent the most powerful site-specific prognostic determinant across binary, continuous, and relative burden models.

• Bone metastatic burden is independently associated with reduced overall survival, demonstrating a dose-dependent effect.

• Other metastatic sites show limited or inconsistent prognostic impact when modeled as continuous imaging-derived variables.

• Spatial distribution of metastatic disease, particularly bone-dominant phenotypes, carries relevant prognostic information.

1 Introduction

The prognostic assessment of metastatic burden in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a critical challenge. In clinical practice, response assessment and disease characterization are still largely based on RECIST 1.1 criteria, which primarily rely on changes in linear dimensions of target lesions and dichotomous classification of metastatic involvement [1]. However, this framework has well-known limitations, as it relies on simple linear measurements and binary response categories that do not fully capture the complexity of tumor evolution.

The clinical landscape is significantly complicated by the assessment of metastatic sites that carry major prognostic weight, such as the brain, bone, and lung. Pulmonary metastatic burden, in particular, exhibits high intra-organ heterogeneity, where patterns such as diffuse bilateral (miliary) involvement, endobronchial extension, or cavitation serve as distinct markers of aggressive biology and systemic treatment resistance [2], [3], [4]. Similarly, bone and brain metastases are frequently underestimated by RECIST, despite their profound impact on morbidity and survival [5], [6].

This challenge is particularly important in the era of immuno-oncology (IO), where traditional RECIST metrics often fail to capture the full spectrum of patient response [7], [8]. Growing evidence suggests that total tumor burden (TTB) acts as a more robust, independent predictor of outcomes in IO-treated patients [9].

Based on these considerations, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the prognostic impact of metastatic burden quantified as lesion area across multiple organ sites in a cohort of patients with metastatic NSCLC. In particular, we investigated how site-specific tumor burden correlates with overall survival and whether quantitative imaging features provide incremental prognostic value beyond standard clinical variables. We also specifically addressed metastatic sites often underrepresented in classical RECIST evaluations, such as brain and bone, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of total disease burden and its prognostic implications [8], [10], [11].

2 Methods

We retrospectively analyzed a cohort of 52 treatment-naïve patients with advanced non-oncogene addicted lung cancer who received first-line chemotherapy plus immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) regimens at the Medical Oncology Department of the Policlinico Universitario Campus Bio-Medico (Rome, Italy) between February 2020 and April 2025. Patients were managed according to routine clinical practice and followed at the discretion of the treating physicians. Data were retrospectively collected, with a cutoff date of May 2025. Baseline clinical data included age, sex, ECOG performance status (PS), PD-L1 expression, smoking status, histology, and subsequent systemic therapy. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from metastatic diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the last available follow-up were censored at the date of last follow-up.

All available baseline contrast-enhanced CT scans of the chest, brain, abdomen, and neck acquired before treatment initiation were independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists, who were blinded to survival outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Metastatic lesions were identified across predefined anatomical sites, including the adrenal glands, lungs, lymph nodes, brain, bone, and liver. For each lesion, the maximum long-axis diameter and its perpendicular short-axis diameter were measured on axial CT images. Lesion area was estimated assuming an elliptical shape using the formula π × (long axis × short axis) / 4. Organ-specific metastatic burden was calculated as the sum of lesion areas within each anatomical site (mm2), whereas total metastatic burden was defined as the sum of lesion areas across all sites.A Associations between metastatic burden and OS were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. For each site, burden was modeled as a continuous variable, including patients with and without metastatic involvement of the specific anatomical site being analyzed. To account for potential non-linear effects of metastatic burden on survival, restricted cubic spline functions were applied to continuous lesion area variables within Cox models. For sites with a wide range of values, we used a log1p transformation to stabilize the data.

All models were adjusted for age, ECOG-PS, histological subtype, and PD-L1 expression, which were selected a priori based on their established clinical relevance as key prognostic covariates. This predefined selection was adopted to avoid overadjustment and multivariable model overfitting. The study strategy is summarized in Fig. 1. In the first level of multivariable analysis, we evaluated the association between baseline clinical characteristics, metastatic site involvement (as binary variables), and overall survival. No statistically significant associations were observed for the other metastatic sites in this model. Histological squamous subtype was also independently associated with survival (HR 2.42, 95% CI 1.34–4.37, p = 0.003), confirming its prognostic relevance within this cohort. No significant associations were observed between overall survival and metastatic burden in lung, lymph node, adrenal, or brain sites when modeled as continuous variables. 4 Discussion

In this study, we investigated the prognostic role of metastatic burden across different organ sites in a cohort of 52 treatment-naïve patients with advanced non-oncogene-addicted NSCLC using a hierarchical analytical framework integrating binary metastatic status, continuous lesion area, and relative burden distribution. Across all analytical levels, a consistent and biologically coherent signal emerged, with skeletal metastatic involvement showing the most robust and reproducible association with overall survival. Bone metastases were independently associated with worse prognosis both when modeled as a binary variable and, more importantly, when assessed as continuous tumor burden and as relative contribution to total metastatic load, suggesting a dose-dependent and context-dependent prognostic effect. These findings are in line with previous evidence highlighting the adverse prognostic role of bone involvement in advanced lung cancer, but extend current knowledge by suggesting that not only the presence but also the quantitative dominance of skeletal disease within the metastatic landscape may carry additional prognostic information [10], [12], [13].In contrast, other metastatic sites, including lung, lymph nodes, adrenal glands, and brain, did not show consistent associations with overall survival across modeling strategies, either as binary variables or when assessed using continuous burden metrics. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that these metastatic sites, or established clinical prognostic factors such as ECOG performance status and PD-L1 expression, lack prognostic relevance. The relatively small sample size, limited number of events, and heterogeneity in metastatic patterns may have reduced the statistical power to detect associations of clinically meaningful magnitude. In particular, the absence of a significant association between brain metastatic burden and overall survival should be interpreted with caution. Only 27% of patients in our cohort had brain metastases, which may have limited the statistical power to detect an association within this subgroup. Moreover, brain metastatic disease is clinically and biologically heterogeneous, with relevant differences in number, size, symptomatic status, local treatment, that were not comprehensively captured by our quantitative burden assessment. Therefore, the finding that skeletal burden showed a stronger statistical association with survival than other sites should not be interpreted as evidence that skeletal disease is intrinsically more clinically or biologically important than others. Rather, it should be considered an exploratory observation requiring validation in larger and independently characterized cohorts.

The ratio-based analysis further suggested that the relative distribution of metastatic burden across organs may provide complementary prognostic information beyond absolute tumor load, with bone-dominant disease phenotypes appearing to be associated with worse outcomes, supporting the concept that spatial patterns of metastatic dissemination may be clinically relevant. However, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing a definitive hierarchy of prognostic importance among metastatic sites.

From a methodological point of view, our results support the application of flexible survival models incorporating continuous imaging-derived metrics and non-linear effects, in line with recent advances in quantitative imaging biomarker research. Moreover, using advanced statistical models—such as spline-based Cox regression—allows us to better analyze tumor burden. These methods are more effective than traditional approaches because they can capture the complex, non-linear relationships between the tumor size and a patient's survival, allowing a more refined characterization of the relationship between tumor burden and survival compared with conventional dichotomous approaches [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. Importantly, our framework addresses baseline prognostic assessment rather than treatment response. RECIST 1.1 is primarily designed for treatment response assessment and remains the predominant framework used in clinical trials; however, bone metastases are generally considered non-measurable lesions unless an associated measurable soft-tissue component is present [1]. The MD Anderson (MDA) criteria provide a validated approach for assessing treatment response in bone metastases, although they are not routinely used as the primary response criteria in most clinical trials [20].

Furthermore, skeletal metastases represent a unique, clinically aggressive biological niche. Growing evidence suggests that the bone marrow microenvironment (TME) in patients with skeletal metastases is characterized by a profoundly immunosuppressive landscape. Bone-derived factors, such as TGF-β and osteoclast-activating cytokines, create a sanctuary site that not only promotes tumor progression but actively reduces T-cell infiltration and effector function [21]. Our findings suggest that quantitative assessment of skeletal burden may provide additional prognostic information beyond the simple presence of bone metastases; however, the present study cannot determine whether skeletal burden directly contributes to therapeutic resistance or poorer outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that incorporating quantitative assessment of bone tumor burden into staging protocols could enhance prognostic stratification. Moreover, the prognostic relevance of skeletal involvement further underscores the importance of appropriate management of bone metastases, including the integration of antiresorptive agents according to established clinical indications [22], [23]. While some studies have explored the potential synergistic role of these agents in NSCLC patients undergoing immunotherapy, hypothesizing that by mitigating the skeletal “vicious cycle,” they might modulate the bone microenvironment, clinical evidence remains exploratory, and these agents are currently primarily utilized for skeletal-related event (SRE) prevention rather than as primary anti-tumor therapies [22], [23].

Furthermore, the development of automated or semi-automated tools for longitudinal tracking of organ-specific burden could facilitate real-time monitoring of disease evolution.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged, including the retrospective single-center design, the relatively small sample size, and the exploratory nature of the analyses, which collectively limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, despite multivariable adjustment for key clinical covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Moreover, the use of two-dimensional lesion area as a surrogate for tumor burden, while practical and reproducible, does not fully capture the true volumetric disease burden. A significant concern in our modeling strategy was the potential for overfitting, given the limited sample size relative to the complexity of metastatic patterns. To mitigate this risk, we adopted a highly conservative approach: we restricted our multivariable models to a pre-defined key clinical covariate known to influence survival. The limited sample size and number of events may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect established prognostic associations, particularly for less frequent metastatic sites and clinical covariates. Finally, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require external validation in larger, independent cohorts before any clinical translation can be considered.

In conclusion, in treatment-naïve patients with advanced non-oncogene addicted lung cancer, skeletal metastatic burden emerged as the strongest prognostic signal within this exploratory cohort. However, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of a causal relationship or a definitive hierarchy of prognostic importance among metastatic sites.

Three complementary levels of analysis were performed: (i) assessment of the association between clinical variables and OS, as well as the presence or absence of metastases across individual organ sites; (ii) evaluation of the relationship between site-specific total lesion area and survival outcomes; (iii) calculation of the ratio between site-specific tumor burden and total metastatic burden (site area divided by total metastatic area).
