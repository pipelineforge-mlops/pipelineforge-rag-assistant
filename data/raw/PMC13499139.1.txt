==============================
Cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized the landscape of cancer treatment, particularly through the development of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. However, the therapeutic efficacy of these interventions is frequently hindered by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which is characterized by hypoxia, poor immune cell infiltration, and impaired antigen presentation. To overcome these barriers, MP-GCZ has been developed as a self-oxygenating biomimetic nanomotor to synergistically reprogram the TME and enhance antitumor immune responses through integrated multimodal mechanisms. This nanosystem combines photothermal therapy (PTT), cuproptosis-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), and localized immune checkpoint modulation to address the complex immunosuppressive network of the TME. By leveraging a metal-organic framework scaffold, MP-GCZ enables controlled delivery of therapeutic components that alleviate hypoxia, trigger immunogenic tumor cell death, and enhance adaptive immune responses. When activated by near-infrared irradiation, MP-GCZ enhances dendritic cell maturation, increases infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby transforming immunologically "cold" tumors into inflamed, immunogenic environments. Preclinical studies demonstrate that this strategy effectively suppresses both primary tumor growth and distant metastases, driven by systemic antitumor immunity. MP-GCZ represents a promising comprehensive approach to overcoming TME-mediated resistance and may offer a valuable solution to enhance the clinical efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.

1 Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has transformed the treatment paradigm for malignant tumors by leveraging the patient's endogenous immune system to target both primary and metastatic cancer cells [1,2]. Among the most impactful strategies, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), particularly monoclonal antibodies targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis, has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy across a broad spectrum of malignancies [3,4]. However, their efficacy is frequently curtailed by the highly immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME), which restricts immune activation and fosters resistance to treatment [5,6]. In this context, the recently identified form of regulated cell death known as cuproptosis presents a promising avenue to overcome these limitations [7]. Cuproptosis is triggered by copper-dependent disruption of mitochondrial metabolism through direct binding to lipoylated components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, leading to a robust immunogenic cell death (ICD) phenotype characterized by the coordinated release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and metabolic collapse [8,9]. This unique capacity of cuproptosis to concurrently induce ICD and potentiate antitumor immunity underscores its potential as a synergistic therapeutic strategy when combined with ICB, offering a potent approach to convert immunologically resistant tumors into responsive ones [10]. Despite this promise, the clinical translation of cuproptosis remains severely limited by the low intrinsic concentration of bioavailable copper ions within tumor tissues and the poor intratumoral delivery efficiency of conventional nanocarriers, which highlights the urgent need for a multifunctional platform capable of efficient copper delivery and robust immune activation [11].

Achieving optimal synergy between cuproptosis and immunotherapy requires advanced therapeutic strategies. Solid tumors establish complex physical and biochemical defenses, including a dense extracellular matrix (ECM) that impedes drug diffusion, disorganized vasculature causing heterogeneous perfusion, and an acidic pH TME that influences drug stability and activity [12]. Although cuproptosis can elicit considerable immunogenic potential through DAMPs release, it often fails to generate sustained systemic antitumor immunity without additional immunostimulatory cues [13,14]. In this context, photothermal therapy (PTT) emerges as a powerful synergistic partner, which utilizes localized heating to ablate tumor tissue under near infrared (NIR) irradiation, offering advantages of high precision and minimal invasiveness [15]. The localized thermal effects not only directly ablate tumor cells but also enhance membrane permeability and upregulate copper ion transporters, thereby facilitating intracellular copper accumulation and potentiating cuproptosis [16]. Furthermore, the combination of PTT and cuproptosis generates synergistic oxidative and thermal stress, amplifying immunogenic cell death and promoting antigen presentation [17]. Nevertheless, the hypoxic and redox-imbalanced nature of the TME often undermines these therapeutic effects by diminishing oxidative stress-mediated cytotoxicity and promoting immune evasion [18].

To overcome these challenges, the unique biochemical features of the TME, with elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and acidic pH, can be harnessed as endogenous triggers for smart drug delivery systems. Self-propelled nanomotors, which convert chemical energy into mechanical motion, offer a particularly promising solution by actively navigating through biological barriers [19,20]. Nanomotors activated by the acidic TME utilize pH-responsive reactions to generate propulsion, often accompanied by the production of oxygen (O2) or other gaseous molecules, thereby improving tumor penetration and alleviating hypoxia [21,22]. Therefore, ROS-responsive materials can further enable controlled drug release, while pH-sensitive components ensure tumor-specific activation. Beyond active penetration, biomimetic modifications such as cell membrane coating can confer homotypic targeting capabilities, enabling nanocarriers to evade reticuloendothelial system clearance and achieve higher tumor-specific accumulation.

Based on this foundational understanding, we herein developed an autonomous self-oxygenating biomimetic nanomotor, denoted as MP-GCZ, to achieve a cascade therapeutic effect by synergistically enhancing photothermal and immunotherapy via cuproptosis. The platform is constructed using a ZIF-8 metal–organic framework as a pH-responsive scaffold. Rather than being encapsulated within the intrinsic micropores of ZIF-8, the relatively large gold nanostars (GNS) are associated with the external surface of the composite to provide efficient photothermal conversion [23,24]. Additionally, copper peroxide nanodots (CuO2) are integrated into the MP-GCZ architecture as an endogenous stimulus-responsive oxygen generator. Its decomposition in the acidic TME produces O2 to alleviate hypoxia and propel the nanomotor, while simultaneously releasing copper ions for therapeutic purposes [25]. The MP-GCZ redefines precision cancer therapy by leveraging the unique biochemical and physical properties of the TME to drive interconnected biological responses that overcome biological barriers and reshape antitumor immunity. First, MP-GCZ exploits the intrinsic capacity of cuproptosis to amplify DAMP release and induce ICD, thereby establishing a robust synergistic effect between photothermal therapy and immunotherapy through a cuproptosis-driven multimodal cascade. The GNS-mediated PTT further enhances tumor cell membrane permeability and upregulates copper transporters, which exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and collectively sensitizes cells to copper-dependent cytotoxicity. This photothermal-cuproptosis interplay not only induces direct tumor ablation but also remodels the TME toward an immunosuppression-attenuated state, where increased infiltration of dendritic cells and activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes amplify the efficacy of ICB [26]. In vivo studies confirm that this integrated therapeutic approach not only inhibits primary tumor growth but also elicits long-term immune memory, converting the systemic immune landscape into a tumor-reactive state that suppresses distant metastases. Second, copper peroxide nanodots encapsulated within the MP-GCZ undergo catalytic decomposition upon encountering elevated H+ in TME, generating O2 microbubbles that enable autonomous movement through the collagen-rich ECM while concurrently mitigating the hypoxic conditions characteristic of solid tumors. This integrated oxygenation mechanism addresses a critical limitation in cancer treatment, where inadequate oxygenation compromises oxidative stress-mediated therapies and contributes to the development of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Third, MP-GCZ dynamically remodels the TME by converting pathological stimuli into therapeutic actions, thereby creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop. The acidic TME triggers ZIF-8 degradation and subsequent release of Zn2+ ions, which activate the cGAS-STING pathway to further enhance antigen cross-presentation. Concurrently, H+-driven oxygenation supports nanomotor propulsion and mitigates hypoxia, while photothermal-cuproptosis-induced ICD recruits immune cells to disrupt stromal barriers, a process that improves both nanomotor penetration and immunomodulation effects. This bioorthogonal therapeutic cascade represents a significant advancement in nanomedicine design, enabling the therapeutic system to dynamically adapt to and actively reprogram its biological environment rather than simply delivering passive therapeutic payloads.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Materials

4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), Poly (vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP, Mw 10000),2-Methylimidazole (2-MI) were purchased from Aladdin, gold (III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), copper(II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O), were purchased from Macklin, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) Purchased from Sinopharm Group, Anti-PD-L1/CD274 Polyclonal Antibody were purchased from Solarbio. Deionized (DI) water was produced by a ULPHW-IIIUPR-I device (Sichuan Youpu Ultra Pure Technology Co., Ltd) and used for all experiments.Critical detection kits including reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit, Hoechst 33342, LysoTracker Green, and Calcein AM/PI cell viability kit came from Beyotime Biotechnology in China, with JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit, and hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining kit acquired from Solarbio in China. Ammonium tetrathiomolybdate (TTM) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Essential cell culture components such as Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin-streptomycin came from Gibco in the United States. Finally, female C57BL/6 mice aged 4–6 weeks were provided by Center For Disease Control of Hubei province for in vivo studies. All reagents were of appropriate analytical grade.

2.2 Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were performed on Empyreanat Fourier, Transform Infrared Spectrometer(FT-IR) were performed on Nicolet 6700, UV-Vis Spectrophotometer were performed on Shimadzu UV2600, Automatic BET specific surface (product) analysis tester were used on micromeritics ASAP 2460, Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were performed on JEM-F200, Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and Zeta potential results were obtained using Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy(XPS) were measured on Thermo Scientific K-Alpha. The colloidal stability of the final MP-GCZ formulation was evaluated by dynamic light scattering. MP-GCZ was dispersed in PBS or PBS containing 10% FBS at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL and incubated at 37 °C. The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90. Three independently prepared batches were analyzed, and each sample was measured in triplicate.

2.3 Synthesis of nanomaterials

CuO2 nanodots were prepared by dissolving 0.2 M CuCl2·2H2O in 5 mL deionized water, adding 0.1 g PVP, followed by 5 mL of 0.02 M NaOH to form a blue precipitate. Then, 100 μL of 30% H2O2 was added and stirred for 30 min until the solution turned dark brown. The product was washed, centrifuged at 11,000 rpm, and dried. AuNPs were synthesized via a seed-mediated method: 100 μL of HAuCl4 (25 mM) was added to 100 mL water at 100 °C, followed by 1% sodium citrate. After boiling for 30 min, the solution was cooled to 90 °C, supplemented with another 100 μL HAuCl4, stirred for 30 min, and stored at 4 °C. GNS were prepared using a seedless approach: 100 μL HEPES solution (0.2383 g in 1 mL water, diluted 1:98) was mixed with 0.1 mL HAuCl4 (25 mM), stirred 30 s, rested 2 h, then supplemented with another 0.1 mL HAuCl4, stirred 30 min, rested 2 h, and collected by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 10 min. ZIF-8 NPs were synthesized by dissolving 6 mM Zn(NO3)2·6H2O and 48 mM 2-methylimidazole separately in 50 mL methanol, combining under stirring at 35 °C for 12 h, followed by centrifugation, methanol washing, and drying at 60 °C. For CZ, 20 mg CuO2 nanodots were dispersed in 25 mL methanol, then mixed with methanolic solutions of Zn(NO3)2 and 2-methylimidazole, reacted 12 h, centrifuged, washed, and dried. GCZ was obtained by dispersing 100 mg CZ in 45 mL methanol, adding 5 mL GNS, stirring 12 h, centrifuging, and drying. For antibody conjugation, 100 mg GCZ was dispersed in 10 mL methanol, mixed with 10 μL anti-PD-L1, stirred 24 h in the dark, centrifuged, washed, and dried.

2.4 Isolation of Hepa1-6 tumor cell membrane

Hepa1-6 tumor cell membrane was extracted by membrane protein isolation kit. Briefly, Hepa1-6 cells were collected and suspended in membrane protein extraction buffer solution. Ultrasound probe sonication was used to destroy the integrity of tumor cells and centrifugation was used to collect the cell membrane. The concentration of the obtained cell membrane was measured by BCA protein assay kits.

2.5 Photothermal performance of GCZ

Aqueous dispersions of GCZ at various concentrations (0, 10, 20, and 50 μg/mL) were prepared in 1 mL volumes using quartz cuvettes with negligible ultraviolet absorption. The samples were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2. The temperature was monitored in real-time using an infrared thermal camera (FOTRIC 220s) until it reached a steady state, after which the laser was turned off to record the cooling phase until the temperature returned to baseline. The temperature changes throughout both heating and cooling processes were recorded for analysis. The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) was calculated according to Equation (1):(1) η=hS(Tmax−Tsurr)−QDisI(I−10−Aλ)

where h is the heat transfer coefficient, S is the surface area of the container, Tmax is the equilibrium temperature at steady state, Tsᵤᵣᵣ is the ambient temperature, QDis represents heat dissipation from the solvent, I is the laser power, and A808 is the absorbance of GCZ at 808 nm.

2.6 Cell culture

Murine hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Hepa1-6 wt, catalog: CL-0371) and human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2, catalog: CL-1031) were obtained from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. These cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 using Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 (RPMI-1640) culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin G sodium, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin sulfate. All cell lines were confirmed mycoplasma-free prior to experimental use.

2.7 Analysis of nanomotor motility in the cellular environment

Cancer cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per milliliter in a culture dish and incubated overnight. Subsequently, Cy5-labeled ZIF-8 (200 μg/mL) and Cy5-labeled MP-GCZ nanomotors (200 μg/mL) were introduced into the system. Their movement trajectories were tracked using a custom inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a 100× objective. Image sequences were manually analyzed via the ImageJ software (version 2.0.0) plugin to determine particle velocities by calculating the mean displacement over defined time intervals. For each formulation, ten individual nanoparticles were tracked to compute the average velocity and mean squared displacement (MSD). The diffusion behavior of particles was characterized by curve fitting of MSD plots, allowing classification of their motion modes.

2.8 Cellular localization of nanomotors in Hepa1-6 cells

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per milliliter in a culture dish and incubated for 4 h. The cells were then treated with Cy5-labeled ZIF-8 and MP-GCZ formulations (200 μg/mL) for 5 min. A control group was subjected to near-infrared (NIR) irradiation to evaluate the colocalization of lysosomes and nanomaterials. Lysosomes were pre-stained with LysoGreen (1 μM in DMSO, Kaijin Biotechnology Co.), and cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (10 μg/mL). After 2 h of incubation, intracellular distribution was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope with a 63× oil-immersion objective.

2.9 Penetration kinetics of nanomotors in 3D tumor spheroids

To evaluate penetration efficiency in tumor spheroids, Hepa1-6 cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well in a 96-well plate pre-coated with 1.5% agarose to promote cell aggregation. After three days of culture, mature spheroids formed at the bottom of the wells and were subsequently transferred to confocal imaging dishes. The spheroids were treated with Cy5-labeled ZIF-8 (control group, CON), Cy5-labeled MP-GCZ, and near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. Following 30 min of incubation, fluorescence distribution patterns were analyzed using z-stack imaging with a 20 μm interval via confocal laser scanning microscopy to quantify depth-dependent penetration.

2.10 Mitochondrial morphology analysis by transmission electron microscopy

Hepa1-6 cells were co-cultured with MP-GCZ-containing medium under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. Control groups were treated with PBS alone. After incubation, cells were collected and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 12 h. The fixed samples underwent sequential processing as follows: staining in 0.1% tannic acid in oxalate buffer, post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide, and further staining with 1% filtered-sterile uranyl acetate. After gradient dehydration and resin embedding, the polymerized blocks were cured at 60 °C for 24 h. Ultrathin sections were finally imaged using transmission electron microscopy.

2.11 Detection of cuproptosis-associated protein expression in cells

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. They were then treated for 6 h with the following: control, CZ, GCZ, MP-GCZ, and MP-GCZ with NIR irradiation. After treatment, the cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed using pre-chilled RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Beyotime Biotechnology). The samples were boiled in loading buffer, and equal amounts of protein were separated by 6–15% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with QuickBlock™ blocking buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against POLD1, ACO2, LIAS, FDX1, and HSP70. The membranes were then washed three times with Western Wash Buffer (10×, Beyotime Biotechnology), followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime Biotechnology, 1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were finally visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate kit (Beyotime Biotechnology).

2.12 Detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. After treatment with the respective experimental groups, the medium was aspirated and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Fresh medium containing 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was then added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. Green fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope to assess ROS levels.

2.13 Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential

To evaluate mitochondrial integrity, Hepa1-6 cells were treated with various experimental groups for 6 h (in 2.0 mL of culture medium per well). Following the treatment, the cells were stained with JC-1 staining solution according to the manufacturer's protocol. Fluorescence images were captured separately using the green channel (indicative of J-monomer formation) and the red channel (representing J-aggregates) under a fluorescence microscope.

2.14 Viability staining of live and dead cells

Hepa1-6 cells were incubated with different treatment groups in 2.0 mL of culture medium for 24 h. The cells were then stained with Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) according to standard protocols. Fluorescence signals were detected using an inverted fluorescence microscope system.

2.15 Copper-chelation rescue assay

To evaluate the copper dependence of MP-GCZ-induced cytotoxicity, Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were pretreated with TTM (10 μM) for 2 h, followed by MP-GCZ treatment (100 μg/mL). After 4 h, the indicated groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 1.0 W/cm2 for 3 min and cultured for an additional 20 h. The groups included Control, TTM, MP-GCZ, TTM + MP-GCZ, MP-GCZ + NIR, and TTM + MP-GCZ + NIR. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay. After treatment, the medium was replaced with 90 μL fresh medium containing 10 μL CCK-8 reagent. Following incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured. Cell viability was normalized to the untreated control group. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

2.16 Measurement of extracellular ATP levels

To determine changes in extracellular ATP levels, Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2.0 × 105 cells per well in 2.0 mL of culture medium and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 500 μL of fresh medium containing control or different treatment groups, followed by another 6 h of incubation. After centrifugation to collect the supernatant, the extracellular ATP content was measured using an ATP assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat: S0026).

2.17 Detection of CRT exposure and HMGB1 translocation

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then treated with different experimental groups for 6 h (with 2.0 mL medium per well). Immunofluorescence staining was performed as follows: after washing with PBS, the cells were fixed with fixative solution for 10 min, followed by blocking for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-CRT and anti-HMGB1 primary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated secondary antibodies were then applied, and fluorescence signals were visualized using a fluorescence microscope. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342.

2.18 RNA-seq data processing and differential expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from HepG2 cells treated with PBS (CON group) or MP-GCZ + NIR (EXP group) for transcriptome sequencing. Three independent biological replicates were included in each group (n = 3 per group; six samples in total). Each biological replicate was obtained from an independently cultured and treated cell sample, followed by separate RNA extraction and library preparation. Following quality control (FastQC), read preprocessing, alignment, and RPKM normalization, differentially expressed genes were identified using thresholds of |log2FC|>1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. Functional Annotation and Pathway Enrichment Analysis: GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.10.0), with KEGG analysis specifically implemented via the enrichKEGG function. GSEA was conducted to characterize key pathways at the whole-genome level. Processed data were visualized through the Bioinformation Cloud Platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) to identify related pathway genes, and a PPI network was subsequently constructed and visualized.

2.19 Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 4-6 weeks were procured from Center For Disease Control of Hubei province and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. All animal procedures were conducted in strict compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. ZZU-LAC20260327 [23]).

2.20 In vivo antitumor immunity experiment

A mouse model was established by subcutaneously injecting Hepa1-6 cells (5 × 106 cells) into the right flank of C57 mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5 per group): control, CZ, GCZ, MP-GCZ, and MP-GCZ with NIR irradiation (1 W/cm2). Each group received intravenous injections of the corresponding agents at a dose of 5 mg/kg.

2.21 Dendritic cell maturation analysis

Lymph nodes were harvested and homogenized in PBS, followed by filtration to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-CD11c, PE-conjugated anti-CD86, and APC-conjugated anti-CD80 antibodies, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

2.22 T cell activation analysis

Spleens were homogenized in PBS to prepare single-cell suspensions. After lysing red blood cells with erythrocyte lysis buffer, splenocytes and tumor cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-CD3, PE-conjugated anti-CD8a, and APC-conjugated anti-CD4 antibodies, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

2.23 Regulatory T cell (Treg) analysis

Spleen tissues were homogenized in True-Nuclear™ Transcription Factor Buffer to prepare single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD4, APC-conjugated anti-CD25, and PE-conjugated anti-FoxP3 antibodies, and analyzed using flow cytometry.

2.24 In vivo and ex vivo organ imaging

C57BL/6 mice (n = 3 per group) were intravenously injected with Cy7-labeled CZ and MP-GCZ formulations (200 μL), with Cy5-labeled ZIF-8 used as a control. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed using a small animal imaging system under continuous isoflurane anesthesia at predetermined time points (excitation/emission wavelengths: 720/830 nm). At 24 h post-injection, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues along with major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested for subsequent ex vivo fluorescence imaging analysis.

2.25 Evaluation of in vivo antitumor efficacy

Tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice inoculated with Hepa1-6 cells were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5 per group). The mice received intravenous injections of the respective materials on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8. The injection volume and concentration were consistent with those described in the in vivo experimental section. All materials were dissolved in 0.9% NaCl solution before administration. During the treatment period, tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured using calipers every two days, and tumor volume (V) was calculated according to Equation (2):(2) V=L×W22

On day 14, the mice were euthanized, and major organs were harvested for further analysis. Tumor tissues from each group were sectioned and subjected to H&E staining, TUNEL assay, and immunohistochemical staining for HP2, HIF-1α, Ki67, and CRT/CD8. The stained sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope. Similarly, sections of major organs were processed with H&E staining and examined microscopically.

2.26 Abscopal effect evaluation

To investigate whether MP-GCZ can elicit potent antitumor immunity, a bilateral tumor model was established. On days −7 and −3, Hepa1-6 tumor cells were subcutaneously injected into the left and right flanks of the mice, respectively. The mice were then randomly assigned to five groups: control, CZ, GCZ, MP-GCZ, and MP-GCZ + NIR (n = 5). On days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8, the primary (left) tumors were treated with nanoparticles via intratumoral injection, while the distant (right) tumors remained untreated. Tumor volumes of both the primary and abscopal tumors were monitored throughout the study. At the endpoint, distant tumors were collected for analysis of CD8+ T-cell infiltration and Ki67 expression.

2.27 Establishment and therapeutic evaluation of a pulmonary hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis model

A metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma model was established via tail vein injection of Hepa1-6 cell suspension (1 × 106 cells in 100 μL sterile PBS), followed by a 7-day incubation period. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five experimental groups (n = 5 per group): PBS control, CZ, GCZ, MP-GCZ, and MP-GCZ + NIR. All groups received intravenous tail vein injections at a dose of 5 mg/kg on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8. The MP-GCZ + NIR group additionally received tumor-targeted NIR irradiation (1 W/cm2, 3 min per session). On day 14, euthanasia was performed, and lung tissues were fixed by whole-body intracardiac perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde. After resection, lungs were macroscopically examined to count metastatic nodules, then subjected to paraffin embedding and sectioning at 5 μm thickness. Serial sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for comprehensive pathological evaluation to quantify metastatic burden and stromal remodeling.

2.28 Hemolysis assay

Whole blood collected from healthy female C57BL/6 mice using EDTA anticoagulation underwent centrifugation at 3000 × g for 8 min. Harvested erythrocytes were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended to prepare a red blood cell (RBC) solution containing 200 μL packed erythrocytes per 1 mL PBS. For hemolysis assessment, 20 μL RBC solution was mixed with 980 μL MP-GCZ solution to achieve final concentrations ranging from 20 to 800 μg/mL. Negative control samples combined 20 μL RBC solution with 980 μL PBS while positive controls used 980 μL deionized water. Following 4-h incubation at 37 °C, photographic documentation was performed prior to centrifugation at 3000 × g. Subsequently, 100 μL supernatant from each sample was transferred to 96-well plates where absorbance at 570 nm was measured spectrophotometrically. 3 Results and discussion

3.1 Working mechanism and characterization of MP-GCZ

The successful construction of MP-GCZ is achieved through a modular design strategy, enabling the stepwise integration of tumor-specific responsiveness, cuproptosis-enhanced multimodal therapy, and immune activation to ensure precise therapeutic performance. As illustrated in Scheme 1A, the assembly of MP-GCZ leverages the hexagonal architecture, high surface area, and pH-responsive degradability of ZIF-8 to construct a hierarchical composite scaffold. In this design, ZIF-8 serves as an acid-responsive structural component for ion release and provides abundant external/interfacial sites for association with therapeutic nanocomponents, rather than acting as a host whose intrinsic micropores accommodate large GNS.Scheme 1 Construction and working mechanism of MP-GCZ for cuproptosis-enhanced antitumor therapy. (B) Schematic illustration of the propulsion mechanism of MP-GCZ. The assembly process involves introducing PVP-stabilized CuO2 nanodots during ZIF-8 formation, yielding a CuO2/ZIF-8-associated composite (CZ) that serves as an acid-responsive reservoir for copper ion release. Given the nanoscale size of GNS relative to the intrinsic microporous structure of ZIF-8, GNS were not considered to be accommodated within the ZIF-8 micropores. Instead, GNS were associated with the CZ composite through surface/interfacial interactions, forming a photothermally responsive GCZ hybrid. The interfacial association between GNS and CZ is likely mediated by combined electrostatic, van der Waals, and possible coordination interactions between the HEPES-derived surface ligands of GNS and accessible Zn- or Cu-containing sites on CZ. As these interactions were not directly characterized, they are proposed as plausible contributors rather than a confirmed binding mechanism. Subsequently, anti-PD-L1 antibodies were introduced onto the GCZ surface through mild incubation to obtain the antibody-associated P-GCZ construct. The final MP-GCZ nanoplatform was prepared by biomimetic coating of P-GCZ with tumor cell membranes, which was designed to improve colloidal stability, prolong circulation, and enhance tumor-associated accumulation while maintaining the major therapeutic components of the platform.

After the intravenous injection, as shown in Scheme 1B, the CM coating guides the MP-GCZ to precisely accumulate in tumor tissues. Within the TME, elevated endogenous H+ levels trigger catalytic decomposition of CuO2, generating oxygen bubbles that promote active penetration through the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) while concurrently alleviating tumor hypoxia. Subsequently, NIR irradiation of the GNS component induces a potent photothermal effect that not only causes localized tumor ablation but also increases cell membrane permeability, facilitating the enhanced cellular uptake of copper ions and amplifying cuproptosis. This photothermal potentiation of cuproptosis results in coordinated ICD, characterized by the release of DAMPs and immunostimulatory zinc ions from the acid-degradable ZIF-8 framework, which collectively activate the cGAS-STING pathway to amplify antigen presentation. Furthermore, the site-specific release of anti-PD-L1 antibodies blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint axis, reinvigorating cytotoxic T lymphocytes and fostering robust and sustained systemic antitumor immunity. This multimodal therapy establishes a continuously amplifying therapeutic cascade that coordinates cuproptosis-mediated immunogenic cell death with oxygen-dependent hypoxia relief and dynamic microenvironment modulation, ultimately establishing durable systemic antitumor immunity (Scheme 1C).

3.2 Structural evolution and photothermal performance of MP-GCZ

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to investigate the structural evolution of the MP-GCZ composite. CuO2 quantum dots exhibited an average size of approximately 5 nm (Fig. 2A1), whereas GNS displayed a star-shaped morphology with an average size of approximately 50 nm (Fig. 2A2). Pristine ZIF-8 nanoparticles showed a uniform hexagonal morphology with an average diameter of ∼85 nm (Fig. 2A3). After CuO2 introduction, small Cu-containing nanodomains were observed in association with ZIF-8 particles (Fig. 2A4), and elemental mapping confirmed the coexistence of Zn and Cu in the CZ composite (Fig. S1). After GNS addition, star-shaped GNS were observed in proximity to the CZ particles, indicating the formation of a hierarchical GCZ composite through surface/interfacial association rather than accommodation within the intrinsic micropores of ZIF-8 (Fig. 2A5). Elemental mapping and EDS analysis further verified the coexistence of Zn, Cu, and Au in the GCZ composite (Fig. S2). Following tumor cell membrane coating, the hydrodynamic diameter of the final MP-GCZ formulation increased to 328.7 ± 2.5 nm in PBS, with a PDI of 0.202 ± 0.005, compared with approximately 290 nm for the uncoated GCZ composite (Fig. S3A). This increase was attributed primarily to the formation of a membrane-derived outer layer and the associated hydration shell. During incubation in PBS at 37 °C for 72 h, the hydrodynamic diameter and PDI showed only slight increases to 337.3 ± 2.5 nm and 0.226 ± 0.005, respectively. The DLS-derived size represents the apparent hydrodynamic diameter of the membrane-coated composite in aqueous dispersion and is therefore larger than the primary particle size observed by TEM. Surface-associated GNS may increase particle size and aggregation tendency, whereas the tumor cell membrane coating improves colloidal stability through steric protection. In addition, EDX analysis detected phosphorus signals localized in the outer region, further supporting the presence of membrane-derived phospholipid components on MP-GCZ (Fig. 2B). 3.3 Motion analysis and tumor penetration mechanism of MP-GCZ

The propulsion capacity of MP-GCZ is a decisive parameter that dictates their ability to overcome biophysical barriers and enable spatiotemporally controlled drug release within deep-tumor microenvironments. The motility mechanism of the MP-GCZ nanomotor is driven by the continuous generation of oxygen microbubbles in the tumor microenvironment (TME). The directional release of these microbubbles generates thrust, thereby enabling autonomous movement. Motility and displacement analyses further demonstrated the enhanced mobility of MP-GCZ (Fig. S4). Compared with ZIF-8 particles, which exhibited weak Brownian diffusion, MP-GCZ showed a pronounced nonlinear increase in MSD and was well fitted by the directed-motion model. The apparent velocity of MP-GCZ reached 2.21 ± 0.34 μm/s, with an R2 value of 0.990 ± 0.006, supporting its active and directional motility. The detailed MSD fitting parameters are summarized in Table S1. To clarify whether MP-GCZ can achieve subsequent therapeutic functions by effectively breaking through the endo-lysosomal barrier, we assessed lysosomal escape efficiency using confocal fluorescence imaging by co-localizing Cy5-labeled nanoparticles (red) with Lysotracker Green-stained lysosomes (green), comparing four experimental groups—bare ZIF-8 (control), bare ZIF-8 + NIR, MP-GCZ, and MP-GCZ + NIR—in mouse hepatocellular carcinoma Hepa1-6 cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, all four groups displayed strong red fluorescence after 4 h incubation, demonstrating the successful internalization of all four nanoparticles. The control group exhibited strong lysosomal retention, with nearly complete overlap of Cy5 and Lysotracker signals in line-scan profiles, indicating persistent nanoparticle entrapment. The bare ZIF-8 + NIR group demonstrated partial lysosomal escape mediated by mild photothermal effects; however, most nanoparticles remained confined within lysosomes.In contrast, the MP-GCZ group exhibited significantly reduced co-localization, primarily attributed to O2-driven autonomous motion. The CuO2 nanodots encapsulated in MP-GCZ could react with endo/lysosomal H2O2 to generate O2 microbubbles, whose rapid nucleation and collapse propelled MP-GCZ to exhibit directional movement within the confined endosomal space, accelerating endosomal rupture and cytosolic diffusion of Cy5 signal. Upon application of NIR irradiation, photothermal stimulation further enhanced both O2 microbubble generation and propulsion velocity, resulting in the lowest degree of co-localization and the highest cytosolic release efficiency among all groups. The asymmetric generation and release of O2 bubbles may provide transient local propulsion, facilitating nanoparticle movement through pre-existing pores and less-dense regions of the heterogeneous ECM rather than mechanically disrupting the entire collagen network. To investigate the endocytic pathways involved in MP-GCZ internalization, Hepa1-6 cells were pre-incubated with several endocytic inhibitors before exposure to nanoparticles (Fig. 3B), including chlorpromazine (CPZ, clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), methyl-β-cyclodextrin (Me-β-CD, caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), nystatin (lipid raft-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), and cytochalasin D (Cyto.D, actin polymerization inhibitor). Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3C) revealed that pre-treatment with Me-β-CD significantly reduced MP-GCZ uptake by 94.8 ± 1.3%. CPZ and nystatin resulted in a 68.7 ± 5.7% and 47.8 ± 2.8% reduction in uptake, respectively. In contrast, cyto.D treatment did not significantly affect internalization, suggesting that actin-mediated endocytosis is not a major contributor to MP-GCZ uptake. Importantly, fluorescence analysis of Cy5-labeled MP-GCZ showed a marked decrease to almost zero at 4 °C compared to 37 °C, indicating that MP-GCZ internalization is energy-dependent. To comprehensively evaluate the synergistic enhancement of cellular internalization mediated by self-propulsion and near-infrared (NIR) triggering, we monitored the intracellular distribution of MP-GCZ in Hepa1-6 cells using confocal fluorescence microscopy. As illustrated in Fig. 3E, MP-GCZ with NIR irradiation achieved deep penetration (∼120 μm from the spheroid surface) after 4 h, whereas control groups remained confined to the peripheral region. 3.4 Cuproptosis induction and mitochondrial dysfunction of MP-GCZ

Having established that MP-GCZ achieves efficient cellular internalization and deep tumor penetration, we next investigated whether this enhanced delivery translates to effective cuproptosis induction through mitochondrial dysfunction. As depicted in Fig. 4A, the CuO2 nanodots within MP-GCZ undergo catalytic decomposition upon encountering endogenous H+, releasing bioactive Cu2+ ions, which are further transported into mitochondria via the ATPase Copper Transporting Beta (ATP7B) transporter. Within the mitochondrial matrix, Cu2+ ions bind to lipoylated residues of key TCA cycle enzymes, primarily dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), destabilizing its structure and promoting oxidative degradation of iron-sulfur cluster proteins [27]. The process is amplified by ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), which reduces Cu2+ to more reactive Cu + species, and lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS), whose impaired activity disrupts lipoic acid metabolism [[28], [29], [30]]. These events lead to proteotoxic stress, TCA cycle dysfunction, and mitochondrial damage, culminating in cuproptosis [31]. To evaluate mitochondrial oxidative stress, we first measured ROS production using DCFH-DA fluorescence staining. As demonstrated in Fig. 4B, all treatment groups (CZ, GCZ, and MP-GCZ) significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels compared to untreated controls, with MP-GCZ showing the most pronounced effect (5.1-fold increase, Fig. 4C). Notably, NIR irradiation further amplified ROS generation in the MP-GCZ group, as evidenced by intense green fluorescence that was approximately 5.9-fold higher than control levels. This synergistic enhancement reflects the combined action of NIR-triggered photothermal effects and Cu2O-mediated Fenton-like reactions, which collectively drive excessive ROS accumulation and subsequent oxidative damage in mitochondria. Additionally, live/dead cell staining demonstrated the notable in vitro antitumor efficacy of MP-GCZ (Fig. S5). To further assess the copper dependence of MP-GCZ-induced cytotoxicity, a pharmacological rescue assay was performed using TTM. TTM treatment alone had negligible effects on Hepa1-6 cell viability. MP-GCZ treatment reduced cell viability to approximately 66.3%, whereas TTM co-treatment partially restored viability to about 85.1%. Similarly, MP-GCZ + NIR markedly decreased cell viability to approximately 34.4%, while TTM increased it to around 57.9% (Fig. S6). These partial rescue effects indicate that copper-dependent cytotoxicity contributes substantially to the antitumor activity of MP-GCZ. Mitochondrial membrane potential, a critical indicator of mitochondrial integrity, was evaluated using JC-1 staining [32]. In healthy mitochondria, JC-1 forms aggregates that emit red fluorescence, whereas monomeric JC-1 (green) indicates MMP depolarization. Fig. 4D shows that all the experimental groups triggered a striking shift from red to green fluorescence compared with the control group, with the MP-GCZ + NIR group exhibiting the highest proportion of monomeric JC-1—reaching 79.5% (Fig. 4E)—indicative of severe MMP collapse. To confirm intracellular Cu2+ accumulation, the Rhodamine B hydrazide (RBH), a fluorescent Cu2+-specific probe, was used to quantify the Cu2+. As shown in Fig. 4F and G, MP-GCZ + NIR treatment resulted in intense red fluorescence in Hepa1-6 cells, indicating significant Cu2+ accumulation, which was quantified as 9.8-fold higher than the control group. This result confirmed that NIR irradiation enhances Cu2+ release from MP-GCZ, providing the initial trigger for cuproptosis. To investigate downstream molecular events associated with cuproptosis, we examined DLAT, a lipoylated TCA cycle enzyme and central target in cuproptosis. Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4H) revealed that MP-GCZ + NIR induced DLAT aggregation (green puncta) in the cytoplasm, consistent with Cu+-mediated disruption of lipoylation. Western blotting (Fig. 4I) further demonstrated that MP-GCZ + NIR significantly downregulated Fe-S cluster proteins (ACO2, LIAS) and FDX1, while upregulating Heat Shock Protein 70 (HSP70, a proteotoxic stress chaperone), confirming Fe-S cluster homeostasis disruption and cellular stress (quantified in Fig. 4J). Control cells displayed intact mitochondria with dense cristae, whereas MP-GCZ + NIR-treated cells showed swollen mitochondria with fragmented cristae and vacuolar degeneration, hallmark features of cuproptosis. Taken together, these results demonstrate that MP-GCZ effectively induces cuproptosis in tumor cells through a copper ion–mediated destabilization of Fe–S cluster proteins, accompanied by robust ROS accumulation, mitochondrial depolarization, and proteotoxic stress. The synergistic action of NIR irradiation enhances copper release and exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby amplifying cuproptosis and establishing a potent cytotoxic mechanism against tumor cells.

3.5 Immunogenic cell death and immune activation of MP-GCZ

A major challenge in achieving effective cancer immunotherapy stems from the inherently immunosuppressive TME, which impedes dendritic cell (DC) maturation, T-cell activation, and the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapies [33]. To overcome this barrier, we leveraged the unique properties of cuproptosis to induce ICD and investigated whether MP-GCZ-mediated tumor cell death triggers immunogenic responses through the release of DAMPs and tumor antigens—key mediators in initiating systemic anti-tumor immunity. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, the successful induction of cuproptosis by MP-GCZ initiates a cascade of immunostimulatory events, beginning with the robust release of tumor-associated antigens and DAMPs, including high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), calreticulin (CRT), and ATP. These immunogenic signals serve as potent chemoattractants, recruiting immature DCs to infiltrate the TME. Following antigen capture and DAMPs recognition, the immature DCs undergo phenotypic and functional maturation, characterized by enhanced antigen processing and presentation capabilities [34]. The activated DCs then migrate to draining lymphoid tissues, where they efficiently present tumor-derived antigens to naive T cells, triggering their differentiation into tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [35]. To confirm the induction of ICD, we systematically evaluated the expression and release of key DAMPs. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated significant intracellular translocation and extracellular release of HMGB1 in the MP-GCZ + NIR treatment group, as evidenced by prominent cytosolic HMGB1 aggregation and enhanced extracellular signal intensity compared to the controls (Fig. 5B). Similarly, surface exposure of CRT—a well-established marker of ICD—was markedly elevated in the MP-GCZ + NIR group (Fig. 5C), while control groups displayed minimal signal. Quantitative flow cytometry measurements further corroborated these findings, showing that MP-GCZ + NIR treatment increased CRT mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) by approximately 4.1-fold relative to control, while MP-GCZ alone produced a 2.1-fold increase (Fig. 5E). Consistent with these observations, extracellular HMGB1 concentration (Fig. 5F) and ATP secretion (Fig. 5G) showed treatment-dependent increases, with the MP-GCZ + NIR group reaching 485.7 pg/mL and 29.8 nM, corresponding to 3.2- and 5.9-fold increases relative to the untreated control group, respectively. These data collectively demonstrate the synergistic induction of ICD through the combined effects of cuproptosis and photothermal activation.

Further studies were conducted to evaluate the ability of released DAMPs and tumor antigens to activate DCs—an essential step in antigen presentation and the initiation of adaptive immunity. Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) cultured with conditioned media from MP-GCZ-treated tumor cells revealed substantial upregulation of maturation markers CD80 and CD86 in the MP-GCZ + NIR group (Fig. 5H and J). These markers are critical for DC functionality, as CD80 and CD86 are co-stimulatory molecules that interact with T-cell receptors to promote T-cell activation and immune priming. Quantitative analysis showed that MP-GCZ + NIR treatment elevated the percentage of CD86+CD80+ DCs to 61.0%, representing a significant increase compared to MP-GCZ alone (42.0%),GCZ (36.3%), CZ (36.2%) and negative control (15.1%) (Fig. 5K). In contrast, CD206 is a marker of M2-like macrophage/DC phenotype, associated with immunosuppressive functions, and is often found in tumors with poor immune infiltration. The CD206/CD80 ratio (Fig. 5I) was used as a critical measure of DC polarization status, reflecting the balance between immunostimulatory (M1-like) and immunosuppressive (M2-like) DCs. The decrease in the CD206/CD80 ratio to 0.27 in the MP-GCZ + NIR group compared to the controls (MP-GCZ alone:0.49, GCZ: 0.51, CZ: 0.51, control: 0.72) indicates a shift towards a pro-inflammatory DC phenotype, which is essential for activating CTLs and promoting an effective antitumor immune response. This shift in DC polarization suggests that MP-GCZ in combination with NIR, reprograms the immune microenvironment from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory, enhancing the adaptive immune response. The enhanced DC maturation observed in the MP-GCZ + NIR group likely resulted from the cooperative effects of cuproptosis-induced DAMP release and photothermal stress. MP-GCZ without NIR produced detectable immune activation, supporting the contribution of copper-dependent cell death, whereas NIR irradiation may additionally promote DAMP exposure and amplify cuproptosis by enhancing copper uptake and mitochondrial damage. Nevertheless, the relative contributions of these two modalities cannot be fully distinguished in the present study.

3.6 Transcriptomics reveals MP-GCZ's anticancer mechanism

3.7 In vivo antitumor efficacy and collagen remodeling induced by MP-GCZ

To rigorously assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of MP-GCZ, a Hepa1-6 murine hepatocellular carcinoma model was established in C57BL/6 mice via subcutaneous tumor inoculation, followed by systemic administration of different nanoparticle formulations with or without NIR irradiation. The treatment regimen was designed to reflect clinically relevant protocols, involving intravenous injection via the tail vein on Days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8, with endpoint assessments conducted on Day 14 (Fig. 7A). Real-time fluorescence imaging using in vivo imaging (IVIS) revealed that MP-GCZ exhibited markedly prolonged tumor retention compared with control nanoparticles. The fluorescence signal of MP-GCZ persisted at the tumor site for up to 48 h, in contrast to the rapid clearance and diffuse biodistribution observed in CZ and GCZ groups (Fig. 7B). Ex vivo organ fluorescence imaging further confirmed the preferential tumor accumulation of MP-GCZ, with minimal distribution in major organs (Fig. 7C). This enhanced retention may be attributed to the CM coating, which could enable MP-GCZ to evade immune surveillance and enhance tumor homing by mimicking the surface properties of tumor cells, thereby facilitating the accumulation of high MP-GCZ concentrations within the TME. Thermal imaging during NIR irradiation validated the potent photothermal conversion capability of MP-GCZ. Upon laser exposure, tumor temperatures in the MP-GCZ + NIR group rapidly increased to approximately 49.3 °C within 5 min, while control groups exhibited only negligible temperature changes (<37.1 °C) (Fig. 7D). The efficient heat generation results from the strong NIR absorption of GNS integrated within the ZIF-8 framework, ensuring both spatially localized hyperthermia and enhanced permeability of tumor cell membranes to copper ions, thereby amplifying cuproptosis. The Macroscopic examination (Fig. 7E) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 7F) of excised tumor tissues further corroborated the imaging findings, with tumors from MP-GCZ + NIR-treated mice exhibiting a marked reduction in size, demonstrating the significant inhibitory effect of MP-GCZ on tumor progression. Endpoint tumor weight measurements on day 14 further confirmed this therapeutic trend, with MP-GCZ + NIR treatment resulting in significantly reduced tumor weights of ∼0.37 ± 0.21 g compared to ∼0.82 ± 0.15 g in the MP-GCZ alone group, 1.16 ± 0.21 g in the GCZ group, 1.29 ± 0.26 g in the CZ group, and 1.45 ± 0.25 g in the negative control (Fig. 7G).Fig. 7 In vivo antitumor efficacy and tumor microenvironment remodeling induced by MP-GCZ. Histopathological analysis provided further insights into therapeutic efficacy and tumor microenvironment remodeling. H&E staining (Fig. 7H–top row) revealed extensive necrosis and loss of cellular integrity in the MP-GCZ + NIR group, while control tumors maintained densely packed viable cells. Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 7H, second row) highlighted a striking reduction in stromal fibrosis for MP-GCZ + NIR tumors with collagen deposition decreasing to 11.5%, with higher levels observed in MP-GCZ alone (27.8%), GCZ (67.2%), CZ (82.5%), and negative control (92.3%) (Fig. 7I), indicating effective ECM remodeling. This ECM degradation may alleviate mechanical barriers to drug penetration and promote immune cell infiltration, addressing critical limitations of conventional therapies in solid tumors. Immunohistochemical staining for Ki67 (Fig. 7H, third row), a key proliferation marker, showed MP-GCZ + NIR treatment strongly suppressed proliferative activity with 11.8% Ki67-positive cells, compared to higher rates in MP-GCZ alone (35.1%), and negative control (93.1%) groups (Fig. 7J). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assays (Fig. 7H, bottom row) demonstrated extensive apoptosis in MP-GCZ + NIR tumors (55.4% TUNEL-positive area), with lower levels in negative control (5.8%) groups (Fig. 7K). Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), a protease implicated in tumor metastasis through degradation of type IV collagen, was found to be significantly downregulated in the MP-GCZ + NIR group based on immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. S8). Collectively, these findings confirm that MP-GCZ + NIR treatment exerts potent antitumor effects while simultaneously remodeling the TME. The synergy between photothermal therapy, cuproptosis induction, and ECM remodeling not only enhances tumor suppression but also creates a more permissive immune microenvironment by reducing fibrosis and increasing antigen accessibility. These remodeling effects are particularly critical for overcoming the challenges posed by the dense stroma and hypoxic conditions commonly found in solid tumors, which typically hinder drug delivery and immune activation.

3.8 MP-GCZ-mediated immune microenvironment modulation and antitumor immune activation

The therapeutic efficacy of MP-GCZ is attributable not only to its direct cytotoxic effects but also to its ability to reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment into an immunologically active state, thereby promoting sustained systemic antitumor responses (Fig. 8A). To validate the effectiveness of this delivery mechanism in vivo, we further analyzed the spatial distribution of the nanomotors within tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. S9, the non-motile control group exhibited limited tumor penetration, with only faint red fluorescence signals detected. In stark contrast, the autonomously propelled MP-GCZ nanomotors demonstrated significantly enhanced tissue penetration. To further explore whether MP-GCZ combined with NIR irradiation exerts immunomodulatory effects, we systematically evaluated immune cell infiltration, stromal remodeling, and effector T cell responses in the hepatocellular carcinoma tumor model. The hypoxia-alleviating capacity of the MP-GCZ was first assessed using the HP2 probe (Hypoxyprobe™-1 Plus Kit), which demonstrated improved tumor oxygenation in the MP-GCZ + NIR-treated group, as evidenced by minimal green fluorescence compared to controls (Fig. 8B). Immunofluorescence staining of HIF-1α (Fig. 8C), a key regulator of hypoxic responses, revealed that tumors in the control, CZ, and GCZ groups exhibited extensive hypoxia, characterized by intense red fluorescence signals. In contrast, MP-GCZ monotherapy reduced HIF-1α levels, and this effect was further amplified by NIR irradiation, consistent with the O2-generating activity of CuO2 nanodots. Concurrently, functional perfused vessels were quantified via lectin+/CD31+ co-staining and expressed as a percentage of the total tumor area. The results (Fig. 8D) demonstrate that MP-GCZ + NIR treatment promoted vascular normalization, a process characterized by enhanced pericyte coverage. Increased pericyte coverage reduces vascular permeability, thereby suppressing tumor cell invasion and distant metastasis. Therefore, we subsequently evaluated pericyte coverage in tumor vessels. Dual immunofluorescence (IF) staining for CD31 and the pericyte marker α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) revealed a higher density of pericyte-covered endothelial cells in tumors from MP-GCZ + NIR-treated mice compared to the control group, confirming that the treatment indeed induced vascular normalization (Fig. S10). To confirm immune cell recruitment, CD95 staining was used as a surrogate marker for infiltrating leukocytes. CD95, a key death receptor in the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily, mediates apoptotic cell death upon binding to its ligand, CD95L (FasL), which is typically expressed by activated cytotoxic immune cells, including CD8+ T lymphocytes and NK cells [39]. To investigate the antitumor immunological mechanisms of MP-GCZ combined with NIR treatment, changes in tumor immunogenicity following therapy were first analyzed. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that tumor cells in the MP-GCZ + NIR treatment group exhibited significantly elevated CD95 expression, which was 6.6-fold higher than that in the control group (Fig. 8E and F). Concurrently, MHC-I expression levels were also markedly increased in the MP-GCZ + NIR group compared to the control (Fig. S11A), further supporting that the combination treatment significantly enhances immune cell infiltration into tumors. Subsequent flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells revealed increased proportions of Ki-67+ CD8+ T cells—a marker of proliferative activity—in the MP-GCZ + NIR group, accounting for 5.02% of total CD8+ T cells (Fig. S11C). Additionally, TNF-α, a cytotoxic cytokine secreted by CD8+ T cells that promotes tumor cell death via ligand-receptor interactions, was also significantly elevated. The percentage of TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells was 4.4-fold higher in the MP-GCZ + NIR group compared to the control (Fig. S11B). These CD8+ T cells also exhibited higher IFN-γ production (Fig. 8G and H), with 33.9% of CD8+ T cells expressing IFN-γ in the MP-GCZ + NIR group, compared to 0.47% in control, 11.4% in CZ, 19.4% in GCZ, and 21.9% in MP-GCZ alone. This robust enhancement of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells suggests robust activation of adaptive antitumor immunity and highlights the synergistic effect of cuproptosis-enhanced anti-PD-L1 therapy and PTT, which together augment antigen presentation and T cell priming. Importantly, these findings align with earlier ICD analyses (HMGB1 release and CRT exposure, data in Fig. 5B and C), establishing a mechanistic link between MP-GCZ-mediated cell death and subsequent T cell activation, which could further sensitize tumors to anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy.

DCs maturation, a critical step for antigen presentation, which is central to bridging innate and adaptive immunity, was also enhanced by MP-GCZ + NIR. The percentage of mature DCs (CD40+CD11c+ cells) in tumors (Fig. 8I and J) increased to 15.3% in the MP-GCZ + NIR group, versus 1.25% in control, 1.89% in CZ, 2.38% in GCZ, and 2.66% in MP-GCZ alone, indicating improved antigen processing and presentation capacity, which are crucial for the cross-priming of cytotoxic lymphocytes and the amplification of adaptive immune responses. Conversely, the number of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which suppress antitumor immunity, was significantly reduced. FOXP3 expression (Fig. 8L), a transcription factor that is primarily expressed in Tregs, was downregulated in MP-GCZ + NIR tumors compared to the controls. Besides DCs and Tregs, NK cells also play a key role in recognizing and eliminating tumor cells that downregulate major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, a common immune evasion tactic in tumors [40]. To further dissect the immune milieu, we analyzed NK cell infiltration through NK1.1 staining (Fig. 8K). The results revealed significantly increased NK cell infiltration in MP-GCZ + NIR tumors compared to other groups, consistent with an immune-activating microenvironment. Immunofluorescence analyses provided additional confirmation of the immune remodeling induced by MP-GCZ. As shown in Fig. 8M, tumors treated with MP-GCZ + NIR exhibited robust infiltration of CD8+ T cells and strong CRT exposure. Notably, this immunogenic cascade was negligible or markedly attenuated in the control groups, highlighting MP-GCZ's distinct ability to convert immunologically quiescent tumors into an inflamed state permissive to immune-mediated destruction. In summary, these findings underscore the ability of MP-GCZ + NIR to simultaneously enhance direct tumor cell cytotoxicity and reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment. By alleviating hypoxia, remodeling vasculature, enhancing antigen presentation, and rebalancing immune cell populations, MP-GCZ substantially reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby contributing to a more favorable immune microenvironment for anti-tumor immunity.

To investigate the underlying mechanisms of this abscopal effect, immune cell infiltration within distant tumors was analyzed. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that MP-GCZ + NIR treatment significantly upregulated the expression of MHC-I on tumor cells compared to the control group (78.3% in the treatment group vs. 0.35% in the control) (Fig. 9F). A similar upregulation trend was observed in the expression levels of CD95 and CD11C (Fig. S12), suggesting significant immune cell enrichment at distal sites. Ki67 staining further revealed a notable increase in proliferating CD8+ T cells within the tumors of the MP-GCZ + NIR group (Fig. 9G), confirming the enhanced proliferation of CD8+ T cells induced by the treatment. The coordinated upregulation of MHC-I and increased CD8+ T cell infiltration provides a mechanistic basis for antigen recognition and immune-mediated tumor cell killing, supporting the hypothesis that MP-GCZ + NIR transforms immunologically "cold" tumors into inflamed, immune-responsive microenvironments conducive to systemic antitumor immunity.

We next evaluated whether this systemic immunity could prevent metastatic colonization using an experimental lung metastasis model. C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with Hepa1-6 cells to mimic hematogenous spread, followed by intravenous MP-GCZ administration and NIR irradiation (Fig. 9H). On day 14, lungs were excised for metastatic burden analysis. Photographic analysis revealed that the control mice developed extensive, large metastatic nodules throughout the lung parenchyma, whereas MP-GCZ + NIR-treated mice displayed only scattered micrometastatic foci (Fig. 9I), indicating substantial suppression of metastatic colonization. Quantitative analysis showed that MP-GCZ + NIR treatment reduced both the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules and the relative metastatic area to only 4.8% and 1.9% of the control group, respectively (Fig. 9K and L). Notably, even MP-GCZ monotherapy showed moderate antimetastatic effects, likely due to its intrinsic cuproptosis-inducing properties, but NIR irradiation significantly enhanced this activity, consistent with our earlier data showing synergistic ICD induction by cuproptosis and photothermal therapy. These results align with the findings from the bilateral tumor model, where MP-GCZ not only inhibited primary tumor growth but also suppressed metastasis through an immune-activating microenvironment. Taken together, the findings from both the bilateral tumor model and the lung metastasis model validate the multifaceted therapeutic efficacy of MP-GCZ. MP-GCZ not only inhibits primary tumor growth through photothermal ablation and cuproptosis but also prevents metastasis by modulating the immune microenvironment and enhancing immune cell infiltration. The combination of PTT and cuproptosis provides a potent synergy that reprograms the TME to promote systemic antitumor immunity. Although the bilateral tumor and lung metastasis models provide evidence of systemic antitumor effects within the investigated period, longer follow-up periods, survival analyses, tumor rechallenge experiments, and longitudinal evaluation of memory T-cell responses will be required to determine whether MP-GCZ induces persistent immune protection and reduces tumor recurrence.

3.10 Safety evaluation of MP-GCZ

To assess the biosafety profile of MP-GCZ, hemocompatibility was evaluated via a hemolysis assay. Across a concentration gradient ranging from 50 to 800 μg mL−1, the hemolysis rate remained consistently low at 3.16 ± 0.4%, demonstrating excellent blood compatibility even at the highest concentration of 800 μg mL−1 (Fig. S13). Furthermore, histopathological examination of major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. S14). No noticeable inflammatory cell infiltration, structural disorder, or necrotic damage was observed in any treatment group. The tissue architecture remained consistent with that of the healthy control group. Collectively, these findings support the favorable short-term biocompatibility of MP-GCZ. However, degradation of CuO2 and ZIF-8 releases Cu2+ and Zn2+, respectively, and repeated administration may potentially lead to metal-ion accumulation or disturbance of metal homeostasis in normal tissues. Long-term biodistribution, clearance, and toxicity studies are therefore required to further establish the safety of MP-GCZ. Despite the promising preclinical efficacy, several translational challenges remain. Large-scale production will require reproducible and GMP-compatible control of particle composition and membrane coating. The tumor cell membrane may introduce batch variability and unintended immunogenicity, requiring standardized sourcing and safety evaluation. Moreover, the limited tissue penetration of 808 nm NIR may restrict treatment of deep-seated tumors, for which interstitial light delivery or longer-wavelength photothermal systems may be required.

4 Conclusion

In this study, we presented MP-GCZ, a self-oxygenating and biomimetic nanomotor platform that integrates multiple therapeutic modalities into a single system to achieve potent antitumor efficacy. At the mechanistic level, the inclusion of copper peroxide nanodots enables MP-GCZ to react with hydrogen ions in the tumor microenvironment, thereby generating both cytotoxic Cu2+ ions to trigger cuproptosis and oxygen to alleviate intratumoral hypoxia. This dual functionality is critical, as hypoxia not only diminishes the efficacy of oxidative stress-based therapies but also fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Simultaneously, the integration of gold nanostars provides a highly efficient photothermal component, where near-infrared irradiation enhances copper uptake, disrupts mitochondrial integrity, and amplifies immunogenic cell death. In parallel, the ZIF-8 framework serves as a pH-responsive carrier that releases zinc ions to stimulate the cGAS–STING pathway, further broadening immune activation. Such a multifaceted design exemplifies the emerging paradigm of nanomedicines that act not as passive drug carriers but as dynamic therapeutic systems capable of interacting with and reshaping pathological environments. Biologically, MP-GCZ achieves a rare convergence of direct tumor killing and systemic immune activation. The induction of cuproptosis produces a characteristic immunogenic profile defined by the release of HMGB1, exposure of calreticulin, and secretion of ATP. These signals drive dendritic cell maturation and subsequent T cell priming, effectively bridging innate sensing with adaptive immunity. The addition of anti-PD-L1 antibodies strengthens this immune cascade by releasing cytotoxic T lymphocytes from checkpoint inhibition, while the relief of hypoxia and normalization of tumor vasculature enhance immune cell infiltration. In preclinical models, these interconnected effects translated into strong suppression of primary tumor growth, abscopal effects against untreated distal tumors, and a significant reduction in metastatic lung colonization. By uniting the mechanistic strengths of cuproptosis, photothermal therapy, immune checkpoint blockade, and dynamic microenvironment remodeling, MP-GCZ demonstrates how rational nanomedicine design can overcome the long-standing barriers that limit conventional cancer therapies. Thus, this platform holds potential as a promising strategy for synergistic and multimodal cancer therapy.
