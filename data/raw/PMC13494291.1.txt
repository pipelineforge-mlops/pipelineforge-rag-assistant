Background

Access to medicines remains a persistent global challenge. In Chile, several policies have sought to strengthen pharmaceutical coverage within the health system. Among them, the Explicit Health Guarantees (GES) and the Pharmacy Fund (FOFAR) represent major policy milestones.

Objective

This study assesses the impact of GES and FOFAR on access to medicines for diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension among beneficiaries of both public and private subsystems.

Design

A contribution analysis approach was applied using a mixed-methods design.

Methods

Quantitative analyses combined descriptive and quasi-experimental difference-in-differences methods based on repeated cross-sectional data from national health surveys (2003, 2009/10, 2016/17), focusing on medicine use and effective coverage. The qualitative component included ten semi-structured interviews with key informants involved in policy design and implementation.

Results

Results show that both GES and FOFAR substantially contributed to improving access to medicines for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Quantitative findings revealed marked increases in medicine use and effective coverage, particularly between 2010 and 2017, while qualitative evidence highlighted their complementary and reinforcing policy roles.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings suggest that GES and FOFAR were likely important contributors to improvements in contact coverage and effective coverage for these chronic conditions in Chile, although these changes occurred alongside ongoing health-system strengthening efforts and should therefore be interpreted accordingly. Overall, incorporating medicines into social protection schemes—especially through enforceable guarantees—plays a key role in expanding access. Sustained impact requires adequate resources, robust logistics, and strong implementation capacity for chronic disease management.

Plain language summary

Access to essential medicines is a challenge around the world, and many people still struggle to get the treatments they need for chronic diseases. In Chile, two major public policies—the Explicit Health Guarantees (GES) and the Primary Health Care Pharmacy Fund (FOFAR)—were created to improve access to medicines for conditions such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol. These conditions are common in the country and can lead to serious complications if not properly treated. This study looked at whether GES and FOFAR helped people obtain and use the medicines required to manage these chronic diseases. To do this, two types of information were combined: data from the National Health Survey (collected in 2003, 2010 and 2017), and interviews with people involved in designing and implementing these policies. The results show that both GES and FOFAR contributed to better access to medicines. Between 2010 and 2017, more people with diabetes and high blood pressure were taking their medications, and more had their conditions under control. For people with high cholesterol who receive care in the public system (FONASA), the FOFAR program also helped increase medicine use compared with those in the private system (ISAPRE), who did not receive the same benefit. Interviews with key decision-makers helped explain why these improvements occurred. They highlighted that GES established enforceable rights to receive care, while FOFAR strengthened primary care by providing extra resources specifically for medicines. Interviewees also described remaining challenges, such as logistical problems, limited resources, and persistent beliefs that free medicines are of lower quality. Overall, the study shows that integrating medicines into social protection policies can significantly improve access, but sustained progress requires good financing, strong primary care, efficient delivery systems, and regular monitoring of policy performance.

Background

Despite the progress made in recent decades, lack of access to essential medicines is one of the most serious public health problems globally, with an estimated two billion people lacking regular access to essential medicines. 1

In Chile, access to medicines depends on the health coverage scheme individuals are affiliated with (National Health Fund or FONASA -80% population-, private insurers or ISAPRE -15%-, or the Armed Forces and other insurers -about 5%-), the health problem or condition they have (depending on whether it is covered or not), whether they have supplementary insurance, and also on individual preferences, as people can always choose to purchase directly from the retail market. 2 As at the global level, access to medicines is a relevant and current problem in Chile. In fact, it has been recognized as a cross-cutting problem, being, in different ways, on the political agenda or in the government programs of the last six elected governments3–8 and is also reflected in the policies or programs implemented during this period.9–12 The problem has been addressed, among others, through the development of national drug formularies (since 1968) and national drug policies. Within this framework, the country has sought to implement policies and programs that, among their objectives, have included improving drug coverage by the health system. The establishment of Explicit Health Guarantees (GES, by its acronym in Spanish), in 2005, 9 and the Primary Health Care Pharmacy Fund (FOFAR, by its acronym in Spanish), in 2014, 11 are part of these policies. GES refers to explicit guarantees of access, timeliness, financial protection, and quality of care, covering 87 health conditions and their associated interventions as of mid-2025. 13

Treatments associated with hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia have been subject to improvements in coverage. These three conditions are highly prevalent in Chile, with 27.6%, 12.3%, and 45.8%/35.8%/27.8% (depending on decreased HDL/elevated triglycerides/elevated total cholesterol) of the Chilean population aged 15 years and older as of 2017. 14 However, and despite the GES for hypertension and diabetes since 2005, access to medicines associated with these three conditions has presented difficulties, which justified, in 2014, the creation of FOFAR. The purpose of this program is to provide more timely access to the population treated in primary care facilities, with close, comprehensive and quality care through strategies to strengthen this level of care, 15 providing funding specifically for hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia. It should be noted that FOFAR does not consider the ISAPRE beneficiary population.

In this context, it is worth asking whether the policies implemented, particularly those aimed at the health system’s coverage of medicines, have had the intended impact in terms of guaranteeing access to medicines for the Chilean population.

Thus, this study aims at assessing the impact of GES and FOFAR on access to medicines associated with the treatment of diabetes, dyslipidemia and hypertension in Chile, for beneficiaries of FONASA and ISAPRE.

This article is composed of this first introductory section, followed by the methods proposed to meet the objective set out above, a third section of results and, finally, the discussion of the results is presented.

Methods

The study considered a contribution analysis approach, based on a mixed-method design, including a quantitative and qualitative perspective.16,17 Contribution analysis is particularly suited to complex contexts where multiple factors may influence outcomes, as in the case of the GES and FOFAR which are not the only programs or interventions that occurred in the period analyzed.18–20 Therefore, the idea was to focus on the potential contributions of GES mainly, and also of FOFAR, on observable outcomes, identifying possible pathways and steps through which these contributions might have been operationalized. To this end, as shown in Figure 1, a theory of change was developed a priori, outlining the expected causal pathways and intermediate steps (inputs, activities). Quantitative analysis was focus on examining outcomes aligned with the proposed theory, while qualitative data provided contextual insights and helped to assess the plausibility of the contribution claim.Figure 1. Causal pathways and methods integration. Source: authors’ elaboration.

The quantitative component included the descriptive analysis of the cascade of care21,22 in the population over 17 years of age, considering:- Awareness: person knows his or her diagnosis,

- contact coverage: person makes use of the medicines, 23 and

- effective coverage: person with clinical control of the condition (blood pressure <90/140; total cholesterol =<200; fasting glycaemia <126), indicating not only access to treatment but also therapeutic effectiveness. 23

In addition, a quasi-experimental difference-in-differences (DID) design24,25 was employed to assess changes associated with the treatment of hypertension and diabetes before and after their incorporation into the GES program in 2005. This analysis focused on the use of medicines (contact coverage) and the achievement of effective coverage. As these conditions were universally covered for all FONASA and ISAPRE beneficiaries from mid-2005, the identification of a traditional untreated control group was not feasible. To address this limitation, individuals diagnosed with dyslipidemia— a chronic condition managed within the same primary healthcare context but not included in GES—were used as a quasi-counterfactual group. Although certain treatments for dyslipidemia may overlap pharmacologically with those for hypertension and diabetes, and increased healthcare contact among GES-covered patients could also increase opportunities for dyslipidemia diagnosis and treatment, this issue was mitigated by restricting the control group to individuals with dyslipidemia only, excluding those with comorbid GES-covered conditions.

Furthermore, the analysis exploited a secondary policy change: the incorporation of dyslipidemia into the FOFAR program in 2014, alongside hypertension and diabetes. For FONASA beneficiaries, the pre-2014 period thus provides a clearer contrast between conditions covered and not covered by public pharmaceutical benefits. In the case of ISAPRE, where dyslipidemia was not incorporated into FOFAR, the comparison of medicine use and effective coverage between FONASA and ISAPRE beneficiaries with dyslipidemia (aged 15 and older) enables a second quasi-experimental contrast after 2014. In this scenario, the ISAPRE population with dyslipidemia functions as the counterfactual, allowing for the estimation of the effect of FOFAR coverage on access to medicines and disease control outcomes among FONASA beneficiaries.

The data used are from the National Health Survey (ENS, by its acronym in Spanish), which was conducted in 2003, 2009/2010 and 2016/2017.26–29 All three versions of the survey correspond to a cross-sectional studies with a complex, randomized, and stratified design, with national and urban/rural representativeness, as well as regional representativeness in the 2009–2010 and 2016–2017 versions.26–29 All three versions of the survey have direct measurement for the three conditions studied, either by blood pressure or blood sampling. In its 2003 version, the ENS included hypertension and diabetes among the disease-specific modules (diagnosis by a professional, self-report of pharmacological treatment), while the 2009/2010 and 2016/2017 versions also included dyslipidemia. In this context, a repeated cross-sectional design was used. 30 In the supplementary material ‘Database preparation for ENS 2003, 2009/2010, and 2016/2017’, the detailed specification of the variables used, as well as the statistical analysis, is provided.

In the case of the DID analysis, similar to recent studies that have reviewed the effects of Medicaid expansion on pregnant women’s access to health care 31 and women of reproductive age, 32 in the GES evaluation (equation 1), the model specification is as follows: (1) Yi,t=β0+β1GESi,t+β2Post+β3(GESi,t·Post)+β4Yeart+β5Xi,t+εi,t

- Outcome: Yi,t takes the value 1 when individuals use medication for their condition or have their condition under control, depending on whether it refers to contact coverage or effective coverage, respectively.

- Exposure: GES takes the value 1 in the case of individuals presenting hypertension and/or diabetes and 0 in the case of presenting dyslipidemia (dyslipidemia only). Post is a 0/1 indicator of whether the outcome was measured pre- or post-2005 (0 for the year 2003, and 1 for the years 2009/2010 and/or 2016/2017).

- Control: Xi,t is a vector of control variables that includes Sex (female=1), Age range (15-64 years; 65 years and over), Educational level (<8 years, 8-12 years, 13 years and over), Marital status (single, separated, or widowed; married or cohabiting) and Area (urban=1).

In the case of the FOFAR evaluation models (equation 2) the specification is similar, and the control variables and Yi,t are the same. In this case, Post is a 0/1 indicator of whether the outcome was measured pre- or post-2014 (0 for the year 2009/2010, and 1 for the year 2016/2017); and FOFAR takes the value 1 for FONASA beneficiaries and 0 for ISAPRE beneficiaries. For the purposes of this analysis, the entire population with dyslipidemia was considered, not excluding those with diabetes or hypertension. (2) Yi,t=β0+β1FOFARi,t+β2Post+β3(FOFARi,t·Post)+β4Yeart+β5Xi,t+εi,t

In both analyses, equations (1) and (2), the effect of interest is the marginal effect associated with the variables GESt·Post and FOFARt·Post respectively, which captures the average causal effect of the intervention (GES or FOFAR) on contact coverage and effective coverage — that is, the differential change in the probability of use/control among individuals with hypertension or diabetes (or dyslipidemia in the case of FONASA beneficiaries) after the implementation of the policy (GES/FOFAR), compared to the change observed in the control group with dyslipidemia (or dyslipidemia in the case of ISAPRE beneficiaries) over the same period.

The validity of this DID approach rests on the parallel trends assumption, which posits that, in the absence of the GES intervention, trends in contact coverage and effective coverage for hypertension and diabetes would have followed a similar trajectory to those for dyslipidemia. While the available survey waves did not allow a conventional formal assessment of pre-intervention parallel trends (only one pre-period), all three conditions are highly prevalent chronic diseases managed within the same primary healthcare system and requiring long-term follow-up. Additionally, supplementary stratified analyses were conducted separately for contact coverage and effective coverage outcomes across survey waves, together with an exploratory placebo-type analysis using the intermediate survey wave (2009/2010) as a reference period and 2016/2017 as the comparison period, to further explore the temporal dynamics of the estimated effects during the phased implementation and consolidation. All analyses were based on repeated cross-sectional survey data and therefore estimate population-level changes across implementation periods rather than individual longitudinal trajectories.

To complement the quantitative findings, a qualitative component consisting of semi-structured interviews was implemented. This approach aimed to explore the perspectives of high-level stakeholders involved in the decision-making processes of the analyzed health policies. The research focused on identifying the implicit and explicit objectives of the GES and FOFAR programs regarding pharmaceutical access, their structural components, and implementation challenges. Specifically, the inquiry addressed stakeholders’ perceptions of the programs’ contributions to medicine access, the mechanisms driving these improvements, and the barriers encountered. Detailed interview questions are provided in the supplementary material, ‘Semi-structured Interview Guide’.

A purposive sampling strategy was employed, considering as a fundamental criterion the inclusion of parties responsible for designing the analyzed programs and/or who have been part of their implementation or monitoring process to date. While acknowledging the relevance of healthcare providers and patients, this study adopted a top-down, population-level perspective. Therefore, the sample was restricted to high-level decision-makers to strengthen the study’s validity through expert insight. Perceived patients’ barriers and facilitators on access to medicines for the treatment of chronic diseases in Chile have been reported elsewhere. 33 Fifteen potential participants, including Ministry of Health (MoH) authorities and representatives from related institutions (2005 up to the date of contact), were contacted by one of the authors via email, from 8 March 2021, using a standard letter subscribed by both of the authors. This communication detailed the study objectives, participant role, and confidentiality protocols, including the requirement for informed consent. Two potential participants, both members of Congress, declined to participate: one due to excessive workload, and the other because of a demanding legislative schedule and a policy of not granting interviews to students.

The final sample comprised former ministers, parliamentarians, current and former MoH division heads, and members of the GES Advisory Council. Interviews were conducted virtually via Zoom between March and July 2021, by one of the authors (a female experienced researcher and PhD candidate, with two master’s degrees), with only the interviewer and interviewee present. Data saturation was reached with ten interviews (with an average duration of one hour), and no repeat interviews were necessary. The data production process involved audio recording, note-taking during the interviews, transcription, and analysis using Atlas. ti 9 software. 34 All data management was conducted on one of the authors’ personal computers, with all appropriate security safeguards in place. The transcripts were not returned to participants for correction, although the final results were shared with them without requesting feedback.

A deductive and interpretative content analysis was performed using cyclical coding. 35 Initial categories were derived deductively from the interview guide’s pre-established dimensions, while supplementary categories emerged inductively from the data. These topics were refined through iterative re-coding and conceptual delimitation until category stabilization.

Following the constant comparative method, data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. This simultaneous approach facilitated preliminary analyses after the initial interviews, allowing researchers to compare findings across respondents. These comparisons informed subsequent interview adjustments, including the selection of further interviewees and the identification of specific themes requiring deeper exploration or conceptual development.

Finally, in the present study, a mixed-methods design was proposed, incorporating the previously described quantitative and qualitative components. In line with the approach adopted to meet the study objectives, integration was carried out using a merging strategy—that is, by combining both types of collected data for analysis, based on the premise that neither source or individual analysis could, on its own, adequately address the main research objective. 36 Integration or triangulation of the quantitative and qualitative components took place during the discussion, interpretation, and analysis of the results. In this context, the findings from one approach were analyzed in light of those from the other, and vice versa, in order to build an integrated narrative regarding the contribution of the analyzed programs to improving access to medicines among FONASA and ISAPRE beneficiaries, combining convergent findings and identifying contradictory ones that could help assess the previously proposed causal pathways.

The project was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine (Universidad de Chile), on 15 December 2020.

Qualitative analysis

The results of the analysis of the key stakeholder interviews are systematized in Figure 5.Figure 5. Diagram of categories and subcategories, interview analysis. Source: authors’ elaboration based on interviews.

The main category of this study is access to medicines, and the questions it seeks to answer relate to how health policies that offer guarantees of drug coverage for certain conditions affect access to medicines, what have been the main problems or shortcomings in this process, and what could be changed to achieve better access.

In this way, three subcategories were identified, which are given by the mechanisms through which GES and FOFAR have modified access to medicines for covered conditions, the barriers that have hindered the expected effects of GES on this access, and finally, the needs for improvement, which would allow a greater impact to be achieved. The results associated with both the main category and the subcategories mentioned above are presented in more detail below.

Main category - Access to medicines: Several relevant points emerge from the analysis in relation to access to medicines in Chile, its evolution, how the GES burst into a specific context in which the problem of access was exacerbating in the country, and how FOFAR came, at the time, to reinforce the GES. Thus, from a historical perspective, prior to the GES, the country had a moment of great development in relation to medicines in the 1960s, with a developed local industry, the development of the first national list of medicines, price regulation, availability of products at low prices, etc. However, in the 1970s, medicines began to be increasingly considered consumer goods, and later, in the 1990s, international industrial property agreements emerged, with the consequent problems associated with access to medicines. In this deteriorating context, following the boom of the 1960s, the implementation of the GES would have effectively improved access to medicines for prioritized health problems. From the analysis, it emerges that this improvement in access is mainly due to GES offering guaranteed coverage and incorporate medicines as part of the social protection system, thus causing a significant change in the way the health system and the people themselves approached medicines. The following is an example of what respondents reported in this regard:“Because access to medicines was not part of social protection policies. In other words, I could have an operation for cancer, for anything, but not the medicines that followed. I could be diagnosed with high blood pressure, and they would give me a prescription, I had to go to the pharmacy and buy that medicine. In other words, there was no social protection in terms of access to medicines” (E2).

FOFAR, on the other hand, is understood by interviewees as a supplement to the GES (or “patch”), which reinforces them precisely in the area of medicines. In fact, certain barriers or deficits (in terms of human and financial resources, etc.) were identified so that the guarantees could achieve their potential to improve access to medicines associated with the three very prevalent conditions covered by FOFAR. This is evidenced by the following interviewee: “FOFAR is how we take bits and pieces of the GES and leave it impeccable. How we move towards good management, good access, a place where the professionals are there as they should be, in other words, it is like curing the GES and leaving it like a jewel” (E5).

Subcategory 1 - GES and FOFAR mechanisms: in relation to the mechanisms through which improvements in access to medicines would be operationalized, interviewees mentioned the fact that they constitute enforceable rights, the strength offered by the Chilean primary health care (PHC), the fact that the same guarantees lead to the strengthening of other policies associated with access to medicines (e.g. bioequivalence, good manufacturing practices), and the way in which FOFAR came to strengthen the GES for better compliance in the three conditions studied (e.g. by incorporating pharmaceutical-chemists in primary care).“…there are very few countries, and the current influenza vaccination is proving it, that have a system like the one we have [regarding PHC coverage]” (E1).

Subcategory 2 - Barriers to GES impact: despite recognizing the positive impact of the GES on access to medicines for the prioritized health problems, as presented above, the analysis also recognizes that this impact could have been greater if some of the barriers identified had been addressed, such as the persistence of the view of medicines as a consumer good (medicines are bought at the pharmacy, in the shop; captured by the industry), the deficient allocation of resources (with greater emphasis on inpatient care), the rigidity of the GES updating process (studies required and steps prior to updating or incorporating new medicines into the guarantees), the fragmentation of the system (different institutions and departments with defined roles), and the logistical problems related to the medicines delivery process (stock-outs, waiting times, frequency of delivery).“And from the point of view of medicines, it seems that the same thing happens, it enters into a mercantilist, economic circuit, but not in the liberal sense, which can also fall into the same thing, but also in this separation that health is exercised by doctors and medical services have a scope that involves medicines but ends in the prescription and medicines are available in pharmacies and pharmacies are also regulated by a sanitary code, etc., but pharmacies are also seen as a trade” (E4).

“There is also a bias regarding the quality of medicines given for free vs. those bought at the pharmacy, in chains or branded, which is also an important issue” (E3).

“…or what I was telling you about the pharmacy [of a public facility], that people go to the pharmacy and they never attend to you or it takes a long time, or if they go and give you part of the medicines because there is not enough stock, then it means that the person has to come again” (E9).

Sub-Category 3 - Needs for Improvement: Closely related to the barriers identified in the previous sub-category, the analysis of the interviews revealed several areas that would require improvement, as well as suggestions as to what these improvements should be. The potential areas of intervention that would allow for better performance and thus better access to medicines are found in different areas, some more systemic and others closer to the medicines themselves. The areas identified are: medicines policy and regulation (e.g., deconcentration of medicines market), public procurement (greater and better use of market power), management and control of the conditions studied (to avoid reaching other diseases such as renal or cardiovascular failure), monitoring and evaluation of health policies (establishment of evaluation protocols from the design of policies and programs), the health technology assessment – HTA (strengthening and institutionalization of these processes), comprehensive health policies (starting from prevention, incorporating medicines, lifestyles, nutrition, etc.), and the improvement of drug coverage for the conditions studied and beyond (e.g. progress in improving drug coverage in a comprehensive manner). “I think it was a very good policy; I think it has to be evaluated, I think that in all kinds of things one can always improve, one has to optimize. I think that there are all the arguments and not only the arguments, I think that today there is enough material to be able to make a good evaluation” (E7).

“Or, that I reach the situation where I am so well, in quotation marks, practically in everything, that I can move to a complete national health plan. To a different system of coverage, which was what many people, remember that when GES started, there were many people who were against GES because of the discrimination it produced between pathologies and who always thought that there had to be a universal health plan. Maybe the time has come when we can move towards that, I don't know” (E6).

Integration of findings

From the interviews conducted, it was possible to confirm that the GES and FOFAR, since their design, have sought to contribute to improving access to medicines associated with the treatment of the conditions analyzed, insofar as they offer guaranteed coverage and incorporate medicines as part of social security.

According to the findings of this research, both policies have made a relevant contribution to this purpose, with significant increases in the coverage of use and control of the conditions, especially in the period between 2010 and 2017. Indeed, the difference-in-differences analysis shows significant effects for both medication use and effective control or coverage in the case of hypertension between 2010 and 2017 (using individuals with dyslipidemia but not hypertension or diabetes as the comparison group), and significant effects on medication use in the case of dyslipidemia for FONASA beneficiaries (using ISAPRE beneficiaries as the comparison group). These significant increases are in line with what was stated by the interviewees, insofar as in the period 2010 - 2017 FOFAR was implemented as a complementary support to the GES for the treatment with medicines of the conditions studied, which, according to what was stated, would have been justified due to the deficiencies in resources presented by primary care for the care of chronic health problems with high prevalence, such as diabetes and hypertension.

Several mechanisms were identified through which the contribution of the GES and FOFAR programs may have been operationalized to improve access to medicines for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Among the main mechanisms are the legal obligation associated with the GES guarantees, the strengthening of primary health care, the previously mentioned reinforcement provided by FOFAR, and the fact that, in the context of the implementation of these programs, the health system also had to reinforce other pharmaceutical policies—such as centralized drug procurement, bioequivalence requirements, and good manufacturing practices.

At the same time, it is acknowledged that, had certain barriers not been present, the contribution could have been even greater. These barriers include deficiencies in the distribution of resources, the rigidity of the GES update processes, and the persistent perception in Chilean society of medicines as consumer goods.

Finally, in order to ensure that the causal pathways perform better in the future in terms of their impact on access to medicines—beyond the achievements reported here—several areas for potential improvement were identified. These include, for example, pharmaceutical market regulation, the way medicines are purchased to meet coverage guarantees, and how the health system manages the conditions analyzed.

All in all, it is clear that it is essential, from the perspective of policy design and implementation, in this case related to guaranteed coverage, to have the necessary resources, both financial and human, at the level of care at which they will be addressed, and also with a logistical design and installed capacity that allows for their operationalization (for example, to avoid long waits or partial deliveries).

Discussion

Access to medicines continues to be a major public health problem, and several initiatives have been designed and implemented to contribute to the resolution of this problem. In general, high-income countries have made significant progress in integrating pharmaceuticals into the package of services provided by their health systems.37,38 In contrast, in many low- and middle-income countries, medicines continue to be predominantly financed out-of-pocket by individuals. In these contexts, policy discussions often center on the development of national essential medicines lists and the pursuit of strategies to advance toward universal coverage of these products.39–43 Evaluating policies aimed at improving access to essential medicines—or to treatments for the most pressing health needs—as well as identifying barriers to their effective implementation, becomes particularly relevant. Notable examples include the Remediar program in Argentina, 44 Farmácia Popular (also known as Health Has No Price) in Brazil, 45 and the GES and FOFAR programs in Chile, which are the focus of the present study. These experiences offer valuable insights and evidence to inform the design and implementation of similar policies across the region and potentially in other countries facing similar challenges.

Concerning the Chilean programs, the GES offers explicit guarantees to address, to date, 87 health problems, including different levels of intervention depending on the condition (diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, etc.); and the FOFAR offers guarantees for access to medicines for three conditions: hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia (the first two are also part of the GES). The results show that these policies, since their design, have sought to contribute to improving access to medicines associated with the treatment of the conditions analyzed, with FOFAR supplementing the GES in that purpose for these three specific conditions, and they have done so in a relevant way, with significant increases in both contact coverage and effective coverage of medications for diabetes and hypertension among beneficiaries of both the public and private health systems, especially in the 2010–2017 period. Furthermore, in the case of medications for dyslipidemia included in the FOFAR program for FONASA beneficiaries, this study has found a significant rise in contact coverage compared to ISAPRE beneficiaries. This latter finding is particularly encouraging, given the structural differences between the two subsystems, with the FONASA population being generally older, having fewer resources, and bearing a greater burden of disease. 46

Looking at the evolution of contact coverage and effective coverage or control of these three conditions separately, there is indeed an increase in both indicators over the period analyzed, especially between 2010 and 2017. Importantly, while descriptive analyses showed substantial improvements over time in both contact coverage and effective coverage, the relative effects estimated through the DID analyses were less consistent across outcomes, particularly for some diabetes outcomes. This distinction is relevant because DID models estimate changes relative to the comparison group rather than absolute temporal improvements alone. In this context, some of the observed improvements may also reflect system-wide improvements in chronic disease management, pharmaceutical access, and primary healthcare strengthening occurring during the study period. Additionally, supplementary analyses across survey suggest that some improvements may have occurred progressively over time, which is compatible with the gradual implementation and consolidation of GES and FOFAR rather than a single abrupt policy effect. Despite this, as of 2017, only around 60% of people aged 17 years and older with diabetes and hypertension were on medication, and only 48% of people with diabetes and 33% of people with hypertension had glycaemia and blood pressure levels within defined ranges. Similar results are presented by a study of the hypertension cascade (awareness, treatment, control), a serial cross-sectional study using the three available ENS surveys 21 and also a global study on changes in prevalence, rate of use and control of hypertension between 1990 and 2019. 22 In the case of dyslipidemia, meanwhile, medication use reaches only 32%, and control 17.8%. In other words, despite improvements, there is still much room for progress in access to medicines for these conditions, starting with awareness of the condition or previous diagnosis, without which it would be difficult to access treatment. The present study shows significant increases in this indicator in the period analyzed, especially for hypertension and diabetes, reaching 68.7% and 88.4% respectively in 2017; while in the case of dyslipidemia, this research shows that, as of 2017, awareness is only close to 50%. In this sense, it would be relevant to explore why people do not access diagnosis of their conditions, despite, for example, the existence of the Preventive Medicine Examination (EMP, by its acronym in Spanish). However, it is worth mentioning that although the EMP includes examinations for all three conditions, the coverage of this preventive examination is far from complete or close to the targets set. 47

On the other hand, it would be necessary to make progress in the use of medicines among those who still do not have access. While there is ample international evidence regarding the provision of financial coverage in the context of health insurance, or the provision of free medicines by the public sector, there is still a need to make progress in the use of medicines among those who still do not have access to them.48–50 It would be important to identify and characterize the population that, despite the coverage offered, does not have access to the necessary medicines (lack of access to diagnosis, distance, cultural aspects, etc.), so as to generate active search programs or other programs that bring the services closer to these populations. 51

Thus, there is a need to ensure that greater awareness translates into greater use, and this use, in turn, into better outcomes in terms of their control. Indeed, an OECD report on adherence to medicines for diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia notes that, among those patients who obtain their first prescription, only 50% to 70% take their medication regularly (i.e. at least 80% of the time), and that less than half of these patients continue to take their medication within two years of initial prescription. 52 The variation in the reported ranges would depend on differences in the population studied, the medications taken by the patients and the adherence measures used in the studies. 52 Some studies in the Chilean population, all in specific samples (one primary care center, one hospital), show that adherence to pharmacological treatment of hypertension could range between 32% and 54%,53–55 being higher in women than in men. 53 In the case of diabetes, adherence could be between 33.3% and 62.5%55–57 and for dyslipidemia, only one undergraduate thesis found an estimated adherence level of 33.3%. 55 From a research point of view, the development of studies that provide a clear picture of adherence quantification at the population level, representing different realities, as well as the determinants of adherence, would represent a valuable input for the development and strengthening of current programs.

FOFAR, with its adherence component, attempts to address this aspect, with the implementation of appointment management and adherence support messaging, and also with the incorporation of pharmaceutical-chemists (PCs), which was highlighted by interviewees in relation to the role of PCs in educating patients and the health team. From a policy point of view, progress in strengthening this area is essential. These findings also highlight that expanding contact coverage alone may not be sufficient to achieve optimal disease control. Effective coverage depends not only on access to medicines themselves, but also on adherence, continuity of care, treatment intensity, timely follow-up, lifestyle modifications, and broader social determinants of health. In this sense, the persistent gaps observed between medication use and disease control underscore the complexity of translating expanded pharmaceutical coverage into improved long-term health outcomes.

On the other hand, this gap between access to medicines and health outcomes may be due to another aspect highlighted by the interviewees, which corresponds to the comprehensiveness of the approach to chronic conditions. This means the recognition of medication as an important part of the treatment of this type of conditions. However, it is not the only one, being also relevant aspects associated with diet and exercise, accompaniment and follow-up by the health team, as well as empowering patients in relation to self-care, a key aspect recognized by the interviewees as to progress towards better outcomes.

Some of the needs for improvement identified in the interviews go beyond the area of medicines, although they still include them. These include strengthening the evaluation of health technologies to support the process of updating guarantees, and the need for a comprehensive health policy that starts with prevention, making it possible to avoid cases and complications (and also medium- and long-term costs to the health system), and not only focus on treatment once the health problems are already present. As these conditions correspond to risk factors for cardiovascular diseases, therefore, their appropriate treatment should result in reduced risk of heart attacks, strokes, heart failure, other complications, and ultimately also in deaths avoided. 58 In this context, it would be interesting for future studies to examine such as impact indicators, that might be affected by the improvement in the access to medicines for the analyzed conditions, going beyond measures of use or control, which are the focus of the present study.

Finally, from the point of view of the public policy cycle, it is urgent to advance in the establishment of evaluation protocols and information and monitoring systems for the processes, results and impacts of the policies implemented, which are designed in conjunction with the policy itself, and which include both analysis plans and the generation and collection of the necessary data for these purposes. The present study arises in a context in which there is no previously established monitoring or evaluation process, i.e. at the time of the design and implementation of the GES and FOFAR, the basis for evaluation and monitoring was not established. Thus, a key limitation of this study relates to the selection of the comparison group for the DID analysis. Due to the universal and simultaneous implementation of GES for hypertension and diabetes across both public and private insurers, identifying a completely untreated comparison group with similar characteristics was not feasible. As a pragmatic alternative, individuals diagnosed exclusively with dyslipidemia were used as a quasi-counterfactual group, given that dyslipidemia was managed within the same primary healthcare context but was not initially covered by GES during the main implementation period analyzed. Nevertheless, this comparison strategy has important limitations. Dyslipidemia differs from hypertension and diabetes in aspects such as disease trajectory, symptom presentation, and healthcare-seeking behavior. In particular, dyslipidemia is often asymptomatic and may be more frequently detected during routine preventive care, potentially resulting in differences in health literacy, healthcare engagement, or socioeconomic characteristics between groups. Furthermore, increased healthcare contact among individuals with hypertension or diabetes covered under GES may also have increased opportunities for dyslipidemia diagnosis and treatment over time, potentially attenuating differences between groups. To partially mitigate this issue, individuals with comorbid GES-covered conditions were excluded from the dyslipidemia comparison group.

Additionally, while the available survey waves did not allow a conventional formal assessment of pre-intervention parallel trends, supplementary placebo-type analyses using the intermediate survey wave suggest that some differential changes may have occurred progressively over time, which is compatible with gradual policy implementation and ongoing health-system strengthening processes. Accordingly, the DID findings should be interpreted cautiously as quasi-experimental population-level evidence rather than definitive causal estimates attributable exclusively to GES or FOFAR.

For its part, the ENS corresponds to a repeated cross-sectional survey rather than a longitudinal panel, which limits the assessment of individual-level trajectories over time and prevents full control of unobserved time-varying individual characteristics. However, repeated national health surveys provide valuable population-level evidence across key policy implementation periods and are commonly used to assess aggregate changes in health outcomes and healthcare access over time. 30 Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted as estimates of population-level changes associated with policy implementation periods within a broader context of health-system transformation.

Taken together, the findings suggest that GES and FOFAR were likely important contributors to improvements in contact coverage and effective coverage for chronic conditions in Chile, although these changes occurred alongside ongoing health-system strengthening efforts and should therefore be interpreted accordingly.
