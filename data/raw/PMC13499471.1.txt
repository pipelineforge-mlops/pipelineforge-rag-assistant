A rare case report of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome as a pulsatile neck mass with an internal carotid artery aneurysm

==============================
Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, also known as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome type IV, is a rare inherited connective tissue disorder caused by autosomal-dominant pathogenic variants in the COL3A1 gene. Defective type III collagen results in marked fragility of arteries and hollow organs, predisposing affected individuals to spontaneous aneurysms, dissections, and rupture. We report the case of a 35-year-old Bangladeshi woman who presented with a gradually enlarging right-sided pulsatile neck mass accompanied by intermittent headache. Physical examination revealed a soft pulsatile cervical swelling with an audible bruit, thin skin, and joint hypermobility. Doppler ultrasonography followed by computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated a large saccular aneurysm of the right internal carotid artery with additional aneurysmal involvement of the common hepatic and splenic arteries. Based on the characteristic clinical findings and multimodality imaging, a clinical diagnosis of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome was established despite the unavailability of genetic testing. The patient was managed conservatively and referred to a tertiary vascular center for specialist evaluation. This case highlights the value of a systematic, noninvasive imaging approach for the clinical diagnosis of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome in a resource-limited setting. The educational contribution of this report is limited to the diagnostic phase, as long-term follow-up data were unavailable.

Introduction

Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a heterogeneous group of inherited connective tissue disorders characterized by skin hyperextensibility, easy bruising, joint hypermobility, and tissue fragility [1,2]. According to the Villefranche classification, EDS comprises 6 major subtypes: classical, hypermobile, vascular, kyphoscoliosis, arthrochalasia, and dermatosparaxis [3]. Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS) is caused by pathogenic variants in the type III procollagen gene, COL3A1, leading to defective type III collagen and marked fragility of blood vessels and hollow organs [1,2]. This predisposes affected individuals to spontaneous arterial aneurysms, dissections, and ruptures, which are major causes of morbidity and premature mortality [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Mutations in COL3A1 weaken collagen fibrils and reduce vessel-wall integrity, while dysregulated transforming growth factor-β signaling may further contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling and vascular fragility [8]. vEDS accounts for approximately 5%-10% of EDS cases and has an estimated prevalence of 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 25,000 individuals [2,9,10]. Commonly involved vascular territories include the abdominal, renal, mesenteric, and carotid arteries [11].

While carotid artery involvement is a recognized manifestation of vEDS [2,4,11], extracranial internal carotid artery aneurysms presenting as a progressively enlarging pulsatile neck mass remain uncommon and may pose a diagnostic challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings [6,[11], [12], [13]]. In the present case, Doppler ultrasonography followed by computed tomography angiography (CTA) established the diagnosis of a large internal carotid artery aneurysm, while systematic abdominal vascular screening identified additional aneurysms involving the common hepatic and splenic arteries, supporting a clinical diagnosis of vEDS despite the unavailability of genetic testing [1,4,10]. Beyond the clinical presentation, this report emphasizes the educational value of a stepwise noninvasive imaging strategy, multidisciplinary decision-making, and comprehensive vascular assessment for early recognition and risk stratification of vEDS in settings with limited access to specialized vascular and genetic services [4,7,10,12].

Case presentation

A 35-year-old Bangladeshi woman presented with a gradually enlarging mass with intermittent pain and swelling on the right side of her neck and headache for several months. The headache was transient, stabbing in nature, localized to the right side of the head. She visited a local physician who prescribed her analgesics that provided her temporary relief, but her symptoms were not resolved completely. As the mass was enlarging, she came to the outpatient department of Dinajpur Medical College Hospital for better management. She reported no history of trauma, recent infections, or prior vascular interventions.

On local examination, a soft, pulsatile, well-defined, globular shaped, smooth surfaced, mobile mass which is not fixed with underlying structure and free overlying skin was noted on the right side of neck, medial to the sternocleidomastoid muscle aligning to thyroid cartilage. On palpation, the mass was mildly tender, pulsatile in nature which coincides with arterial pulsation and a subtle bruit was heard on auscultation. The mass does not move with swallowing or protrusion of the tongue. There was no palpable lymph node.

On physical examination, the patient was afebrile, with heart rate of 72 beats/min, blood pressure of 135/85 mmHg, and respiratory rate of 14 breaths/min and oxygen saturation of 98%. Her height was 5 feet 2 inches with a body mass index (BMI) of 27 kg/m2. She was alert, orientated and very cooperative. Her skin was thin without any bruise, petechiae or rash. On musculoskeletal system examination, joint hypermobility was noted, but no joint redness or swelling. Her cardiac, pulmonary, abdominal and neurological system examination revealed no abnormality. The patient did not report any nausea, vomiting or photophobia. She did not use tobacco, alcohol or any illicit drugs. She was nulliparous. There was no known positive history of any connective tissue disorder in her family.

Doppler ultrasonography of the neck demonstrated a large saccular aneurysm arising from the right internal carotid artery with turbulent flow within the aneurysm sac and a peripheral echogenic rim suggestive of mural thrombus formation. Subsequent contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) confirmed a smooth-walled saccular aneurysm of the right extracranial internal carotid artery measuring approximately 5.08 × 4.01 cm, without evidence of arterial dissection. Three-dimensional volume-rendered CTA demonstrated a prominent right-sided cervical aneurysmal mass, while axial and coronal CTA reformations confirmed the aneurysm's morphology and anatomical relationship to the right internal carotid artery (Fig. 1). Coronal CTA also demonstrated contrast opacification extending beyond the aneurysm into the distal internal carotid artery with visualization of the Circle of Willis, suggesting preserved distal internal carotid artery patency. A noncontrast CT scan of the head showed no acute intracranial abnormality. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, basic metabolic profile, thyroid function tests, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and routine urinalysis, were all within normal limits. Based on clinical findings and multimodality imaging demonstrating multifocal arterial involvement, a clinical diagnosis of vascular EDS was established. Although genetic testing was recommended to confirm the diagnosis, it was unavailable at the primary care center and was not performed because of financial constraints.

The patient was managed conservatively with analgesia, blood pressure control using metoprolol, and antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel). She was subsequently referred to a tertiary vascular center in Dhaka for specialist evaluation and consideration of definitive endovascular or surgical management because of the high risk of aneurysm rupture associated with the underlying connective tissue disorder. No follow-up data was available at the time of this report.

Discussion

Vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (vEDS) is the most severe subtype of Ehlers–Danlos syndrome because of the marked fragility of arteries and hollow organs resulting from pathogenic variants in COL3A1, which impair type III collagen synthesis [1,7,14]. The resulting reduction in arterial tensile strength predisposes patients to spontaneous aneurysm formation, dissection, rupture, pseudoaneurysm formation, carotid-cavernous fistulae, bowel perforation, and uterine rupture [14]. According to Germain, approximately 25% of affected individuals develop major complications before 20 years of age and nearly 80% by the age of 40, with a median life expectancy of approximately 50 years [14]. Minor trauma, invasive procedures, or even spontaneous events may precipitate catastrophic vascular complications because of increased arterial wall stress associated with reduced intima-media thickness [12]. Consequently, early recognition before a life-threatening vascular event remains one of the greatest clinical challenges in vEDS.

Current diagnostic recommendations are based on the 2017 International Classification of the Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes, which identifies a pathogenic COL3A1 variant as the definitive diagnostic criterion while recognizing several major clinical manifestations, including early arterial rupture, spontaneous sigmoid colon perforation, and third-trimester uterine rupture [1]. Minor features include thin translucent skin, easy bruising, characteristic facial appearance, acrogeria, spontaneous pneumothorax, congenital hip dislocation, clubfoot, and early-onset varicose veins [1]. Although molecular confirmation remains the diagnostic gold standard, clinical diagnosis is frequently necessary where genetic testing is unavailable [1,10]. In such settings, the practical clinical framework proposed by Oderich et al. remains valuable, requiring 2 of 4 major clinical features—thin translucent skin, arterial, bowel or uterine fragility, easy bruising, and characteristic facial appearance—to support the diagnosis [4]. Our patient fulfilled this clinical framework through skin thinning and multifocal arterial aneurysmal disease despite lacking a family history, which is consistent with the fact that approximately half of vEDS cases arise from de novo mutations [7].

The present case is clinically distinctive because the initial presentation was a progressively enlarging pulsatile neck mass rather than rupture, dissection, or stroke, which represent the more common modes of presentation. Systematic vascular imaging identified a large extracranial internal carotid artery aneurysm together with common hepatic artery aneurysm and splenic artery dilatation, establishing multifocal vascular involvement compatible with vEDS. Importantly, the diagnosis was achieved entirely through noninvasive imaging despite the absence of genetic confirmation, demonstrating a practical diagnostic pathway applicable to resource-limited healthcare settings.

The radiological differential diagnosis of a young patient presenting with a saccular extracranial carotid aneurysm includes several inherited and acquired vasculopathies. Fibromuscular dysplasia typically demonstrates alternating stenoses and dilatations producing the classic ``string-of-beads'' appearance, with predominantly fusiform rather than solitary saccular aneurysms and infrequent visceral arterial involvement [4,12]. Loeys-Dietz syndrome frequently demonstrates diffuse arterial tortuosity with predominant aortic root and ascending aortic aneurysms, often accompanied by craniofacial abnormalities such as hypertelorism or bifid uvula [7,12]. Marfan syndrome primarily affects the aortic root and ascending thoracic aorta, whereas extracranial carotid aneurysms are uncommon [7]. Infectious (mycotic) aneurysms generally demonstrate irregular aneurysm walls, surrounding inflammatory change, adjacent soft tissue edema, and elevated inflammatory markers [12]. In contrast, our patient demonstrated a smooth-walled saccular internal carotid artery aneurysm with mural thrombus, multifocal involvement of cervical and visceral arteries, preservation of normal vessel caliber between affected segments, absence of arterial tortuosity, and no imaging or laboratory evidence of inflammation, findings that strongly supported vEDS as the unifying diagnosis [1,4,7,12].

Management of carotid aneurysms in patients with vEDS remains particularly challenging because the underlying collagen defect substantially increases the risk of vessel injury during both open surgical and endovascular procedures [15,16]. No randomized controlled trials or formal consensus guidelines specifically address carotid aneurysm repair in vEDS; therefore, treatment decisions must be individualized according to aneurysm morphology, symptoms, rupture risk, and institutional expertise [4,15,16]. Open surgery carries considerable risk because fragile arterial tissue is prone to intraoperative tearing, uncontrollable hemorrhage, and anastomotic failure [4,7,15,16]. Although endovascular therapy is generally preferred, catheter-based interventions themselves may cause arterial dissection or rupture, and conventional angiography should therefore be reserved primarily for planned interventions rather than diagnosis [4,11,16]. Published reports illustrate individualized treatment strategies, including staged extracranial–intracranial bypass followed by endovascular internal carotid artery occlusion for giant aneurysms [6], as well as transvenous coil embolization for carotid-cavernous fistulae to minimize arterial trauma [11].

Because our patient's aneurysms were identified before rupture and she remained neurologically stable, conservative management represented an appropriate initial strategy consistent with current expert recommendations [4,10,15]. Blood pressure control with metoprolol was initiated to reduce arterial wall stress, while clopidogrel was prescribed because mural thrombus within the aneurysm increased the potential risk of thromboembolic complications [16]. Anticoagulation was avoided because of the underlying bleeding risk associated with vEDS. Referral to a tertiary vascular center was arranged for multidisciplinary evaluation, including vascular surgery, genetics, and cardiology, recognizing that definitive intervention should only be considered in specialized centers experienced in managing connective tissue disorders [7,10,15,16] .

Comprehensive vascular surveillance remains essential because vascular lesions may develop in multiple arterial territories over time. Periodic follow-up with CTA or MRA every 3-6 months is recommended to monitor aneurysm progression, while echocardiography every 3 years may identify associated cardiac manifestations [4,12,13]. Patients should avoid unnecessary invasive procedures whenever possible because catheter angiography, colonoscopy, and other interventions may precipitate vascular or bowel injury [4,14,17]. Lifestyle modification, genetic counselling, pregnancy counselling, constipation prevention, and avoidance of strenuous physical activity also constitute important components of long-term management [4,7,14,17]. Early diagnosis is particularly important in women of reproductive age because pregnancy is associated with a substantial risk of uterine rupture and maternal mortality, making timely counselling regarding contraception and family planning essential [4,14].

This case provides several practical educational messages. First, a gradually enlarging pulsatile neck mass may represent the initial manifestation of vEDS even in the absence of previous vascular catastrophe. Second, a stepwise imaging strategy beginning with Doppler ultrasonography followed by CT angiography and targeted abdominal vascular screening can establish a confident clinical diagnosis of vEDS without genetic testing in resource-limited settings. Finally, recognition of multifocal arterial involvement through systematic vascular screening can facilitate timely referral for specialist multidisciplinary management before catastrophic vascular complications occur.

Conclusion

vEDS poses significant diagnostic challenges, particularly in resource-limited settings where molecular confirmation may not be readily available. This case demonstrates that a systematic noninvasive imaging approach, combining Doppler ultrasonography, computed tomography angiography, and targeted abdominal vascular screening, can establish a confident clinical diagnosis based on characteristic connective tissue features and multifocal arterial involvement. The atypical presentation as a progressively enlarging pulsatile neck mass further emphasizes the need to consider vEDS in the differential diagnosis of unexplained cervical masses in young adults.

The educational contribution of this report is limited to the diagnostic phase, as the patient was referred to as specialist vascular evaluation and no follow-up data were available. Rather than demonstrating treatment outcomes, this case highlights a practical diagnostic framework that may facilitate early recognition, comprehensive vascular assessment, and timely referral for multidisciplinary management in similar resource-constrained settings.
