Brunner’s gland hamartoma in the setting of a duodenal diverticulum: A case report

Brunner’s gland hamartoma is a rare mass-forming lesion that primarily affects the proximal duodenum. These lesions are often detected incidentally on imaging but may manifest as gastrointestinal bleeding or obstructive symptoms depending on size and anatomical location. Diagnosis is achieved by a combination of imaging modalities and pathological analysis but remains challenging due to their nonspecific imaging characteristics and often nondiagnostic biopsies. We report the case of a 70-year-old female patient referred to our institution for further management of a known duodenal bulb mass. Imaging redemonstrated the mass in the superior aspect of the duodenal bulb with an associated large diverticulum. Considering the patient’s worsening obstructive symptoms, a pancreaticoduodenectomy was performed. A diagnosis of Brunner’s gland hamartoma was achieved on pathological analysis. We correlate this patient’s clinical presentation with the imaging findings and highlight important diagnostic concerns in the management of this rare duodenal lesion.

Introduction

Brunner’s glands are submucosal, mucin-producing glands of the proximal duodenum that function to produce fluids that protect the intestinal mucosa from gastric acid exposure [1]. Proliferative lesions of these glands range from diffuse hyperplasia to focal hamartomas. Hyperplasia is common, especially in the setting of peptic duodenitis. Brunner’s gland hamartomas, on the other hand, are rare, mass-forming lesions composed of elements (fibromuscular tissue, adipose tissue, and dilated and hyperplastic Brunner’s glands) that accounts for less than 1% of all primary small intestine tumors [2]. Although often asymptomatic and incidentally detected, larger lesions may present with gastrointestinal bleeding or obstructive symptoms, including gastric outlet obstruction [3]. Preoperative diagnosis can be challenging due to their submucosal location, frequent nonspecific endoscopic biopsies, and imaging overlap with other duodenal and periampullary neoplasms. Endoscopy, cross-sectional imaging such as computed tomography (CT), and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) play a critical role in lesion characterization, assessment of local extent, and guiding management [1]. We present the case of a 70-year-old female patient with a Brunner’s gland hamartoma arising in the duodenal bulb, associated with a sizable duodenal diverticulum, and discuss the imaging findings.

Case

A 70-year-old female patient with a past medical history of childhood grand mal seizures, Barrett’s esophagus, peptic ulcer disease, hypothyroidism, and cholecystectomy presented to another institution with a 1-year history of progressive postprandial bloating, early satiety, dysphagia, and regurgitation. Initial evaluation with esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) demonstrated a large duodenal nodule immediately distal to the pylorus, causing a partial gastric outlet obstruction. Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen demonstrated a 3.0 × 2.9 cm enhancing, heterogeneous, predominantly intraluminal polypoid mass centered in the first portion of the duodenum, without evidence of transmural invasion or distant metastatic disease. Additionally, a large duodenal diverticulum containing air and contrast material was identified but deemed to be anatomically unrelated to the mass.

Repeat evaluation with EGD and EUS confirmed a smooth, subepithelial mass within the duodenal bulb, arising from the submucosal layer, along with a large diverticulum in the second portion of the duodenum. EUS further characterized the lesion as a homogenous, isoechoic mass containing multiple small cysts and scattered hyperechoic foci, without evidence of locoregional lymphadenopathy or invasion. The initial endoscopic biopsy was nondiagnostic.

The patient was subsequently referred to our institution, where a new CT of the abdomen was performed using oral water contrast and a single intravenous bolus of iodinated contrast (100-120 mL; Omnipaque 350 mg I/mL, GE Healthcare), administered at a rate of 4-5 mL/s. Using abdominal aortic bolus tracking, arterial and venous phases were acquired approximately 30 seconds and 60-70 seconds after contrast injection, respectively. Axial images were reconstructed at a 0.75 mm slice thickness using a soft-tissue kernel, with coronal and sagittal multiplanar reformations generated. Additional 3-dimensional cinematic renderings (CR) were created on dedicated workstations (Syngo.via, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) to enhance depth perception and facilitate image interpretation. The repeated contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a 2.8 × 2.6 cm intraluminal mass within the first portion of the duodenum, alongside the known large duodenal diverticulum measuring 4.7 cm causing mass effect of the pancreas resulting in mild pancreatic ductal dilation (Fig. 1). The patient underwent comprehensive endoscopic evaluation at our tertiary referral center. Repeat EGD evaluation demonstrated a 1.3 × 3.0 cm submucosal lesion originating at the pylorus and extending along the superior wall of the duodenal bulb. The lesion appeared predominantly hypoechoic with internal anechoic cystic components. Based on its location, the lesion was deemed unsuitable for endoscopic resection by the treating endoscopist. Tissue sampling was performed via EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA). Histopathologic analysis demonstrated bland, glandular epithelium with scattered non-neoplastic neuroendocrine cells with positive INSM1, synaptophysin, and chromogranin staining. Given the patients worsening clinical course, including the new onset and progression of abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting in the setting of an indeterminate preoperative diagnosis, the patient underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure). Gross examination of the surgical specimen demonstrated a firm 3.0 × 2.5 × 1.8 cm submucosal lesion in the proximal duodenum, located 3.8 cm from the proximal resection margin. A separate 5 cm duodenal diverticulum was identified adjacent to the ampulla without communication with the lesion. Surgical pathology confirmed a Brunner’s gland hamartoma arising from the duodenal submucosa, characterized by hyperplastic lobules of mucin-producing glandular epithelium with focal mucin extrusion, associated xanthomatous inflammation, and minor components of benign fibromuscular and adipose tissue (Fig. 2). The surrounding duodenum demonstrated the diverticulum noted grossly, and the pancreas showed low-grade pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN). All surgical margins were negative, and all 10 resected lymph nodes showed no evidence of malignancy. The patient had an uncomplicated postoperative course and was discharged in stable condition.Fig. 2 Low magnification view (A) demonstrating the well-demarcated submucosal mass. Discussion

Brunner’s glands are mucin-secreting glands located within the submucosa of the duodenum, most prominently in the duodenal bulb, with a progressive decrease in size and number of glands in the distal duodenum [1]. Their primary function is to protect the duodenal mucosa from gastric acid through the secretion of alkaline mucin; additionally, they secrete pepsinogen and urogastrone, which contribute to the inhibition of gastric acid secretion in response to luminal acidity [1]. Proliferative lesions involving Brunner’s glands range from diffuse hyperplasia to focal hamartomas. Brunner’s gland hyperplasia tends to produce small lesions (<0.5 cm) that are confined to the duodenal mucosa and characterized by the increased number and size of lobules of mucin-secreting glands. Hamartomas are typically greater than 0.5 cm, and, as the name implies, often include other components typically found in the duodenum, such as fibromuscular connective tissue and fat [2]. Historically, these lesions were initially termed adenomas but are now more accurately classified as hamartomas as they are not encapsulated, contain native tissue elements, and lack cytologic atypia [1].

Brunner’s gland hamartomas are rare, accounting for approximately 5%-10% of benign duodenal tumors and less than 1% of all primary small intestinal tumors [2]. They most commonly occur in middle-aged individuals with a mean age of approximately 48 years, without a clear sex predilection, although cases have been reported across a wide age range [1,3]. Clinically, patients may present with gastrointestinal hemorrhage, obstructive symptoms, or may be asymptomatic with incidental detection [4].

These lesions typically range from 1-3 cm in size, although there have been reported cases of lesions measuring greater than 5 cm [1,2]. While small lesions are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on EGD, larger lesions may result in symptoms such as early satiety, postprandial fullness, gastrointestinal bleeding, or gastric outlet obstruction, necessitating intervention [3]. More specifically, a study evaluating 27 cases of Bruner’s gland hamartomas found that asymptomatic patients had a mean lesion size of 1.6 cm, while patients with obstructive and bleeding symptoms had mean sizes of 2.1 and 2.8 cm, respectively [4]. Furthermore, a study comparing the incidence of gastrointestinal obstruction between hyperplasia and hamartomas found that approximately 81% of patients presenting with obstructive symptoms had Brunner’s gland hamartomas, while the remaining cases were attributed to Brunner’s gland hyperplasia [5]. As in the present case, this patient’s progressive postprandial bloating, early satiety, and dysphagia were attributable to a large intraluminal mass within the duodenal bulb causing partial gastric outlet obstruction, consistent with previously reported symptomatic presentations of Brunner’s gland hamartomas.

The pathogenesis of Brunner’s gland hamartomas is not fully understood, but it has long been thought to be associated with chronic acid-related stimulation and peptic duodenitis. However, evidence supporting this relationship is controversial due to inconsistent findings. For example, a study analyzing 20 patients with confirmed Brunner’s gland hyperplasia or hamartoma found that only 45% of patients demonstrated hyperchlorhydria [6]. Reported associations include hyperacidity, Helicobacter pylori infection, chronic inflammation, chronic pancreatitis, and uremia [1,8]. Additionally, the frequent coexistence of duodenal ulcers and chronic gastric erosions further suggests that chronic acid-related irritation, rather than a true neoplastic processes, may contribute to their development [8]. Nonetheless, management attempts with acid-suppressive therapy have failed to eliminate or reverse these lesions [7].

The differential diagnosis of Brunner’s gland hamartoma is broad, and includes Brunner’s gland hyperplasia, adenomatous polyps, adenocarcinomas, carcinoid tumors, gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), leiomyomas, lipomas, and metastatic disease [9,10]. Imaging plays a key role in the evaluation of this entity and a myriad of imaging modalities are frequently employed. EGD allows for direct visualization and localization of the mass. However, lesions located on the posterior wall of the duodenal bulb, the transitional zone, and proximal descending duodenum are sometimes missed on EGD, and biopsy is frequently nondiagnostic or only reveals Brunner’s glands of uncertain significance [1]. EUS is valuable for characterizing the lesion’s layer of origin, internal architecture, and vascularity [1]. Typical EUS findings include involvement of the mucosal and submucosal layers, heterogeneous echogenicity with variable hypoechoic areas, and multiple internal cystic changes [1]. In our case, EUS demonstrated a subepithelial lesion with internal cystic component and scattered echogenic foci, findings that correlate with the underlying histologic features of dilated glands and mixed stromal elements.

CT plays a critical complementary role, particularly for defining lesion size, excluding extraluminal extension and metastasis, and delineating relationships with adjacent structures, including the pancreas, bile duct, and vessels [1]. On CT, Brunner’s gland hamartomas typically appear as well-circumscribed, intraluminal, polypoid masses arising from the duodenal bulb [11]. These lesions typically demonstrate heterogeneous attenuation reflecting a mixed histologic composition and may contain occasional intralesional fat components and internal cystic changes [12]. On unenhanced CT, lesions are typically isoattenuating relative to the adjacent bowel wall, while contrast-enhanced CT may demonstrate peripheral rim-like enhancement in the arterial phase with more homogeneous enhancement on delayed imaging, reflecting their mixed glandular and stromal composition [11]. Additional imaging considerations in the differential may include neuroendocrine tumors, ectopic pancreas, duplication cysts, and ampullary lesions. Neuroendocrine tumors and GISTs may demonstrate hyperenhancement and can present as subepithelial masses, whereas adenomas and adenocarcinomas more commonly manifest as mucosal-based lesions with associated wall thickening or invasive features. Ectopic pancreas may appear as a submucosal lesion in the proximal duodenum but typically demonstrates imaging characteristics similar to native pancreatic tissue. In contrast, lipomas exhibit macroscopic fat attenuation and duplication cysts demonstrate predominantly cystic imaging features, making these entities less likely in the present case. Ampullary lesions can also also considered; however, the lesion in this case originated from the proximal duodenum and was anatomically separate from the ampulla.

In the present case, CT demonstrated a heterogeneously enhancing intraluminal polypoid mass within the first portion of the duodenum without evidence of transmural invasion or metastatic disease, imaging features consistent with a benign submucosal process. The combination of a well-circumscribed submucosal duodenal mass with heterogeneous enhancement and internal cystic components favored a Brunner gland hamartoma over alternative considerations, although definitive diagnosis required histopathologic evaluation. Larger lesions may lead to secondary findings, including proximal bowel dilation, gastric outlet obstruction, or, less commonly, intussusception [12].

Notably, in this case, the presence of an additional large adjacent duodenal diverticulum resulted in mass effect on the pancreas with mild pancreatic duct dilation, representing a potential diagnostic confounder by mimicking pancreaticobiliary pathology. Recognition of the intraluminal origin and the lesion’s noninvasive imaging characteristics is therefore critical for narrowing the differential diagnosis. Additionally, the presence of intralesional fat attenuation within a duodenal polypoid lesion is a particularly helpful distinguishing feature from neoplastic processes [12]. Although duodenal diverticula are common and typically asymptomatic, the size and location of this diverticulum make it difficult to completely exclude a contributory role in the patient's symptoms. Nevertheless, the symptoms were thought to be primarily related to the Brunner’s gland hamartoma located in the first portion of the duodenum, which likely contributed to the clinical presentation consistent with a gastric outlet obstruction. The mild pancreatic duct dilation may have been related to the diverticulum, given its location and associated displacement of the adjacent pancreatic parenchyma.

Furthermore, there are many radiological features of Brunner’s gland hamartoma that overlap with other etiologies in the differential. Brunner’s gland hamartoma can mimic a GIST, demonstrating mixed growth patterns. GISTs most commonly occur in the stomach and small intestine, with only about 5% arising in the duodenum [13]. When the duodenum is involved, it is usually the second portion, whereas Brunner’s gland hamartomas typically arise in the proximal duodenum. Carcinoid tumors, on the other hand, are known to most frequently occur in the proximal duodenum (75%) much like Brunner’s gland hamartomas [14]. Ultimately, radiological features of Brunner’s gland hamartomas are nonspecific, and a definitive diagnosis requires pathological study.

Histologically, Brunner’s gland hamartomas consist of a disorganized proliferation of mucin-producing Brunner’s glands admixed with other elements normally found in the duodenum, including fibrous stroma, smooth muscle, and adipose tissue, often with cystic glandular dilation [2]. In our case, surgical pathology confirmed a benign submucosal lesion with mucinous glandular proliferation and associated xanthomatous inflammation. EUS-guided sampling can be limited by the deep submucosal location of these lesions; in this case, tissue sampling yielded nonspecific bland epithelium with scattered neuroendocrine cells, highlighting the limitations of endoscopic biopsy in establishing a definitive diagnosis. Immunohistochemically, these lesions characteristically express MUC6, a marker of pyloric and Brunner’s gland differentiation, while variable expression of gastric-type mucin MUC5AC reflects a metaplastic, non-neoplastic process driven by chronic mucosal injury and luminal acid exposure [3,15].

Management depends on symptomatology and lesion characteristics. Small, asymptomatic lesions may be observed, while symptomatic or larger lesions typically require resection to prevent complications such as bleeding or obstruction [2]. Endoscopic resection is increasingly favored due to its minimally invasive nature and favorable safety profile; however, feasibility depends on lesion size, morphology, and location.

Surgical resection, including pancreaticoduodenectomy, is generally reserved for large, symptomatic, or indeterminate lesions in which malignancy cannot be excluded [2]. Eng et al. reported a 29% false-negative rate for endoscopic biopsy, highlighting the risk of underdiagnosis [16]. In this case, the patient presented with persistent symptomatology related to gastric outlet obstruction secondary to the lesion. Endoscopic evaluation deemed the lesion unsuitable for endoscopic resection by the treating endoscopist. In addition, the presence of a large adjacent duodenal diverticulum contributed to the complexity of the case. Following multidisciplinary evaluation and discussion of management options, the patient elected to proceed with pancreaticoduodenectomy.
