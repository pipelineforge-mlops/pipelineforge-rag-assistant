Highlights

• Wounds could be strategic incubators for multidrug-resistant organisms.

• Over 80% of the Enterobacterales in wounds were Klebsiella spp. and E. coli.

• The highest level (>91%) of resistance was found against third-generation cephalosporins.

• About 25.7% were multidrug-resistant organisms, with some carrying blaKPC, blaOXA-48, and plasmid-mediated mcr-1 and mcr-2 genes.

• This study provided updated local surveillance data on priority antimicrobial resistance genes in wounds.

Objectives

Carbapenem- and colistin-resistant Enterobacterales remain an important challenge in the management of wound infections in resource-limited settings. This study determined the species, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) phenotypes, and genotypes of antimicrobial-resistant Enterobacterales, as well as factors associated with Enterobacterales-positive wounds in Ibadan, southwestern Nigeria.

Methods

Two hundred twenty-one nonrepetitive wound swabs and biopsies obtained from patients were processed to recover bacteria, which were identified using the API 20E system. AMR phenotypes and mechanisms were determined by the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method and polymerase chain reactions, respectively. Structured questionnaires were used to collect patients’ sociodemographic data and risk-factor data.

Results

One hundred seventy-five (79.2%) samples were culture-positive. Enterobacterales accounted for 42.3% of isolates (74 of 175), with Klebsiella spp (47.3%), Escherichia coli (32.4%), and Proteus mirabilis (20.3%) predominating. Of the 74 Enterobacterales, resistance was highest (>91%) against third-generation cephalosporins and amoxicillin-clavulanate, nalidixic acid (90.5%), gentamicin (81.1%), and imipenem (80%). About 25.7% (19 of 74) presented an MDR phenotype; the following resistance genes were detected: qnrB (84.2%), blaKPC (57.9%), aac(6’)-Ib (52.6%), blaOXA-48 (36.8%), qnrC (10.5%), mcr-2 (10.5%), qepA (5.3%), and mcr-1 (5.3%). The co-occurrence of carbapenemase and mcr genes indicates potential for highly drug-resistant strains. A significant association was observed between Enterobacterales wound infections and diabetes mellitus (odds ratio = 5.81; 95% confidence interval, 0.76-44.35; p = 0.034).

Conclusion

MDR-Enterobacterales account for about a quarter of the etiological agents of wound infections, with major implications for surgical site infections, as their extensive resistance profiles threaten the effectiveness of empirical therapy, increase the risk of treatment-refractory postoperative infections, and facilitate rapid hospital spread. The detection of Enterobacterales of critical priority carrying colistin- and carbapenem-resistance genes underscores the need for local genomic surveillance to monitor and control their spread; coordinated health system planning to ensure sustained access to effective antibiotics; strengthening infection prevention and control practices; improving antimicrobial stewardship; and implementing targeted patient-level risk-factor management.

Introduction

Globally, wound infections are a major contributor to human morbidity and mortality, posing a significant healthcare, productivity and financial burden on patients [1]. In resource-limited settings, these infections often prolong hospital stays, increase healthcare costs, and worsen patient outcomes, particularly when caused by multidrug-resistant organisms, with the burden highest in low- and middle-income countries where access to advanced wound care is limited [2]. The skin serves as a robust physical barrier that keeps pathogens, environmental elements, and physical damage at bay [3]. Its low pH, lipid-rich secretions, and resident microbiota create a hostile environment for potential pathogens [4]. Any disruption of this barrier, whether caused by trauma, surgery, burns, diabetic ulcers, or pressure sores, exposes underlying tissues and creates a warm, nutrient-rich environment that supports microbial colonization and proliferation [5].

The transition from colonization to established infection is influenced by multiple host and microbial factors, including wound type, depth, and location; the presence of necrotic tissue or foreign bodies; the adequacy of blood supply; and the host’s immune status [6]. However, the contribution of multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales to wound infections in Nigeria remains under-documented, creating gaps in treatment guidance and AMR surveillance. The virulence attributes (such as toxins, enzymes, and biofilms) and antimicrobial resistance profiles of colonizing bacteria further influence the severity and progression of wound infections [7,8]. Other relevant epidemiological risk factors include increasing age, comorbidities such as diabetes and peripheral vascular disease, surgical techniques, adherence to perioperative prophylaxis, hospitalization in intensive care units, and prolonged hospital stay [9,10].

Although wounds may harbor diverse microorganisms, the Enterobacterales species are most important in wound infections due to their high virulence potential and multidrug resistance, with Escherichia coli and Klebsiella spp being the most common (especially K. pneumoniae) [8,11]. In low-resource healthcare settings, inadequate infection prevention and control practices, limited hygiene infrastructure, overcrowding, and suboptimal wound care facilitate rapid transmission to severe conditions [12].

Antimicrobial resistance is recognized globally as one of the most significant public health challenges of the 21st century. Gram-negative organisms, particularly multidrug-resistant (MDR) Enterobacterales, are a critical concern due to their increasing resistance to last-line agents, including carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins [13]. The accumulation of multiple resistance determinants within single strains has resulted in the emergence of extensively drug-resistant and pandrug-resistant phenotypes, leaving clinicians with limited therapeutic options [14]. These challenges are especially severe in low- and middle-income countries, where diagnostic capacity is limited and over-the-counter access to antibiotics (including last-line ones) is available [15]. These contribute to inappropriate antibiotic use and accelerate the development of resistance.

Surgical site infections (SSIs) and wound infections represent major global health challenges due to their contribution to postoperative morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs [16]. They are frequently caused by multidrug-resistant organisms, which complicate treatment and worsen outcomes [17]. SSIs impair wound healing, elevate the risk of sepsis, and remain a leading cause of preventable surgical complications [18]. The burden of SSIs, including wound infections, is especially high in low-resource settings, where limited infection-control capacity and antimicrobial resistance amplify risks [19].

Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing bacteria are widespread in Nigeria, with a pooled prevalence of 18.3% [20]. Moreover, Nigeria had the highest number of individual studies on extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacteria in West Africa (17 out of 35) [20]. There is a paucity of molecular-based studies on carbapenem and colistin resistance in Enterobacterales in Nigeria, underscoring the rationale for the present study, which focuses on these antimicrobial resistance traits.

Considering the foregoing, this study determines the species diversity, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and the presence of resistance genes in Enterobacterales isolated from wound infections in Nigeria. It identifies clinical and sociodemographic risk factors associated with Enterobacterales-causing wound infections. The study aims to confirm and document current resistance patterns and key resistance genes in a defined Nigerian setting. This is a confirmatory, surveillance-oriented study that provides updated local AMR data on Enterobacterales-causing wound infections. These data are critical because antibiotic susceptibility patterns change rapidly, often rendering standard, empirical, or outdated treatment protocols ineffective. Regular surveillance enables healthcare providers to make informed decisions to curb the spread of multidrug-resistant organisms, particularly in settings such as surgical units and hospitals.

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Ibadan, the capital of Oyo State in Nigeria. By geographical area, Ibadan is one of Nigeria's largest cities. The estimated population of Ibadan was about 4,100,000 (https://worldpopulationreview.com/cities/nigeria/ibadan), and it lies in southwestern Nigeria at approximately 7°23′N and 3°53′E. The principal inhabitants are the Yoruba ethnic group.

Study population

The study population consisted of inpatients and outpatients attending selected health facilities (primary, secondary, and tertiary) in Ibadan who consented to participate and were not on antibiotic therapy at the time of sample collection. Participants were systematically recruited based on a wound infection, defined as purulent discharge or signs of inflammation (erythema, pain, tenderness, warmth, or induration) [21].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee, Ministry of Health, Oyo State, Nigeria (Ref. No: AD13/479/44130). The management of participating facilities granted permission to collect samples. Ethical principles were strictly observed, including obtaining informed consent from all respondents, ensuring the confidentiality of all information, and adhering to proper standards for specimen handling, storage, and disposal. Participant privacy was maintained by assigning identification numbers instead of names, with sample labels corresponding to anonymized demographic data. Informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. However, parents or legal guardians provided informed consent on behalf of their children or wards. All generated data were handled with the utmost confidentiality and analyzed anonymously. Samples and data collection from participants were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2024.

Data collection

A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to obtain sociodemographic data and information on risk factors and wound types.

Specimen collection, storage, and handling

Prior to sampling, the wound bed was prepared using Levine’s technique [22]. Briefly, visible surface exudates and contaminants were removed with sterile gauze moistened in normal saline. A sterile swab stick was then rotated over a 1 cm² area of the wound for 5 seconds, applying sufficient pressure to express fluid and bacteria from the wound tissue.

Collected wound swabs and biopsy samples were placed into 0.5 mL sterile normal saline for bacterial preservation and transported promptly to the Microbiology Laboratory and analyzed in batches within 4 hours of sample collection. Samples included burns, ulcers, open wounds, and postoperative wounds from various anatomical sites.

Laboratory analyses

Sample processing and culture

Wound swab samples were aseptically inoculated onto 5% blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar plates using sterile wire loops to ensure optimal bacterial recovery. The inoculated plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 hours to allow bacterial growth. After incubation, colonies displaying distinct morphological characteristics were carefully examined and subcultured onto fresh media to obtain pure isolates. Preliminary identification of isolates was based on colony morphology, Gram reaction, and results from standard biochemical tests, including catalase, coagulase, and oxidase. These preliminary assays provided an initial classification of isolates prior to confirmatory identification.

Bacterial identification

Presumptive isolates were further confirmed and identified to the species level using the API 20E identification kit (Oxoid) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The API 20E system comprises 20 standardized biochemical microtests that facilitate the identification of Enterobacterales and other Gram-negative bacteria based on their metabolic and enzymatic activity profiles. Following incubation, colorimetric changes were recorded and interpreted using the APIweb identification software/database, which generated a numerical profile corresponding to the most probable bacterial species.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method, in accordance with the 2024 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. The Oxoid antibiotic disks tested included gentamicin (GEN, 10 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 µg), cefixime (CFM, 5µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30µg), amoxicillin-clavulanate (AUG, 30 µg), cefuroxime (CRX, 30 µg), Imipenem (IPM, 10 µg), colistin (COL, 10 µg), nalidixic acid (NA, 30 µg), ofloxacin (OFX, 5 µg), ciprofloxacin (CPR, 5 µg) and levofloxacin (LEV, 15 µg). Antibiotic disks were placed at least 15 mm from the plate edge to prevent overlapping inhibition zones. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours, and inhibition zone diameters were measured. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain.

Bacterial DNA extraction and amplification of antimicrobial resistance genes

Genomic DNA from Enterobacterales isolates was extracted using a commercially prepared kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Clinical Enterobacterales isolates resistant to at least three antibiotic classes were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) [23] and selected for amplification and detection of AMR genes by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The oligonucleotides and PCR conditions used are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, following previously described protocols [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. PCR amplification was performed using a GeneAmp 9700 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems). Target genes were amplified using specific forward and reverse primers. Each reaction included a positive control (known target DNA) and a negative control (nuclease-free water) to validate results. The PCR reaction mixture (total volume 25 µL) consisted of 12.5 µL Taq 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 1 µL forward primer (10 µM), 1 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL DNA template, and 8.5 µL nuclease-free water.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 30 (IBM Corp, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics. Bivariate analysis was performed to assess the association between comorbidities and lifestyle factors and Enterobacterales-positive wound infections. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by comparing participants with and without each exposure. For variables with zero counts in one cell of the 2 × 2 contingency table, the Haldane-Anscombe correction (addition of 0.5 to each cell) was applied to permit estimation of ORs and CIs. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 221 wound samples, comprising 189 swabs and 32 biopsies, were collected from patients with various wound infections. The mean age of participants was 45 years (range: 13-87 years), with females representing the majority (125; 56.6%). Most participants were married (97; 43.9%), 68 (30.8%) were self-employed, and 95 (43%) had completed at least primary education (Supplementary Table S2). Of the 221 samples processed, 175 (79.2%) yielded bacterial growth, comprising monomicrobial (73.1%) and polymicrobial (26.9%) growth (Figure 1). Gram-negative organisms were predominant, accounting for 60.6% of the isolates, while Gram-positive organisms constituted 39.4% (Figure 1). Enterobacterales accounted for 42.3% (n = 74) of all isolates recovered (Figure 1). Among the Enterobacterales, Klebsiella spp (47.3%) was the most common, followed by E. coli (32.4%), P. mirabilis (20.3%) (Figure 2). Klebsiella spp (22.5%) and E. coli (11%) were the predominant organisms in open wounds, while Proteus mirabilis (8%) predominated in the postoperative wound samples (Figure 3). Discussion

This study has important implications for the management of SSIs. Enterobacterales—particularly Klebsiella spp., E. coli, and Proteus mirabilis—are well-recognized causes of postoperative wound infections [29], and the high levels of multidrug resistance observed in this study highlight a growing threat to effective SSI treatment.

The overall bacterial recovery rate from wound samples in this study was 79.2%, closely comparable to the 79.7% reported by Akoachere et al. [30]. This highlights the substantial burden of bacterial colonization and infection among patients with wounds in healthcare facilities across the study area. Usually, Enterobacterales are a significant cause of wound infections, particularly SSIs, with studies showing they account for 24% to 37% of cases [31,32]. Such a high isolation rate in our study suggests that wound infections remain a significant problem requiring urgent attention, especially in environments where infection prevention and control measures are suboptimal. The predominance of Klebsiella spp. and E. coli in the wound samples corroborated findings from previous similar studies worldwide [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. Diabetes is known to impair innate and adaptive immune responses, reduce tissue perfusion, delay wound healing, and increase susceptibility to bacterial colonization and infection [39]. Consequently, diabetic patients are at increased risk of developing chronic and difficult-to-treat wound infections [39].

The increasing prevalence of MDR-Enterobacterales significantly limits therapeutic options, complicates treatment outcomes, and contributes to prolonged morbidity. Approximately 25.7% of participants exhibited an MDR phenotype in the present study. This is lower than the prevalence of MDR-Enterobacteriaceae among wound patients in Malawi (36%), South Africa (38.1%), and Ethiopia (41%) [[35], [36], [37]]. The clinical implications of these are profound, as MDR bacteria are often refractory to first-line antibiotics, leading to persistent infections, prolonged hospitalization, and increased morbidity and mortality [40]. Their persistence in the wound environment not only impedes healing but also increases the risk of secondary infections and sepsis. These findings highlight the need for targeted antibiotic stewardship interventions to preserve the efficacy of remaining treatment options.

The high level of resistance observed among all the antibiotics in the group of third-generation cephalosporins and imipenem leads to treatment failures, increased patient mortality, more extended hospital stays, and higher healthcare costs, forcing reliance on last-resort antibiotics like carbapenems, which further fuels resistance, creating a cycle of poor outcomes, especially for wound infections in vulnerable populations like immunocompromised individuals, with the World Health Organization prioritizing new drugs for these resistant bugs [41,42].

The detection of mcr-1 (5.3%) and mcr-2 (10.5%) genes among Enterobacterales isolates in this study is of significant clinical and epidemiological concern. The emergence and spread of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance compromise the efficacy of this critical therapeutic option and mark a significant setback in the global effort to combat infections caused by MDR-Enterobacterales.

Its detection in this study, although at a relatively low prevalence (5.3%), underscores the potential for horizontal gene transfer and the dissemination of colistin resistance within hospital environments and the community. The persistence of mcr-1-carrying plasmids is facilitated by co-selection with other antimicrobial resistance determinants, driven by the indiscriminate use of antibiotics and colistin in livestock production and clinical settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [43,44].

Colistin susceptibility was determined by disk diffusion. However, all the colistin-resistant strains were further tested using PCR. While the study assessed factors associated with Enterobacterales-positive wounds, the variables collected were limited to patient sociodemographic characteristics and selected clinical risk factors, as specified in the study objectives. Variables such as time after surgery, severity of infection, and healthcare facility-related characteristics were not included in the study design; they could have provided additional insight into the association between these variables and Enterobacterales wound infection. Given the high rates of resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, many of these isolates may be ESBL producers. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that inclusion of ESBL phenotypic testing and their corresponding genes would have provided additional insights into the mechanisms of ESBL production present in these isolates. Whole-genome sequencing should be considered in future studies, as it provides detailed data on potential mobile genetic elements, transmission pathways, and relatedness among carbapenem- and colistin-resistant strains.

Conclusion

About 40% of the wound infections were caused by Enterobacterales, of which a quarter were MDR strains, complicating their management. Comorbidities significantly increase the risk of Enterobacterales wound infections. The detection of carbapenemase-producing and colistin-resistant strains, including isolates carrying blaKPC, blaOXA-48, and mcr genes, underscores the potential for treatment failure in postoperative patients who rely on these agents as last-line therapies.

These results emphasize the need for strengthened infection prevention and control practices in surgical units, improved perioperative antibiotic stewardship, and routine local surveillance to guide empirical therapy. By providing updated resistance data from a resource-limited setting, this study supports more informed clinical decision-making aimed at reducing SSI-related morbidity and improving patient outcomes.
