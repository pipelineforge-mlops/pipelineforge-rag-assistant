Splenic artery aneurysm with arteriovenous fistula is rare and technically challenging, particularly in the setting of marked tortuosity and high-flow shunting. We report the case of a 69-year-old woman treated with a fully endovascular approach guided by intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography. A dual microcatheter technique enabled targeted deployment of detachable coils followed by Onyx embolization, achieving complete fistula occlusion while preserving collateral splenic perfusion through short gastric arteries. The procedure was completed without complications, and the patient was discharged the following day. Advanced endovascular techniques offer a safe and effective alternative for complex visceral vascular pathology.

Splenic arteriovenous fistulas (SAVFs) are rare entities, with up to 86% of spontaneous cases occurring in women. In more than half of the cases, SAVFs are associated with a concomitant splenic artery aneurysm (SAA).1 Despite their low incidence, SAA rupture carries a high mortality rate (25%-75%).2 Current guidelines recommend repair of asymptomatic SAA above the size of 30 mm.3,4 Optimal management for SAVF and SAA remains uncertain, as comparative data are lacking.

We report the endovascular treatment of a patient presenting with SAVF associated with multiple SAAs. The patient consented to the use of deidentified images and case information as part of our institutional procedural consent process.

Case report

A 69-year-old woman with a history of cholelithiasis, and multiple pregnancies presented with significant elevation of her liver enzymes. She had no history of flank or upper left quadrant pain. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) scan demonstrated a severely tortuous enlarged splenic artery (10 mm) with a 3 × 2.5 cm mid-artery aneurysm. A large venous aneurysm measuring 3.5 × 2.6 cm was also visualized at the splenic hilum representing a high flow vascular malformation with tortuous outflow to the portal system (Fig 1). Given the aneurysm size and fistula flow, she was at significant risk of rupture and open surgical repair with splenectomy was recommended. However, the patient declined and an endovascular approach was pursued to preserve splenic perfusion.Fig 1 A, Preprocedural computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrating the anatomy of the splenic arteriovenous fistula (SAVF). The patient was taken to a hybrid operating room equipped with a robotic angiography system (Artis Pheno VE10B; Siemens Healthineers) for diagnostic angiography and possible endovascular treatment under general anesthesia. Under ultrasound guidance, the right common femoral artery was accessed and a 6F introducer sheath (Pinnacle, Terumo Medical Corp) was inserted. A Bentson wire (Cook Medical) was advanced into the aorta, and the micropuncture sheath was exchanged for an 8.5F steerable sheath (Aptus TourGuide; Medtronic). An aortogram was performed, and the celiac axis was catheterized with the steerable sheath to better define aneurysm morphology. Cone-beam CTA (CBCTA) was performed using a 4s digital subtraction angiography protocol after selective contrast injection into the celiac axis (Video 1, online only). Multiplanar and volume-rendered CBCTA reconstructions were reviewed. Inflow and outflow channels were marked with color-coded overlays to support endovascular road mapping; a technique commonly used in complex interventions (Fig 2). The splenic artery was catheterized with a Raptor 074 aspiration catheter (Balt Montmorency) and a RED 43 reperfusion catheter (Penumbra) which were advanced beyond the distal aneurysm into the venous outflow. Through this access, two microcatheters; an Access25 delivery microcatheter (Penumbra) and a Headway-17 microcatheter (MicroVention Terumo) were navigated into the distal splenic hilum aneurysm. Multiple Penumbra 400 and gen II SwiftPAC coils were deployed first (Video 2, online only) to scaffold subsequent Onyx embolization, aiming to eliminate early splenic venous filling while preserving collateral splenic perfusion. Onyx 24 (Medtronic) was then injected under fluoroscopic guidance into the aneurysm and into the arterial inflow (Fig 3) (Video 3, online only). Sequential angiography showed progressive shunt slowing and eventual occlusion of the fistula. Splenic perfusion was partially preserved through collateral short gastric arteries (Video 4, online only). Total procedure time was 208 minutes. Total fluoroscopy time was 22.5 minutes, with a total radiation exposure of 412.7 mGy. Mean radiation dose from the CBCTA scans was 77.87 mGy. She had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged home on postoperative day 1. There was a small infarct of the spleen with a small amount of Onyx extending into a peripheral intrasplenic arterial branch, however greater than 90% of the spleen appeared to be enhancing. The fusiform mid-artery aneurysm remained stable with a 2 cm diameter and is going to be monitored and evaluated overtime.

Discussion

SAVF may remain asymptomatic until arteriovenous shunting causes portal hypertension.5 Variceal bleeding is the most life-threatening complication in this setting.6 Early diagnosis and timely intervention are essential to prevent irreversible hepatic damage.

Multiple treatment strategies have been described for SAA and SAVF, including open surgical, laparoscopic, endovascular, and more recently robotic-assisted approaches.1 Open and laparoscopic techniques frequently accompanied by splenectomy, which carries lifelong risk of overwhelming postsplenectomy infection despite appropriate vaccination.7 Vaccination was not performed because embolization was selective, with postoperative CTA confirming substantial viable tissue and vaccination is generally reserved for cases in which functional asplenia is anticipated.8,9 Surgical management of SAVFs can be technically challenging due to the distal location of the lesion, dense adhesions, and the presence of extensive portal collaterals.10 Endovascular therapy has emerged as an increasingly used alternative, offering a less invasive and relatively low-risk approach.5,11, 12, 13 However, anatomical factors may limit certain techniques. In the present case, stent graft placement was not feasible due to marked splenic artery tortuosity and the presence of a high-flow fistula. Alternative strategies, including deployment of Amplatzer occlusion devices in the splenic artery, have been described.6 A recognized limitation of embolization is the risk of partial splenic infarction, particularly in distal lesions. In our case more than 90% of splenic perfusion was preserved through collateral circulation. Reintervention rates following endovascular treatment also remain a consideration.

Robotic-assisted surgery provides a minimally invasive approach with the advantage of long-term durability.14 However, current evidence is limited and largely restricted to small series.15,16 A robotic approach was not considered given the high-flow nature of the fistula and concerns regarding immediate vascular control during significant bleeding.

Navigation within a tortuous visceral anatomy is challenging. In this case, we combined intraoperative CBCTA with a triaxial catheter system to optimize visualization and access. Selective intra-arterial CBCTA provides better spatial resolution compared with intravenous CTA and a more detailed identification of morphology and branch vessels.17 Accurate delineation of inflow and outflow is critical, particularly before embolization, as postprocedural coil artifacts may obscure subsequent imaging.

The triaxial system has been described in complex endovascular interventions to improve support and distal access in tortuous anatomy. The system consists of a guide sheath, an intermediate catheter and a microcatheter with a guidewire, providing improved distal stability, controlled microcatheter movement, and reduced catheter prolapse. The ability to use dual microcatheters further enables controlled, staged embolization, and ballon-/stent-assisted coiling.18,19 In this case, the technique provided two major advantages: it allowed staged embolization through separate access channels and maintained stable distal access. Coils were first deployed to reduce shunt flow and create a scaffold, after which Onyx was injected in a more controlled fashion to occlude the residual high-flow channel. Advanced imaging and stable catheter support enabled aneurysm and fistula exclusion.

Conclusions

This case highlights the unique challenges of treating concomitant SAA and arteriovenous fistula, where vessel tortuosity, aneurysmal compression, and turbulent high-flow shunting often preclude the use of stent grafts. In such scenarios, transcatheter coil and liquid embolization represent an effective alternative, allowing targeted occlusion of both the aneurysm sac and fistula while reducing portal venous pressures and preventing life-threatening complications. This combined imaging and catheter-based strategy offers an effective solution for complex SAA-SAVF anatomy not fit for conventional endovascular or surgical approaches.
