Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) disrupts lipid metabolism, contributing to renal dysfunction seen in acute kidney injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN). While antiretroviral therapies (ARTs) slow viral replication, prolonged use causes lipid dysregulation, leading to nephrotoxicity. Lipid metabolism has been hypothesized to be altered through viral manipulation, altering the metabolism, storage, and physical properties of lipids. These manipulations have been observed to generate increased levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids, cholesterol/sphingomyelin domains, and pro-inflammatory phospholipid tails. Since the kidney is composed of a network of spatially distinct functional units, each with a unique physiological role and differential vulnerability to infection and drug toxicity, methods capable of preserving native molecular context are critical for identifying region-specific perturbations. In this paper, we investigate the lipidomic changes in simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV)-infected and ART-treated rhesus macaque kidneys using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI MSI). By using this approach, we can analyze discrete tissue functional units, such as glomeruli, proximal tubules, distal tubules, and collecting ducts, and gain insights into how viral infection and ART reshape lipid metabolism. Through this work, we further elaborate on the lipidomic changes in glomeruli specifically, identifying the critical role that sphingomyelins play in the context of viral fusion and budding, in addition to other phospholipids and their role in altered oxidative phosphorylation and inflammatory signaling.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is broadly characterized by its depletion of CD4+ T lymphocytes, leading to acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) after several years of infection. , In addition to targeting T lymphocytes, HIV also infects somatic cell types through CD4 receptors and the CCR5 and CXCR4 coreceptors, leading to widespread dysfunction in major organs. − In the kidney specifically, HIV is known to increase rates of acute kidney injury (AKI), HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Successful development of antiretroviral therapies (ART) aims to slow viral replication during the early stages of infection, extending the life expectancy of infected individuals. While ART drugs are successful in slowing viral effects, lifelong ART usage has been shown to be nephrotoxic and can cause or worsen conditions such as AKI, CKD, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. ,

HIV disrupts normal cellular processes by integrating into the host genome and reprogramming metabolic pathways to fuel its replication through multiple mechanisms of action. First, viral-driven reprogramming of host metabolism supports replication and membrane formation through increased levels of phospholipids, fatty acids, triglycerides, sterols, and acyl-carnitines, which are crucial for the assembly and budding of new viral particles. − Many of these lipids have been identified as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that increase the fluidity and curvature of the plasma membrane, helping to facilitate viral fusion. Elevated levels of PUFAs, such as sphingomyelin (SM), and membrane cholesterol (Chol) create lipid domains that provide the optimal environment for viral binding and fusion through the CD4 receptor complex. − Furthermore, following virion assembly, the viral Gag protein organizes similar lipid domains rich in SM, Chol, and glycolipids to initiate viral budding, capturing and incorporating portions of the host plasma membrane during viral release. , The composition of the viral capsid has been known to be enriched in SM, Chol, and phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2), with reduced levels of unsaturated phosphatidylinositol (PI) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) lipids that are typically abundant in the plasma membrane. ,

Second, the global increase in lipid abundance during HIV infection has been linked to viral manipulation of fatty acid synthesis pathways, in addition to lipid uptake and storage, similar to CKD. , Clinical studies have shown elevated levels of triglycerides in serum that serve as a substrate for lipases that generate free fatty acids (FFAs). CD36, a transporter of FFAs, is increased during infection, leading to increased import of FFAs and mitochondrial dysfunction and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ,, ROS are biologically deleterious, mutating DNA, modifying proteins, and disrupting critical signaling cascades, in addition to activating inflammatory pathways that can further oxidative stress and damage. Another key target of viral manipulation is sterol regulatory binding proteins (SRBPs). These proteins regulate cholesterol homeostasis and have been shown to be a primary target of the viral Nef protein, allowing for increased plasma membrane (PM) cholesterol and easier viral fusion through SM/Chol domains. Chol and intermediates in Chol synthesis have been another target, as physicians regularly prescribe statins to help regulate Chol levels.

In the context of the kidney, the mechanism of in vivo infection remains unclear, largely because renal cells lack the CD4 receptor complex. In vitro studies using podocytes and renal tubular epithelium (RTE) have shown how infected CD4+ T cells can extend tunneling nanotubes, allowing transmission of the virus. Infected macrophages are also a source of cell-to-cell infection for RTE. Once infected, Nef has been shown to induce cellular proliferation and dedifferentiation in podocytes, interacting with multiple signaling pathways, such as Src family tyrosine kinases, phospholipase C, mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), serine/threonine kinases, and p21-activated kinases. ,

Certain ART drugs, represented among common nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NTRIs), integrase inhibitors (INIs), and protease inhibitors, can mirror viral-induced effects, altering lipid metabolism, storage, and distribution. − Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) is a NTRI that has been used in many long-term studies, which has been correlated with long-term nephrotoxicity, tubular dysfunction, and CKD. , The proposed mechanism that contributes toward the cytotoxic effects relates to the depletion of and mitochondrial dysfunction within proximal tubular epithelial cells as a result of greater influx than efflux of the drug. At high plasma concentrations, these effects are exacerbated, leading to poor reabsorption of electrolytes and low molecular weight proteins.

The combined impact of viral manipulation and ART intervention leads to highly nuanced biological questions that integrate lipid metabolism and lipid handling at their core. Given the overall heterogeneity of the kidney, we hypothesize that these effects of dysregulations from viral interruption and off-target therapeutic effects will differ between the tissue functional units of the kidney (e.g., glomeruli, proximal tubules, etc.), as they each possess unique cell types that perform unique physiological roles. To accurately assess the change in lipid composition associated with viral infection, comparing relevant functional units to each other is critical. In order to achieve these comparisons, a spatially defined measurement is required to better provide insight into the adverse side effects of ART treatments, aiding in the design of better therapeutic strategies. −

Most of the literature exploring lipid dysregulation involves liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), where biomolecules are extracted from tissue, cell dissociates, or other biofluids and undergo separation prior to ionization, providing high-dimensional chemical data. − While informative, these studies are difficult to perform in a spatial context. For example, the substructures that compose an individual nephron are responsible for tubular absorption and have unique molecular profiles that reflect their unique functions. , One spatial biology tool capable of analyzing metabolites without disturbing their native spatial context is matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI MSI). In this approach, fresh-frozen tissue is coated with a UV-absorbing chemical matrix that facilitates desorption and ionization of cocrystallized biomolecules when ablated by a UV laser. − By rastering the laser across the tissue, spectra from each position can be used to build heatmaps of hundreds to thousands of endogenous molecules. Along with broad chemical detection, MALDI MSI offers high dynamic range and exceptional sensitivity. Recent technological advances have further enabled single-cell spatial resolution, allowing for molecular profiling of cellular networks that comprise complex histological structures.

In this study, we analyze the lipidomic changes in the kidney associated with simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) infection and subsequent ART treatment within a rhesus macaque model. Using MALDI MSI, we detect these lipid changes at 10 μm spatial resolution within the histological functional units composing the nephron. By analyzing these substructures, we gain insights into how the virus impacts the kidney, as well as its individual functional components. Additionally, we examine how ART drugs interact with specific regions of the nephron through their influence on lipid composition. MALDI MSI to identify phospholipid and sphingolipid biomarkers in other nephrotic conditions like AKI, bacterial infection, diabetes and obesity, and drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Similar to these cases, we hope to expand upon the HIV-relevant lipid biomarkers available for future studies to further elucidate biological mechanisms around viral or ART-related metabolic interruption, induced inflammation, and the myriad of other interwoven components that contribute to HIV-associated renal dysfunction.

Methods

SIV-Infected Rhesus Macaque Model

Animals and procedures in this study were approved by and in compliance with the Emory University Animal Care and Use Committees (IPROTO202300000032). Animal handling and euthanasia were conducted as stated under the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the USDA Animal Welfare Regulations. We used samples from a well-established SIV-infected rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) model, optimal for studying brain pathology, immunological compromise, viral replication, and the impact of therapeutics on the HIV outcome. − All rhesus macaques, approximately 2 to 3 years of age, used in this study were inoculated intravenously using SIVmac251 as previously described. , Two weeks after SIV infection, ART-treated rhesus macaques were treated daily with intraperitoneal injections of combined ARTs consisting of tenofovir (TFV; 20 mg/kg), dolutegravir (DTG; 2.5 mg/kg), and emtricitabine (FTC; 40 mg/kg) as previously administered. The ARTs were donated from Gilead (Foster City, CA) and ViiV Healthcare (Raleigh, NC). This model achieved peak viremia at 14 days postinoculation and accomplished complete viral suppression by day 42 postinoculation (28 days after ART initiation). All macaques were monitored to ensure that there were no signs of pain or distress. Although not necessary for these rhesus macaques, we followed criteria for humane euthanasia prior to the planned end point if any of the following were observed: (a) weight loss of greater than 15%, (b) CD4+ T lymphocyte count less than 5% of baseline level, (c) clinical signs of neurological disease, (d) intractable diarrhea, or (e) opportunistic infection.

Necropsy and Harvest

At approximately 130 days postinoculation, the juvenile SIV-infected, ART-treated rhesus macaques were euthanized in accordance with federal guidelines and institutional policies. Euthanasia occurred with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital while under ketamine sedation (15- to 20- mg/kg intramuscular injection) before perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco) to remove blood from tissues. ,,, The necropsy was performed in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines for euthanasia of animals. ,,, The fresh kidney was cut into four longitudinal sections. Kidney was fresh frozen using liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until cryosectioning, where optimal cutting temperature medium (OCT; Sakura Finetek Inc., Torrance, CA) was used to mount the tissue for cryosectioning.

MALDI MSI

Harvested kidney samples were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C before being cryosectioned. Samples were mounted using OCT prior to sectioning, taking caution that none of the collected sections contained residual OCT. Tissue was sectioned to a 10 μm thickness at −20 °C using a Leica Cryostat. The tissue was then thaw-mounted on an indium tin oxide-coated glass slide (Delta Technologies, Loveland, CO). The slide was thawed under vacuum for 30 min prior to matrix application. 2,5-dihydroxyacetophenone (DHAP; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO; 10 mg/mL in 70% ACN) was selected as the MALDI matrix due to its effectiveness for phospholipid and sphingolipid detection in both positive and negative ion modes. DHAP produces relatively homogeneous crystal morphology, enabling high spatial fidelity at 10 μm spatial resolution and minimizing matrix-related background in the m/z range analyzed. DHAP was sprayed over the tissue section using an M3+ automated aerosolized sprayer (HTX, Chapel Hill, NC). The sample was sprayed for 8 passes using N2 gas heated to 50 °C, a flow rate of 120 μL/min, a nozzle velocity of 1200 mm/min, a track spacing of 2.5 mm, and a drying time of 2 s. The matrix-coated slides were loaded into a Bruker timsTOF fleX (Bruker Scientific, Billerica, MA) mass spectrometer. Samples were imaged at 10 μm spatial resolution in both positive and negative ion modes for an m/z range of 500-1,850. Overall, a 160,000-pixel area of interest was chosen along the corticomedullary boundary within each sample as determined by H&E histological staining. Full instrument settings for positive ion and negative ion modes are provided in the SI Table 1.

Data Analysis and Statistics

All data visualization and statistics were performed in SCiLS Lab (version 2025b, Bruker Scientific, Billerica, MA) unless otherwise noted. Spectra were recalibrated using lipids known to be present in the sample (Positive Ion Mode: [PA(34:1)+Na]+, [PA(34:1)+K]+, [PC(32:0)+H]+ [PC(32:0)+Na]+, [PC(34:1)+H]+, [PC(34:2)+H]+, [PC(36:3)+H]+, [PC(36:2)+K]+, [PC(38:4)+K]+); (Negative Ion mode: [PA(34:1) - H]−, [PE­(36:1) - H]−, [PA(36:2) - H]−, [PS(36:1) - H]−, [PI(36:4) - H]−, [PI(38:4) - H]−, [SHexCer­(42:1;O3) - H]−). Spectra were then root-mean-square normalized, and ion features were selected using the T-ReX automated peak picking algorithm (SCiLS Lab, version 2025b). Average spectra were generated by aggregating signals across all pixels within each condition or segmented region to represent the overall lipid profile. For visualization purposes only, spectra displayed in figures were binned by using a uniform bin width of 10 ppm to improve clarity and readability. Putative lipid identities were assigned from the LIPID MAPS database, using a 5 ppm mass accuracy tolerance and subsequently confirmed using MS/MS (SI Figures 1–10). With the curated list of m/z values, a bisecting k-means clustering algorithm was used to analyze data and assign regions based on the mass spectra collected per pixel. After segmentation, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) scores were computed to compare the conditions across conditions. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using unit variance scaling to descriptively visualize variance, followed by two-tailed t tests to determine statistical significance of the most varying detected ions.

Results and Discussion

In this study, rhesus macaque kidneys from three treatment conditions were analyzed by MALDI MSI (Figure ). In sum, we compared the lipidomic profiles of SIV-infected animals (n = 3), SIV-infected animals treated with ART (n = 3), and uninfected control animals (n = 2) (SI Table 2). Overall, 120 lipids were detected in positive ion mode and 98 lipids were detected in negative mode SI Tables 3, 4). Individual ion images are available in the Supporting Information SI Figures 11–67). In brief, we detected lipids from the following lipid classes: phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylinositol (PI), sphingomyelin (SM), sulfatides (SHexCer), ceramides (Cer), hexosyl ceramides (HexCer), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), and diacylglycerols (DG).

PUFAs, such as PC(36:4) and PA(44:10), showed a significant increase in relative intensity compared to the uninfected control as a result of SIV infection (Figure c). These PUFAs, along with SM species like SM­(d36:1), play a crucial role in membrane fluidity and curvature, easing the fusion and budding of viral particles. Signaling lipids were observed to show a significant decrease at the global level. PI lipids, like PI(34:1), are central intermediates in signaling pathways like the PI3K/Akt pathway, which viruses use to modulate immune responses and evade macrophage degradation (Figure c). PS lipids, such as PS(38:5), play a role in signaling, contributing to viral-induced inflammation and can be a potential substrate for phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which releases arachidonic acid from one of the acyl tails, serving as a pro-inflammatory signaling intermediate.

Similarly, SM­(d36:1) is another lipid of interest in the context of chronic inflammation, serving as the substrate for nSMases, which hydrolyze SMs to Cer and PC components to further inflammation and oxidative stress. The kidney consists of distinct histological macrophage-mediated apoptosis, and facilitates lipid–protein interactions, like with phospholipase C (Figure c). , In addition to increasing membrane fluidity, PC(36:4) may be a molecule with unique function within each kidney functional unit, where each functional unit performs specific roles in tubular reabsorption, requiring unique molecules to perform these functions and enable unsupervised segmentation of MALDI MSI data into individual nephron components. We used a bisecting k-means algorithm to define regional boundaries around the glomeruli, proximal tubules, distal tubules, and collecting ducts (Figure ). In the context of segmenting MALDI MSI data, the spectra are recursively divided into clusters based on spectral similarities. Since each functional unit has a unique molecular profile, this algorithm is advantageous, leveraging spectral similarities rather than relying on optical segmentation. Validated lipid biomarkers from multimodal experiments, including immunohistochemistry of proteins specific to each functional unit, were used to accurately classify the segmented regions. The lipid distributions were then aligned with the segmented clusters described by the bisecting k-means algorithm to map them to their corresponding regions. PI(36:1), SM­(d34:1), SHexCer­(t40:0), and SM­(d42:1) were localized to the distal tubules, glomeruli, collecting ducts, and proximal tubules, respectively.

After segmentation, each region was analyzed for discriminating lipid features using ROC scores. , Here, the relative intensities and spatial distribution within each segmented region offer insights into the molecular features that are most altered by viral infection and subsequent ART administration. Intensities and ROC scores were compared in a pairwise fashion across conditions in each individual functional unit to allow for a broad view across the lipid class and degree of acyl tail unsaturation (Figure ). Broadly speaking, unsaturated PC and SM lipids were more abundant in every functional unit infected with SIV, regardless of the treatment status with respect to the uninfected control. PC and SM lipids are known markers of kidney injury, again pointing to the role these species play in inflammation, and did not show a significant decrease when ART was administered. Interestingly, HexCer­(d30:1) showed an increase in ART-treated PT and DT when compared to SIV-infected PT and DT, while HexCer­(d38:2) increased significantly in Glom and CD, but not in PT and DT (Figure a,b). PI and PS lipids were broadly unchanged across infection and treatment, with one exception being PI(36:1) in the collecting duct in SIV and ART cases, where there was a significant decrease in both treatment conditions (Figure ). Saturated lipid species, such as PC(32:0), were less affected compared with their unsaturated counterparts, perhaps indicating a housekeeping role (Figure ).

Lipids driving variation caused by SIV infection were SM­(d34:1), HexCer­(d30:1), and PC(38:4). SM­(d34:1) is a short-chain SM that is a known marker in AKI and is a substrate for nSMases, which drives inflammatory signaling. Furthermore, the short chain decreases its stability in SM/Chol domains, allowing for easier viral entry upon fusion with the CD4 receptor complex. HexCer­(d30:1) has been shown to accumulate in proximal tubules as a result of SIV inhibition of β-galactosidase. β-galactosidase is responsible for cleaving β-galactose from these kinds of ceramides to enable lysosomal recycling and membrane reconstruction. Inability to remove the sugar moiety from these further destabilizes the SM/Chol domains, making it easier for viral entry. Finally, PC(38:4) is a PUFA that is responsible for increasing membrane fluidity associated with viral budding and serves as a substrate for inflammatory signaling through PLA2.

ART-related lipids were PA(36:2), SM­(d36:1), and PC­(O-38:4) (Figure ). PA(36:2) is often associated with phospholipase D (PLD) activation from endoplasmic reticulum stress. PLD activation has been linked to mTOR inhibition that usually leads to autophagy or apoptosis. SM­(d36:1) is an intermediate-length-chain SM, indicating some recovery of catabolism, allowing for partially stabilized SM/Chol domains. Lastly, PC­(O-38:4) could be increased as a mechanism to combat inflammatory effects from ROS, with the ether linkage from the glycerol backbone being resistant to oxidation.

The overlap cases demonstrate similar observations and lipid species between the deficient/exploited species and species seen in the uninfected controls. Uninfected/ART lipids were described by longer intermediate chain length SM lipids and ROS-resistant ether-linked PC lipids. SIV/ART overlapping species significantly increased PUFAs, such as PE(44:10), which contains adrenic acid, that can act as a pro-inflammatory substrate promoting TNFα/IL-β-induced signaling that further drives oxidative stress. Conclusions

The HIV epidemic remains a persistent problem worldwide. While infection rates continue to fall with the development of new therapeutic strategies, there are still many unknowns regarding disruptions to metabolic homeostasis and the downstream health impact in people living with HIV. We demonstrate that the lipid composition is impacted by infection and ART. However, little is known about the mechanistic and regulatory consequences of altered lipid composition. Here, we utilized MALDI MSI to study the lipidomic changes occurring in rhesus macaque kidneys from SIV infection and ART treatment groups at the single-cell level. The resolution provided by MALDI MSI allows for k-means clustering, which was used to segment data into discrete histological functional units. PCA was then used to identify the lipids within glomeruli that were most variable in response to infection and subsequent treatment and indicated a heterogeneous molecular response within glomeruli. Short-chain SM species, HexCer lipids, and lipids containing precursors for inflammatory signaling were identified as driving the biological variance associated with SIV-infected and ART-treated animals. Additionally, increases in PUFAs were observed, enabling a more fluid and curved PM for viral fusion and viral budding. While in agreement with existing hypotheses, these findings underscore the complexity of lipid metabolism alterations in response to viral infections and therapeutic interventions. By capturing the spatial resolution of lipid distributions in different nephron regions, we provided a clearer picture of how SIV and ART impact the kidney at a cellular level. This work not only supports existing theories of lipid dysregulation in viral infections but also opens new avenues for exploring therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating metabolic disruptions and preserving kidney function in HIV patients. Further investigation into these lipidomic changes could lead to more targeted treatments that address both viral control and metabolic health.

Finally, we aim to leverage the power of MALDI MSI to analyze more complex scenarios faced by people living with HIV. Unfortunately, many individuals with HIV endure a myriad of comorbidities, which add to the challenges of managing the virus, which remains a reservoir in critical organs with incomplete ART penetration. Conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hepatic diseases, and substance dependence all contribute to the already complex metabolic alterations caused by HIV infection to renal function and across other organs. These comorbidities create additional layers of chemical complexity. We hope that by using the model demonstrated here, we can simulate more realistic scenarios for those living with HIV, ultimately helping physicians at the molecular level to guide their patients toward optimal outcomes.
