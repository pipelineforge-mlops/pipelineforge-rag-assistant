Background:

People with tuberculosis (TB) have a suicide risk up to 25 times higher than the general population. It may be due to stigma, isolation, prolonged treatment, and psychological distress from the disease. Hence, empowering healthcare workers (HCWs) who are involved in TB care with suicide prevention skills is essential.

Novelty:

This study will be integrating suicide prevention into TB care and will be evaluating an asynchronous learning management solution (LMS)-based training model against traditional in-person gatekeeper training for HCWs of high-risk population.

Objectives:

This study will examine the effectiveness of the gatekeeper suicide prevention training online e-module compared to in-person training for National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP) HCWs.

Methods:

HCWs in the Karnataka State NTEP program will be randomly assigned to two training groups using parallel cluster-randomization at the district level. The online training group (intervention group) will receive training through customized and locally adapted e-modules hosted on an LMS. The In-person training group (control group) will undergo training through presentations, role-play simulations, and discussions. Training effectiveness will be assessed using the Kirkpatrick model, with statistical evaluation and comparison of pre- and post-test scores to measure knowledge acquisition, skill retention, and application (number of referrals) across both groups.

Expected Outcome:

The study anticipates that e-module training will be as effective as in-person training for developing competence and confidence in suicide prevention and early identification and referral of suicidality.

Key Messages:

This article describes the protocol of a research project funded by the Indian Council of Medical Research that aims to address suicidality in tuberculosis (TB) patients.

The study will examine the effectiveness of training National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP) healthcare workers in suicide prevention through online and in-person gatekeeper training.

This training will enable early identification of suicide risk and timely referral to mental health services, thereby strengthening sustainable mental health support within routine TB care settings.

Suicide is a preventable cause of death, with India accounting for 19.8% of global suicide deaths. 1 In 2023, India reported 171,418 suicides (12.3 per lakh population), with 19% linked to medical conditions such as cancer (0.8%), mental illness (8.2%), and prolonged medical conditions (9.3%). 2 TB is one of the medical conditions associated with a higher rate of suicidality. 3

A noteworthy step towards suicide prevention so far has been the launch of the National Suicide Prevention Strategy (NSPS) of India (2022), which affirms the government’s commitment to suicide prevention and aims to reduce suicide-related mortality by 10% by 2030. 4 However, the same does not specifically address the intersection of TB and suicide, highlighting an urgent need for suicide prevention training tailored to people with TB and the healthcare providers managing their care.

To address the critical issue of suicide prevention among people with TB, it is essential to develop a capacity-building training program to raise awareness, improve help-seeking behaviors, and equip HCWs with the skills to identify individuals at risk.

Literature Review

The prevalence of suicide in people with TB ranges from 12.1% to 24.8% in South Africa and Ethiopia.3,5 As well as, findings from Russia (1980–2015) show a positive correlation between TB mortality and suicide rates in men. 6 A recent systematic review reported that suicidality among individuals with TB is associated with female gender, extrapulmonary and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), comorbid HIV infection, and depression.7,8 In India, data from the states of Karnataka, Kerala, and a recent meta-analysis estimated TB deaths due to suicide at 2.2%–8.4%, translating to 229–840 suicides per lakh population, which is 20–70 times higher than the general population.7,9 These findings highlight both the overwhelming burden and the need to prioritize suicide prevention in people with TB in India.

Among available capacity-building training methods, Gate Keeper Suicide Prevention Training (GKSPT) is considered the most effective approach to empower HCWs in identifying and addressing suicide risks, and is also endorsed by the WHO. 10 Technology integration could play a pivotal role in expanding GKSPT to reach HCWs in the community and ensure cost-effective delivery. Hence, this study evaluates the effectiveness of the online GKSPT e-module program (intervention group) compared to the in-person structured GKSPT program (control group) for HCWs.

Novelty

This study protocol will be novel by integrating suicide prevention into tuberculosis care in India and by establishing the effectiveness of an asynchronous LMS-based online training method, comparing it with traditional in-person gatekeeper training using a cluster-randomized design. The study will strengthen the field by combining capacity-building, clear referral pathways, and wider application of technology-based training in real-world settings.

Objectives

Primary Objective

To investigate the effectiveness of gatekeeper suicide prevention training (GKSPT) delivered via online e-module training compared to in-person training for healthcare workers (HCWs) of the National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP) in Karnataka.

Secondary Objective

To investigate the HCWs’ ability to screen for early suicide risk and the rate of timely referrals to mental health services, before and after training, across groups.

Methodology

Study Design

This study will be a parallel cluster-randomized non-inferiority-controlled trial that evaluates the effectiveness of online GKSPT (Intervention group) compared to in-person GKSPT (Control group) for participants (HCWs). The unit of randomization is the district. The districts of Karnataka, India, will be enumerated and stratified by urban-rural classification and district-level suicide rates to ensure balanced representation across intervention arms. Using a computer-generated random allocation sequence, the districts will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group receiving the online e-module training or the control group receiving the conventional in-person training. District will be communicated to implementation teams only after the allocation process is complete, to ensure concealment before intervention assignments. A district-level cluster design will be used to prevent overlap in training exposure among HCWs within the same district, as they frequently interact and work together. Figure 1 shows the randomization of GKSPT for KSTEP participants (HCWs).

Figure 1. Randomization of Gatekeeper Suicide Prevention Training (GKSPT) for KSTEP (Karnataka State Tuberculosis Elimination Program) Health Care Workers (HCWs).

The study investigators will enroll participants (HCWs). To ensure blinding during analysis, individual participant (HCW) data will be anonymized before analysis, and the remaining members of the research team will be blinded to group allocation. The training will involve minimal risk, and a formal independent Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) will not be established. Oversight will be maintained through periodic internal review meetings to monitor recruitment, training completion, follow- up assessments, and data integrity, in accordance with the institute’s ethics committee guidelines.

Interventional Group (Online Training):

Participants (HCWs) in this group will receive the Online GKSPT module, which will be customized and locally adapted to equip them with gatekeeper skills for suicide prevention. The training will be delivered through an interactive e-learning platform – learning management solution (LMS), allowing flexibility in engagement and self-paced learning. The training will consist of 10 modules, each featuring a 15–20-min infographic video. At the end of each module, learners must complete their corresponding assessments.

Control Group (In-person Training)

Participants (HCWs) in this group will undergo traditional face-to-face GKSPT training, conducted by the research team, following a structured curriculum. This method emphasizes direct interaction, role-playing, teaching through presentations, and in-person discussions to enhance learning outcomes in a single day.

Follow-up

Participants (HCWs) will be assessed for their suicide prevention skills using the gatekeeper behavioral scale (GBS), 11 and willingness to help, and changes in attitude will be measured using the willingness to intervene against suicide (WIS) scale, 12 before and after the training. One follow-up assessment will be conducted between 6 and 12 months after completion of the training program. We will collect the feedback from participants (HCWs) on the training via a Google Survey. The survey will assess various domains, including the usefulness of the training, applicability in clinical practice, new learning gained, relevance of the content, clarity of presentation, and overall usefulness of the program. Feedback about the training will be obtained through online feedback forms and interviews. All these procedures will be used in lieu of the Kirkpatrick Model,13,14 to assess the effectiveness of a training program. Participants (HCWs) who do not complete assigned training components or fail to attend post-training assessments will be considered non-completers. Those who do not respond to follow-up assessments at 6 months or 1 year, despite three documented contact attempts, will be classified as lost to follow-up. Reasons for withdrawal or attrition will be recorded where available.

Study Participants (HCWs)

Eligibility Criteria

The HCWs, will include: (a) TB Home Visitors (TBHVs) who support TB control by raising community awareness, identifying TB cases, linking patients to support, and regularly following up to ensure treatment adherence; (b) Senior Technical Supervisors (STS) who manage patient care throughout treatment, coordinate with laboratories, and provide home-based health and nutrition counselling; and (c) Senior TB Laboratory Supervisors (STLS) who oversee rapid DNA testing centers, ensure quality control and proper sample handling, have the maximum direct interaction with people with TB. Only participants (HCWs) who consent to participate and complete the training program will undergo the training.

Inclusion Criteria

The HCWs aged 18 years or older, regardless of gender, will be included. Participants (HCWs) who have been involved in TB care for at least the last 6 months under the KSTEP program.

Exclusion Criteria

Participants (HCWs) who have not been involved in TB patient care for at least 6 months under the KSTEP program or who are unwilling to consent to the study will be excluded.

Setting

National Tuberculosis Elimination Program, Karnataka.

Sample Size

A district-level cluster-randomized design will be used, with 31 clusters randomized equally to online or in-person training (16 and 15 districts, respectively). Each cluster will include approximately 15–20 participants (HCWs), yielding an anticipated sample of 450–620 participants (HCWs). Assuming an intra-cluster correlation coefficient of 0.02 and allowing for 10% attrition, the final expected sample size will be approximately 500–700 participants (HCWs). This sample will be sufficient to detect a moderate intervention effect with 80% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05 using cluster-adjusted analysis. The sample size calculation is based on detecting a moderate difference in training-related gatekeeper skills between intervention groups, as measured by validated self-efficacy scales such as the GBS 11 and the WIS scale, 12 administered pre- and post-training.

Implementation Plan

This study will be carried out in three phases over 3 years.

Phase 1: Development and Validation of Suicide Prevention Training Material (For In-person & Online E-module)

The NIMHANS research team will review existing suicide prevention materials and prepare a culturally sensitive, locally adaptable, context-specific, and customized GKSPT module for NTEP participants (HCWs) in manual form. Expert opinions will be sought to validate the content and the accuracy of end-user feedback. All training materials, including the manual, infographic videos, and presentations for offline training sessions, will be translated into Kannada, the local language. Feedback on the sessions will be collected at the end through forms and interviews. This material will be used for both in-person group training and the development of online e-module infographic videos delivered via LMS.

Online Training Materials

Online suicide prevention training will be delivered asynchronously via LMS, allowing participants (HCWs) to access content at their convenience. The module will include infographic videos, quizzes, and reading materials, enabling participants (HCWs) to learn at their own pace. Trainers will use the LMS to upload content, manage learners, track progress, deliver assessments, and provide and receive feedback.

Learning Management Solution

The online training program will be delivered through the “Kshya Jeevan Raksha” learning management system (LMS), an independent website hosted on the institute server and developed in collaboration with the project’s technical partner (https://jeevanraksha.nimhans.ac.in/). The LMS will be made compatible with desktops and Android/iOS devices. Sensitization sessions will be organized to familiarize HCWs with the LMS interface. During these sessions, participants (HCWs) will receive step-by-step guidance on registering on the platform, logging in, completing their profiles with demographic details, and providing informed consent. They will also learn how to navigate the website, access and complete the learning modules, and take the required pre-tests and post-tests. Participants (HCWs) will be guided through completing each module and assessment in the LMS. Figure 2 shows the development of GKSPT Modules.

Figure 2. Development of Gatekeeper Suicide Prevention Training (GKSPT) Modules.

Phase 2: Training of KSTEP Participants (HCWs)

NTEP participants (HCWs) will be invited to participate in the training program via state-level NTEP administrative teams and District TB Officers through official program meetings and departmental communication channels. Study investigators, in coordination with district authorities, will oversee the enrollment process. Only those who provide informed consent will be enrolled in the study, and an honorarium (₹300 INR) will be provided. Figure 3 shows the registration and hands-on training of participants (HCWs).

Figure 3. Registration and Hands-on Training of Healthcare Workers.

Phase 3: Screening and Assessment

The evaluation of the GKSPT training program will follow the Kirkpatrick Model,13,14 assessing its impact at four levels.

At the “Reaction” level, the study will measure participant (HCW) satisfaction and engagement by conducting post-training surveys to understand how HCWs perceive the training. Additionally, the study will be collecting feedback through interviews to gain deeper insights into their experiences and identify areas for improvement. To assess “Learning,” the study will administer pre- and post-training Gatekeeper behavior Scale (GBS) assessments to evaluate improvements in suicide risk identification skills. For “Behavior,” the study will be examining whether participants (HCWs) are applying their training in practice using the WIS questionnaire and by following up with them at 6 months. These follow-ups will help assess how they implement the training in their interactions with people with TB. Follow-up assessments at 6 months and 1 year will be conducted electronically via the LMS platform by the research team.

At the “Results” level, the study will be measuring the impact of the training on suicide prevention. Participants (HCWs) who trained in GKSPT will screen people with TB from Karnataka enrolled in the Ni-kshay platform for suicidality at the initiation of treatment, every 3 months during treatment, and upon completion of treatment. Ni-kshay is a web-based, patient-centric digital platform developed by the Government of India under the NTEP. It is used for real-time notification, monitoring, and management of people with TB across public and private sectors. Ni-kshay facilitates treatment adherence and outcome tracking to support India’s goal of eliminating TB. High-risk patients identified through suicidality screening will be referred to the respective District Mental Health Program (DMHP) center or general hospital psychiatric units for further care, facilitated through resource mapping. The number of high suicide risk cases identified by trained participants (HCWs) before and after the training, and the patient referrals made, will be recorded. Gatekeeper Behavior Scale

A simple, self-administered tool designed to assess self-efficacy in gatekeeper skills training. It demonstrates strong internal consistency and convergent validity. 11

WIS Questionnaire

A tool used to measure individuals’ readiness to intervene in suicide prevention scenarios. 12

A brief screening tool will be developed as part of this study, using existing tools,15,16 to assess suicidality among people with TB in community settings by participants (HCWs). Relevant questions from existing screening tools will be reviewed and customized to suit community settings and be easily administered by participants (HCWs). The customized tool will then undergo expert validation and further adaptation to ensure its effectiveness in screening for TB in the community. The screening tool will undergo pilot testing among a small group of participants (HCWs) before full implementation to check the feasibility.

Ethics Review

The study protocol has been approved by the NIMHANS Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No.: NIMHANS/EC (BEH.SC.DIV.) MEETNG/2023). The study is a collaborative initiative involving three partnering institutions, each of which has obtained formal administrative approval from its respective organizational heads. The project has also received oversight and approval from the Mission Director of the National Health Mission (NHM) of the Government of Karnataka.

All procedures will be conducted in accordance with established ethical standards. Participation of HCWs in the training program, including pre- and post-training assessments, will be entirely voluntary. Informed consent will be obtained electronically via the LMS before participation in either the in-person or online training arms. The electronic consent process will provide detailed information regarding the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality measures, and the participants’ (HCWs’) right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Participants (HCWs) will have the opportunity to contact designated study investigators for clarification before providing consent.

Potential risks are minimal and may include time commitment, technical difficulties related to the LMS platform, and possible emotional discomfort while responding to questions related to suicide prevention. Anticipated benefits include the acquisition of gatekeeper skills for suicide prevention and the contribution to improved integration of mental health support within TB care. Data confidentiality will be ensured through anonymization and secure storage of participant (HCW) information throughout the study.

As this is a minimal-risk educational intervention, no additional ancillary or post-trial care provisions are required beyond routine healthcare services. Participants (HCWs) experiencing distress during training or assessment will be provided with appropriate referral information. Any significant protocol modifications will be submitted to the Institutional Ethics Committee for approval and will be updated in the trial registry. Relevant stakeholders and participating sites will be informed of approved amendments.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis Plan

Descriptive analyses will compare baseline characteristics of participants (HCWs) who did and did not complete outcome assessments. The primary outcome variable will be the change in training-related gatekeeper skills between intervention groups, measured using validated self-efficacy scales administered pre- and post-training. Secondary outcome variables will include willingness-to-intervene scores (continuous), the proportion of people with TB screened for suicidality (percentage), and the proportion of high-risk individuals referred to mental health services (percentage). This study will analyze data using a paired t-test for pre- and post-test comparisons. At the same time, longitudinal changes at 6 months post-training will be assessed through repeated measures analysis of variance (RMANOVA) or linear mixed-effects models (LMM), depending on data characteristics and assumptions, to evaluate the effectiveness of GKSPT among participants (HCWs) in terms of knowledge retention, skill acquisition, attitude change, and the prevalence of suicidality screening among people with TB. Additionally, the suicidality screening and detection by participants (HCWs) will be examined using trend analysis to identify changes over time, particularly in response to the training. The impact of the training will also be evaluated by correlating improvements in participants’ (HCWs’) competencies with the prevalence and effectiveness of suicidality screening, providing a comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s short- and long-term outcomes. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, and statistical significance will be set at a two-sided alpha of 0.05. Missing outcome data will be assessed for patterns and extent. Where appropriate, mixed-effects modeling will be used. Sensitivity analyses may be conducted to assess the robustness of findings to missing data. No formal interim analyses or early stopping rules are planned, given the minimal risk of the educational intervention. The findings will be reported in accordance with the CONSORT Trial flow chart. A structured proforma will capture suicidal risk identification rate, referral rates, and referral patterns before and after training across groups. No biological samples will be collected as part of this study. Data will be stored on a secure server, on devices, and in RedCap.

Timelines

The study will be conducted over 36 months. Phase 1 of developing training materials and the LMS system will be in the first 9 to 18 months. Phase 2 of training will take place between months 18 and 30. In Phase 3 (the last 6 months), re-assessment of training, along with data cleaning, statistical analysis, and a summary report, will be conducted. Preparation of manuscripts, dissemination of findings to stakeholders, and submission for publication will be undertaken following completion of data analysis.

Discussion

The primary objective is to compare the effectiveness of online and in-person GKSPT modalities; therefore, measuring change in gatekeeper competencies using validated self-efficacy instruments provides a proximal, quantifiable indicator of training impact. Improvements in knowledge, confidence, and perceived preparedness are recognized precursors to behavioral change in suicide prevention frameworks. However, competency gains alone do not guarantee translation into clinical practice. For this reason, the inclusion of secondary outcomes, specifically suicide risk screening rates and referral patterns, strengthens the evaluation by assessing real-world behavioral implementation within routine TB care. This dual-level measurement approach enhances ecological validity by linking training outcomes to service delivery indicators. Nevertheless, reliance on self-reported scales may introduce response bias, and district-level contextual variability within a cluster-randomized design may influence effect estimates. While clustering reduces intervention spillover between districts and enhances pragmatic relevance, it may also introduce heterogeneity due to differences in local system capacity. The incorporation of longitudinal follow-up further strengthens the design by allowing the assessment of retention and the sustainability of behavioral change beyond immediate post-training effects. Collectively, these methodological considerations position the study as a rigorous yet pragmatic evaluation of integrating suicide prevention into public health systems.

India bears the highest burden of TB globally, accounting for approximately 26% of the world’s TB cases and an estimated 2.8 million cases annually. The mortality associated with TB in India stands at 3,23,200 deaths per year, and suicide is emerging as one of the contributing causes of death in this population.17,18,19 The rate of suicide among TB patients is 2.2%–8.4% of all TB deaths and significantly higher than that of the general population. 18 Information derived from the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) data, Ni-kshay records, and previous studies highlights the significant risk of suicide in this population.2,21 The elevated risk of suicide arises from multiple factors, including the direct impact of TB itself due to its chronic nature, coexisting medical conditions, prolonged treatment regimens, and neuropsychiatric side effects of anti-TB medications. Additionally, associated mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, and psychosis—exacerbated by stigma, financial strain, and psychosocial stressors—further contribute to this risk. 22

Research from high-income countries underscores the need for targeted interventions to address social isolation, stigma, and psychological distress. 23 However, in low- and middle-income countries like India, where the TB burden is disproportionately high, a critical gap remains in addressing mental health, substance use disorders, and suicidality among people with TB, as current research and interventions predominantly focus on a biomedical model emphasizing TB pharmacotherapy with insufficient attention given to the mental health vulnerabilities. A holistic and patient-centered approach to TB care is essential, requiring suicide prevention efforts that involve understanding patients’ unique challenges and implementing context-specific interventions. Ground-level action plans should be enhancing participants’ (HCWs’) capacity to screen for mental health, substance use, and suicide risk, strengthening referral systems, and integrating mental health and substance use services—such as linking patients with the DMHP to reduce suicide risk among people with TB. This can be achieved through gatekeeper training programs and the use of innovative tools like a Suicide Risk Assessment app, which can assess suicide risk levels and recommend targeted interventions. Testing such tools in real-world settings can provide insights into their scalability and effectiveness.

Under the NTEP, aligning suicide prevention strategies with the National Suicide Prevention Strategy is imperative. 24 Online training programs for participants (HCWs), supported by skill certification and incentives, can enhance engagement and empower them to address suicide risks effectively. The findings of our study could highlight the innovative use of technology, such as LMS, to improve accessibility and empower participants (HCWs). By integrating suicide prevention into TB care, it would address a crucial gap and provide a scalable model for ground-level interventions. Future efforts should focus on evaluating the impact of these tools and strategies, optimizing online and in-person training formats, and ensuring sustained engagement through structured incentives and support mechanisms. By tackling the dual challenges of TB and suicide, the integrated approach would not only enhance patient care but also support India’s commitment to public health improvement under the NTEP and NSPS. The findings potentially have broader implications as the integrated approach discussed can be expanded to address suicidality in other high-risk conditions, such as HIV, cancer, and non-communicable diseases. Programs like the National AIDS Control Program (NACP) and the National Cancer Control Program (NCCP) may benefit from adopting similar models, tailored for local languages to achieve wider reach.

Expected Strengths and Limitations

This study will examine the effectiveness of character-based infographic videos in enhancing engagement and interactivity in online training, making it a viable alternative to traditional offline training. If demonstrated to be comparable in effectiveness, the study could support the wider implementation of digital learning, which would be both cost-efficient and convenient. As a state-wide initiative, the program could be easily adapted for other regions through minor modifications, such as language translations, allowing integration into other states’ NTEP for people with TB and HCWs. The study employs validated and reliable assessment tools to measure knowledge retention and application through pre- and post-training evaluations. Additionally, app-based referrals documented during the study will offer valuable insights into how the training is applied in real-world settings. A subset of this suicide prevention training could also be integrated into other national programs that address persons at high suicide risk, such as the National Program for Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD).

Variability in digital literacy, internet access, and engagement levels may affect learning outcomes. The use of self-reported measures may introduce the risk of response bias. Real-world knowledge application, assessed through app-based referrals, may be influenced by external factors such as workplace dynamics and resource availability. While the program is adaptable, differences in healthcare infrastructure and cultural contexts may affect scalability.

Expected Outcomes

We hypothesize that both training methods will enhance early identification and referrals for at-risk patients, with participants (HCWs) demonstrating increased willingness to intervene during a suicide attempt. Furthermore, we expect the outcomes of the online training group to be comparable to those of the in-person training group, reinforcing the effectiveness of a scalable, digital approach to suicide prevention training.
