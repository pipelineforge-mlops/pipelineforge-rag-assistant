Tuberculous appendicitis is an extremely rare manifestation of gastrointestinal tuberculosis and is often clinically and radiologically indistinguishable from conventional acute appendicitis. We report the case of a 27-year-old man who presented with fever and pain in the right lower quadrant. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed an enlarged appendix with an appendicolith, a distal wall defect, and periappendiceal abscess formation, consistent with perforated acute appendicitis. Laparoscopic appendectomy was performed. Histopathological examination revealed acute gangrenous appendicitis with multiple caseating granulomas predominantly involving the mucosa and submucosa. Although acid-fast bacilli staining was negative, the interferon-gamma release assay was positive, and chest computed tomography revealed pulmonary nodular lesions, supporting the clinical diagnosis of tuberculosis. Anti-tuberculous therapy was initiated. Isolated appendiceal tuberculosis lacks specific imaging features and is frequently indistinguishable from typical acute appendicitis, particularly when complicated by an appendicolith or perforation. This case highlights the importance of considering tuberculosis when granulomatous inflammation is identified on histopathology and performing a systemic evaluation even in patients presenting with typical features of acute appendicitis.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major health concern that affects millions of people worldwide [1]. Although pulmonary involvement is the most common manifestation, extrapulmonary TB accounts for approximately 15%-30% of all TB cases and may involve various organs, including the abdomen [2,3]. Gastrointestinal TB most frequently affects the ileocecal region, whereas isolated appendiceal involvement is exceedingly rare [4].

Tuberculous appendicitis is an uncommon clinical entity, with a reported incidence of 0.1%-3.0% among appendectomy specimens and a low rate in cases of isolated appendiceal disease [5]. Clinically and radiologically, this condition is indistinguishable from conventional acute appendicitis. Consequently, a preoperative diagnosis is extremely uncommon, and most cases are identified only after histopathological examination of the resected appendix [5,6].

Because imaging findings are generally nonspecific, particularly in cases complicated by appendicoliths, perforations, or abscess formation, distinguishing tuberculous appendicitis from typical acute appendicitis remains challenging. Herein, we report a case of perforated acute appendicitis in which histopathological examination revealed underlying tuberculosis, highlighting the diagnostic limitations of imaging alone and the importance of further systemic evaluation when granulomatous inflammation is identified.

Case report

A 27-year-old man presented with a 1-week history of lower abdominal discomfort. He developed high-grade fever (body temperature, 39°C) and pain in the right lower quadrant 1 day before admission and was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Physical examination revealed tenderness in the right lower quadrant with mild muscular guarding. Rebound tenderness was not observed. Laboratory tests revealed elevated levels of inflammatory markers (white blood cell count, 9630/μL; C-reactive protein, 7.62 mg/dL). Acute appendicitis was suspected, and contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT) was performed.

Imaging findings

Unenhanced and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT images were obtained, with contrast-enhanced images acquired 90 seconds after intravenous contrast administration. The appendix was dilated up to approximately 13 mm in diameter with circumferential wall thickening measuring up to 4 mm (Fig. 1A). The appendiceal wall demonstrated relatively homogeneous enhancement without definite hypoenhancing or nonenhancing areas suggestive of ischemia or necrosis. An approximately 8-mm appendicolith was identified approximately 25 mm distal to the appendiceal base, and more prominent dilatation of the appendix was observed distal to the appendicolith (Fig. 1B). The appendiceal lumen contained nonenhancing fluid collection. Near the appendiceal tip, a focal wall defect measuring approximately 14 × 6 mm was identified, through which fluid extended into the periappendiceal region (Fig. 2). A periappendiceal abscess cavity measuring approximately 45 × 25 mm with peripheral rim enhancement had formed surrounding the appendiceal tip (Fig. 3). Mild surrounding fat stranding was present around the abscess cavity and periappendiceal region, suggesting inflammatory extension. Several enlarged lymph nodes measuring up to approximately 8 mm with homogeneous enhancement and without central necrosis were observed near the appendiceal base, consistent with reactive lymphadenopathy (Fig. 4).Fig. 1 Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrating acute appendicitis. Surgical findings

Intraoperatively, the appendiceal tip was adherent to the retroperitoneum with abscess formation. The mesoappendix appeared thickened. Gross examination of the resected specimen demonstrated marked wall thickening approximately 8 mm distal to the appendiceal base, where an impacted appendicolith was identified. A focal wall defect measuring approximately 10 mm was identified approximately 10 mm proximal to the appendiceal tip, penetrating into the mesoappendix and forming an intramesoappendiceal abscess (Fig. 5). No gross intraoperative findings distinguishable from ordinary perforated appendicitis or specifically suggestive of abdominal tuberculosis were identified.Fig. 5 Gross appearance of the resected appendix. Pathological findings

Histologically, the lesion showed marked neutrophilic infiltration with abscess formation and transmural necrosis, consistent with perforated acute gangrenous appendicitis. In addition, multiple epithelioid granulomas with multinucleated giant cells and central caseous necrosis were observed, predominantly involving the mucosa and submucosa, with transmural extension toward the resection margin (Fig. 6). Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative for acid-fast bacilli, whereas Periodic acid–Schiff staining and Grocott staining were negative for fungal organisms. Although Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative, the presence of granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis was considered highly suggestive of tuberculosis.Fig. 6 Histopathological findings of the appendix. (B) High-power view (HE, ×200) shows epithelioid granulomas with multinucleated giant cells and central caseous necrosis (arrow).

Clinical course

Although the patient had no respiratory symptoms, further systemic evaluation for possible tuberculosis was performed based on the histopathological findings. Chest CT revealed an irregular nodular lesion in the right lower lobe surrounded by centrilobular nodules (Fig. 7). Acid-fast bacilli smear and loop-mediated isothermal amplification testing of sputum and bronchial lavage fluid yielded negative results; however, the interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) (T-SPOT) was positive. Colonoscopy revealed no gross abnormalities in the ileocecal region, and biopsy specimens demonstrated neither epithelioid granulomas nor acid-fast bacilli on Ziehl–Neelsen staining. Based on the histopathological findings, chest CT findings, and positive IGRA results, the patient was clinically diagnosed with TB despite the absence of microbiological confirmation, and anti-tuberculous therapy was initiated. The patient continued anti-tuberculous therapy on an outpatient basis and remained clinically stable during follow-up.Fig. 7 Chest computed tomography (CT) findings suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis. Discussion

Extrapulmonary TB accounts for approximately 15%-30% of all TB cases, and abdominal TB represents approximately 5% of these cases [[2], [3], [4]]. Abdominal TB may involve the gastrointestinal tract, peritoneum, lymph nodes, or solid organs [7]. Concomitant pulmonary involvement has been reported in 15%-25% of abdominal TB cases [7].

Gastrointestinal TB accounts for 3%-10% of extrapulmonary TB cases [5]. Although any part of the digestive tract may be involved, the ileocecal region is most frequently affected, representing 64%-80% of cases [7]. This predilection has been attributed to the relative stasis and abundance of lymphoid tissue in the terminal ileum [7].

Tuberculous appendicitis was first described in 1873 [1], and its exact pathogenesis remains unclear [5,8]. It is classified as primary when no other tuberculous focus is identified and as secondary when associated with adjacent ileocecal disease, lymphatic spread, or peritoneal involvement [5,6]. Secondary involvement is considered common. The reported incidence of appendectomy specimens ranges from 0.1% to 3.0% [5], with isolated appendiceal TB accounting for approximately 0.08% [4]. Clinically, this condition is indistinguishable from acute appendicitis [5,6]. Singh et al. categorized its presentation into 3 types: chronic, acute appendicitis-like, and incidental [6,9]. Preoperative diagnosis is rare, and most cases are identified only after histopathological examination [5,6]. Although tuberculous appendicitis is rare and is usually diagnosed only after histopathological examination, perforation has been reported in a number of published cases, suggesting that tuberculous inflammation may contribute to the development of complicated appendicitis [2]. Nevertheless, an impacted appendicolith was also identified in the present case. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether tuberculosis directly caused the appendicitis and subsequent perforation or represented an underlying coexisting condition.

Histopathologically, tuberculous appendicitis is characterized by caseating granulomas comprising epithelioid histiocytes and Langhans-type multinucleated giant cells [2]. In intestinal TB, multiple granulomas are typically located within the mucosa and submucosa [7]. Caseous necrosis is the key diagnostic feature [7]. However, granulomatous inflammation of the appendix is not specific to TB and may be observed in Crohn’s disease, Yersinia infection, sarcoidosis, fungal infections, and parasitic infections [5,10,11]. Among these conditions, Crohn’s disease represents the most important differential diagnosis because of its substantial clinical, endoscopic, and histopathological overlap with intestinal TB, with reported misdiagnosis rates of 50%-70% [10]. Although molecular techniques such as polymerase chain reaction and IGRA improve diagnostic accuracy [3], negative acid-fast staining does not lead to exclusion of TB from the differential diagnosis.

In the present case, the histological examination demonstrated acute gangrenous appendicitis with perforation and multiple caseating granulomas predominantly involving the mucosa and submucosa. Although Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative, the presence of caseating granulomas prompted further systemic evaluation. Based on the histopathological findings, chest CT abnormalities, and positive IGRA results, a clinical diagnosis of TB was established despite the absence of microbiological confirmation.

From a radiological perspective, CT is the modality of choice for evaluating abdominal TB [4]. In intestinal TB, the findings may include mural thickening of the ileum and cecum, involvement of the ileocecal valve, or associated lymphadenopathy [4]. However, isolated appendiceal TB often lacks specific imaging features and is frequently indistinguishable from conventional acute appendicitis, particularly when associated with appendicolith, perforation, or abscess formation [2,4,5]. When tuberculous lymphadenitis is present, imaging may reveal enlarged lymph nodes with homogeneous enhancement in the early stages and low-attenuation centers with peripheral rim enhancement as central caseous necrosis develops [4]. Nevertheless, these findings are neither sensitive nor specific, and imaging alone is usually insufficient to establish the diagnosis.

In our patient, CT revealed appendiceal enlargement with an appendicolith, perforation, and periappendiceal abscess, which were consistent with perforated acute appendicitis. Small regional lymph nodes were present but lacked necrosis or rim enhancement, and no significant thickening of the ileocecal wall or other findings suggestive of abdominal TB were identified. Therefore, the diagnosis of TB could not be established based on imaging findings alone.

This case illustrates that tuberculous appendicitis may present as typical perforated acute appendicitis and may be radiologically indistinguishable from common inflammatory conditions. In the present case, no imaging findings specifically suggested TB, and the diagnosis was ultimately prompted by the histopathological identification of caseating granulomas. Although extremely rare, TB should be considered when granulomatous inflammation is identified on histopathological examination and further systemic evaluation should be performed to assess occult pulmonary involvement. Awareness of this possibility is important because acute appendicitis may occasionally represent the initial manifestation of systemic TB.
