Background

Older adults undergoing lumbar spine fusion are vulnerable to impaired postoperative recovery and persistent postsurgical pain. Dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal general anesthesia may reduce volatile anesthetic and opioid exposure, but its efficacy and tolerability in older adults undergoing high-pain spine surgery remain uncertain. This randomized controlled trial evaluated whether electroencephalographic spectrogram-guided multimodal general anesthesia improves early recovery compared with conventional balanced anesthesia.

Methods

Patients aged ≥60 years undergoing elective lumbar spine fusion were randomized 1:1 in a parallel-group, superiority design to receive either dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal general anesthesia guided by electroencephalographic density spectral array monitoring or conventional bispectral index-guided balanced anesthesia. All patients received standardized perioperative care. The primary efficacy outcome was the 24-hour change in Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score from baseline, analyzed as the between-group difference in mean change score. Secondary outcomes included opioid requirements, 3-month pain outcomes, and safety and tolerability outcomes. All secondary outcomes, including the 3-month pain outcomes, were exploratory.

Results

Among 100 randomized patients, 50 were assigned to each group. The multimodal general anesthesia group had a smaller 24-hour decline in QoR-15 score than the control group (−22.5 vs −33.5 points; mean difference, 11.0; 95% confidence interval, 0.8–21.2; p=0.036). Multimodal general anesthesia reduced sevoflurane consumption [28 (22–42) vs 43 (36–53) mL; p<0.001], intraoperative fentanyl dose [150 (100–200) vs 200 (125–250) μg; p=0.003], and post-anesthetic care unit morphine requirement [0 (0–2) vs 2 (0–3) mg; p<0.001], at the expense of increased intraoperative norepinephrine and atropine requirements. No significant between-group differences were observed in postoperative safety outcomes, although the trial was not powered to exclude clinically relevant differences. Exploratory 3-month analyses showed lower average pain intensity in the multimodal general anesthesia group [1 (0–2) vs 2 (0–4); p=0.033], whereas the proportion of pain-free patients did not differ significantly [41.7% vs 28.0%; p=0.203].

Conclusion

Dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal general anesthesia implemented with spectrogram-guided titration improved early patient-centered recovery and reduced immediate perioperative opioid requirements, at the expense of increased intraoperative vasopressor and atropine use. Exploratory analyses suggested a possible reduction in 3-month average pain intensity, which requires confirmation in trials adequately powered for persistent postsurgical pain.

Plain Language Summary

Older adults who undergo lumbar spine fusion often experience slow recovery after surgery and may develop persistent pain. Standard general anesthesia commonly uses inhaled anesthetics and opioids, but higher exposure to these drugs may affect recovery. Multimodal general anesthesia combines several anesthetic and analgesic drugs with different mechanisms, allowing clinicians to reduce reliance on any single drug.

In this randomized trial, we compared two anesthetic strategies in 100 patients aged 60 years or older undergoing lumbar spine fusion. One group received conventional balanced anesthesia guided mainly by the bispectral index, a single processed number that summarizes the brain’s electrical activity. The other group received multimodal anesthesia using dexmedetomidine and ketamine, guided by an electroencephalographic spectrogram—a colored map that displays the full range of brain-wave frequencies over time and helps clinicians recognize the specific brain patterns produced by these drugs, which a single index number may not capture. Patients receiving multimodal anesthesia had better early recovery at 24 hours after surgery, as measured by the Quality of Recovery-15 questionnaire. They also received less inhaled anesthetic, less fentanyl during surgery, and less morphine in the recovery room. However, they required more medication to support blood pressure (norepinephrine) and heart rate (atropine) during surgery, so this approach requires careful monitoring.

Postoperative complications, including delirium, gastrointestinal impairment, neurocognitive recovery, intensive care unit admission, and hospital stay, did not differ significantly between groups. At 3 months, patients in the multimodal anesthesia group reported lower average pain intensity, but this was an exploratory finding and the proportion of completely pain-free patients did not differ significantly.

These results suggest that dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal anesthesia, when carefully guided by brain monitoring, may improve early recovery after lumbar spine fusion in older adults, but larger studies are needed to confirm its effects on persistent pain.

Introduction

The demand for lumbar spine fusion is increasing with the high prevalence of low back pain and an expanding older surgical population.1,2 Older adults undergoing these prolonged procedures are vulnerable to delayed recovery and chronic postoperative pain.3,4 Conventional balanced general anesthesia with volatile anesthetics and opioids remains widely used, but excessive anesthetic and opioid exposure may impair early recovery and contribute to perioperative adverse effects, supporting strategies that preserve hypnosis and analgesia while limiting unnecessary drug exposure.5,6

Multimodal general anesthesia (MGA) offers a pharmacologically rational approach by combining agents with complementary mechanisms to reduce reliance on a single hypnotic or analgesic.7 Ketamine and dexmedetomidine are relevant adjuncts because they may modulate central sensitization, neuroinflammation, and perioperative stress responses, mechanisms implicated in postoperative pain after spine surgery.8–10 However, these mechanistic considerations do not establish that reducing volatile anesthetic or opioid exposure improves long-term pain outcomes. Consistent with this uncertainty, clinical benefits of opioid-free or multimodal anesthesia have been inconsistent across surgical populations.11–13 A distinct rationale concerns intraoperative titration. Ketamine and dexmedetomidine produce drug-specific electroencephalographic patterns that may be incompletely represented by conventional processed indices.14–17 In older adults, in whom both underdosing and excessive anesthetic depth may be poorly tolerated, spectrogram monitoring may provide clinically useful brain-state information by displaying time–frequency signatures, helping clinicians avoid electroencephalographic suppression and recognize patterns suggesting inadequate antinociception or noxious stimulation.

We therefore evaluated a combined clinical strategy rather than any single pharmacologic or monitoring component. No randomized trial has specifically tested a protocolized dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based MGA regimen implemented with spectrogram-guided titration in older adults undergoing lumbar spine fusion. We compared this approach with conventional bispectral index-guided balanced anesthesia in patients aged 60 years or older undergoing elective lumbar spine fusion. We hypothesized that the combined strategy would improve the 24-hour change in QoR-15 score from baseline, the prespecified primary outcome. Opioid requirements, safety/tolerability outcomes, and 3-month pain outcomes were assessed as secondary exploratory endpoints; the trial was not designed to establish an effect on persistent postsurgical pain.

Methods

This single-center, parallel-group, superiority randomized controlled trial was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (approval number: 202112040MIND) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05247177; February 15, 2022). The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study enrolled patients aged 60 years or older who were undergoing elective lumbar spine fusion surgery. Both open and minimally invasive approaches and primary or revision procedures were eligible, and patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of I–III were included. Exclusion criteria included cardiovascular disease classified as New York Heart Association functional class III or higher, liver cirrhosis more severe than Child–Pugh class B, impaired renal function defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 60 mL·min−1·1.73 m−2, a diagnosis of dementia, and any contraindication to the study drugs (ketamine, dexmedetomidine) or to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding. After enrollment, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based MGA or conventional balanced general anesthesia (control group). The allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in participant enrollment or perioperative care, using a computer-generated 1:1 randomization schedule with randomly permuted block sizes of 4 and 6. No stratification was applied. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. Because anesthetic induction was identical in both groups, the envelope was opened by a study anesthesiologist only after induction of general anesthesia and before initiation of the assigned maintenance anesthetic strategy.

A research nurse enrolled participants, and the attending anesthesiologist implemented the assigned intervention according to the allocation envelope. Because of the nature of the anesthetic interventions, attending anesthesiologists could not be blinded to group allocation. However, patients, surgeons, ward staff, post-anesthetic care unit staff, outcome assessors at 24 hours and 3 months, and the data analyst were blinded to group allocation.

Study Intervention

All patients received standard intraoperative monitoring, including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, end-tidal anesthetic concentration, invasive arterial pressure monitoring, bispectral index (BIS), and analgesia nociception index monitoring. General anesthesia was induced using fentanyl 1.0–2.0 μg·kg−1, propofol 1.5–2.5 mg·kg−1, and cisatracurium 0.2–0.3 mg·kg−1. After tracheal intubation, patients received one of two anesthetic strategies according to group allocation.

Electroencephalographic Monitoring

In all patients, processed electroencephalographic monitoring was performed using a BIS Vista™ monitor (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), with a frontal BIS sensor positioned according to the manufacturer-recommended placement. The density spectral array was displayed using the manufacturer’s default spectrogram settings and frequency range, without user modification. No customized frequency bands or display scales were applied.

In the control group, anesthetic depth was titrated primarily to maintain a bispectral index (BIS) value between 40 and 60. Raw EEG traces were available at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist, but the density spectral array spectrogram was not displayed and was not used to guide titration in the control group, reflecting conventional index-based balanced anesthesia practice and minimizing contamination between groups. In the MGA group, the density spectral array spectrogram displayed by the BIS Vista monitor was used to guide intraoperative titration by interpreting drug-specific time–frequency signatures.14–18 Attending anesthesiologists in the MGA group received standardized training in spectrogram interpretation before the trial, and adherence to the titration protocol was monitored during the study.

Electroencephalographic suppression was quantified objectively using the suppression ratio automatically computed by the BIS Vista monitor, defined by the device as the percentage of each 63-second epoch during which the EEG was isoelectric, corresponding to periods of at least 0.5 seconds during which EEG voltage remained within approximately ±5 μV. Cumulative EEG suppression time during anesthetic maintenance was derived from this measure. In both groups, attending anesthesiologists were instructed to avoid EEG suppression throughout the procedure.

Conventional Balanced General Anesthesia

Patients in the control group received conventional balanced general anesthesia consisting of sevoflurane, opioid analgesia, neuromuscular blockade, and intravenous fluid therapy with balanced crystalloids.19 Anesthetic depth was titrated primarily to maintain a BIS value between 40 and 60. Raw electroencephalographic traces were available at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist, but the density spectral array spectrogram was not displayed and was not used to guide titration in the control group, reflecting conventional index-based balanced anesthesia practice; this separation was intended to minimize contamination between groups. Intraoperative opioid administration was guided by clinical judgment and the analgesia nociception index, as described in the standardized perioperative care protocol.

Dexmedetomidine–Ketamine-Based Multimodal General Anesthesia and Spectrogram-Guided Titration

The experimental intervention was designed as a combined pharmacologic–titration strategy rather than as an isolated drug or monitoring intervention. It consisted of a dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal pharmacologic regimen implemented with EEG density spectral array-guided titration.15,16,20 This strategy was intended to reduce volatile anesthetic and opioid exposure while maintaining adequate hypnosis, antinociception, and hemodynamic stability.

In the MGA group, anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane, dexmedetomidine, ketamine, neuromuscular blockade, and intravenous fluid therapy with balanced crystalloids.19 Drug infusions were calculated using actual body weight. Dexmedetomidine was infused at 0.3 μg·kg−1·h−1 from anesthesia induction. After a stable sevoflurane-induced EEG spectrogram pattern was established, ketamine was administered as a 300 μg·kg−1 bolus followed by continuous infusion at 3 μg·kg−1·min−1. The ketamine infusion was titrated within a permitted range of 1–5 μg·kg−1·min−1 and adjusted in steps of 0.5 μg·kg−1·min−1 every 10 minutes according to predefined spectrogram features and clinical signs. Ketamine infusion was increased when spectrogram features suggested inadequate antinociception or light anesthesia, including beta arousal, alpha dropout, or delta arousal, or when the analgesia nociception index decreased below 50. The infusion was decreased when the spectrogram showed excessive low-frequency delta power, global reduction in EEG power, or patterns approaching EEG suppression. The titration goal was to maintain EEG patterns consistent with sevoflurane–ketamine anesthesia while avoiding excessive anesthetic depth.15,16,18 Both dexmedetomidine and ketamine infusions were discontinued at wound closure to reduce the risk of delayed emergence.

Protocol adherence was assessed using objective intraoperative source data. In the control group, adherence to conventional index-guided management was evaluated from the electronic anesthesia record, including recorded BIS values. In the MGA group, the complete intraoperative density spectral array spectrogram files were digitally archived. These records were used to assess whether the dexmedetomidine–ketamine regimen and spectrogram-guided titration were implemented according to protocol. For the MGA group, archived spectrogram files were reviewed by an independent anesthesiologist trained in spectrogram interpretation who was not involved in intraoperative care and was blinded to postoperative outcomes. Protocol adherence was assessed according to predefined criteria: availability and intraoperative use of the density spectral array display; administration of dexmedetomidine and ketamine within the specified dosing ranges; ketamine titration according to predefined spectrogram features and clinical signs. All randomized patients were retained in the intention-to-treat analysis regardless of protocol adherence.

Safety Monitoring and Hemodynamic Management

Safety monitoring focused on hemodynamic tolerance, excessive anesthetic depth, delayed recovery, and postoperative adverse events. Bradycardia was treated with intravenous atropine according to predefined clinical criteria. The dexmedetomidine infusion could be reduced stepwise by 0.1 μg·kg−1·h−1 in cases of persistent bradycardia, defined as heart rate below 50 beats per minute despite repeated atropine treatment. Hypotension was treated with intravenous norepinephrine and crystalloid boluses to maintain mean arterial pressure at or above 65 mmHg. Systolic blood pressure was maintained below 160 mmHg, and heart rate was maintained between 50 and 100 beats per minute.

Standardized Perioperative Care

Perioperative management was standardized across both groups and was designed to align with contemporary enhanced recovery principles for spine surgery.21 All patients received structured preoperative education, minimized preoperative fasting, prophylaxis for postoperative nausea and vomiting, non-opioid analgesia, regional analgesia, goal-directed fluid management, normothermia maintenance, and encouragement of early postoperative mobilization and oral intake.

For postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis, intravenous dexamethasone 5 mg and ondansetron 4 mg were administered after induction. A bilateral single-shot ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block was performed in all patients after induction of general anesthesia and tracheal intubation, after positioning and before skin incision. The block was performed at the planned surgical level by an experienced anesthesiologist using a standardized technique. With the patient in the prone position, a low-frequency curvilinear transducer was placed in a paramedian sagittal orientation to identify the transverse process at the intended surgical level. A block needle was advanced using an in-plane approach until the needle tip was positioned deep to the erector spinae muscle and superficial to the transverse process. After negative aspiration and ultrasound confirmation of appropriate interfascial spread, ropivacaine 0.375% was injected, 20 mL per side. No catheter was inserted. Technical success was defined as visualization of the needle tip in the correct fascial plane and visible cranio-caudal spread of local anesthetic deep to the erector spinae muscle. Because the block was performed after induction of general anesthesia, sensory testing was not performed. The block procedure was standardized across groups and applied before skin incision in both groups.22,23 Intraoperative nociception was guided using the analgesia nociception index, which was maintained between 50 and 70. Intravenous fentanyl boluses of 25–75 μg were administered when clinically indicated. After skin closure, intravenous ketorolac 30 mg was administered.

Continuous cardiac output monitoring was performed using the fourth-generation Vigileo/FloTrac system to support goal-directed hemodynamic management.9,24 Mechanical ventilation was delivered using pressure-controlled volume-guaranteed ventilation, with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL·kg−1 ideal body weight and 5 cmH2O positive end-expiratory pressure. The fraction of inspired oxygen was maintained at 40%, and respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 mmHg. Body temperature was maintained above 36°C using forced-air warming.

After surgery, patients were transferred to the post-anesthetic care unit (PACU) unless direct postoperative ICU admission was planned. Readiness for discharge from the post-anesthetic care unit was assessed using the Modified Aldrete Score, with a discharge threshold of 9 or higher. Rescue analgesia with intravenous morphine (2–4 mg) was available on patient request both in the PACU and on the ward to maintain a numeric pain rating score below 4. Ward analgesia consisted of scheduled paracetamol 1 g every 6 hours and celecoxib 200 mg every 12 hours during the first two postoperative days. Intravenous morphine 2–4 mg every 2 hours was available as rescue analgesia upon patient request to maintain a numeric pain rating score below 4.

Efficacy Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the 24-hour change in Quality of Recovery-15 score from the preoperative baseline. The Quality of Recovery-15 is a validated patient-centered measure of postoperative recovery, with total scores ranging from 0 to 150 and higher scores indicating better recovery.25 Secondary efficacy outcomes included sevoflurane consumption, intraoperative fentanyl dose, morphine requirement in the post-anesthetic care unit, cumulative in-hospital morphine consumption, and pain outcomes at 3 months after surgery. Chronic postsurgical pain was assessed through structured telephone interview using the Chinese version of the Brief Pain Inventory.26 Pain outcomes included the proportion of pain-free patients, worst pain score, least pain score, average pain score, and pain-related interference with general activity, mood, walking ability, normal work, relations with other people, sleep, and enjoyment of life.

Safety and Tolerability Outcomes

Safety and tolerability outcomes included intraoperative norepinephrine dose, atropine use, electroencephalographic suppression time, post-anesthetic care unit stay, postoperative delirium, postoperative gastrointestinal impairment, intensive care unit requirement, hospital length of stay, and postoperative neurocognitive trajectory.

Postoperative gastrointestinal impairment was assessed using the I-FEED scoring system.27 Daily I-FEED scores were recorded, and the highest overall score was used to classify patients as having normal gastrointestinal function, gastrointestinal intolerance, or postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction. Postoperative delirium was assessed using the standard Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) twice daily, in the morning and evening, during the first 72 hours after surgery. Assessments were performed after emergence from anesthesia by a trained research assistant who was blinded to group allocation and did not participate in perioperative care. The CAM assessment was used to identify delirium irrespective of psychomotor subtype, including hypoactive delirium. Patients requiring postoperative ICU admission were extubated after surgery and admitted primarily for overnight observation rather than continuous sedation or mechanical ventilation; therefore, a separate sedation-level screening procedure, such as the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale before delirium assessment, was not used. Postoperative cognitive function was assessed using the Taiwanese version of the Quick Mild Cognitive Impairment test at three time points: preoperatively, 24 hours postoperatively, and 6 months after surgery.28

Statistical Analysis

Thresholds for QoR-15 score changes in patients undergoing lumbar spine fusion surgery were not available at the time of study initiation. Therefore, we estimated a mean between-group difference of 12 points in the decrease of QoR-15 on postoperative day (POD) 1, with an assumed standard deviation of 20 based on our pilot dataset. The 12-point assumption was chosen conservatively to reflect a difference exceeding the QoR-15 minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 6 points,25 while remaining plausible for an intervention acting predominantly through reduced anesthetic and opioid exposure; the pilot dataset used for this estimate did not overlap with the final trial population. The required sample size was 90 patients (45 per group), providing 80% power with a two-sided α of 0.05. To accommodate potential attrition, 100 patients (50 per group) were enrolled and randomized. All 100 randomized patients were analyzed in the group to which they were assigned, constituting the intention-to-treat population for the primary outcome and all in-hospital analyses; no patient was lost before in-hospital assessment and no observations were excluded as outliers. The 3-month pain outcomes were analyzed using available data at the 3-month assessment without imputation. Longitudinal Qmci-TW trajectories were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with all available repeated observations; participants with missing 6-month assessments therefore continued to contribute their available preoperative and 24-hour measurements. No multiple imputation or weighting procedure was applied. Because follow-up missingness was small and broadly balanced between groups, missingness was assumed to be non-informative after consideration of the observed data; however, residual bias from unverifiable missing-data assumptions cannot be excluded.

The primary analysis was a between-group comparison of the 24-hour QoR-15 change score using Student’s independent-samples t-test on the intention-to-treat population, with the mean difference and its 95% confidence interval also displayed as a Gardner–Altman estimation plot (Figure 1).29 Normality of the change score was assessed using the D’Agostino–Pearson test and visual inspection of histograms, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using the F-test; where the assumption of equal variances was not met, the Welch t-test was applied. As a covariate-adjusted sensitivity analysis, postoperative 24-hour QoR-15 scores were compared between groups using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA),30 with treatment group as the fixed factor and preoperative QoR-15 score, number of fused levels, and revision surgery as covariates. The homogeneity of regression slopes assumption for the preoperative QoR-15 covariate was assessed by testing the interaction between group allocation and preoperative QoR-15 score. This ANCOVA analysis was interpreted as estimating the adjusted between-group difference in POD1 QoR-15 after accounting for baseline QoR-15 and measured surgical covariates, and is related to but distinct from the unadjusted change-score estimand. Generalized estimating equations with a Gaussian distribution, identity link, robust standard errors, and an exchangeable working correlation structure were additionally used as a supportive repeated-measures analysis to assess whether the change in QoR-15 over time differed between groups, with group, time, and the group-by-time interaction included in the model. Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test was applied for dichotomous variables. Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, whereas the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied to non-normally distributed or ordinal data. For Qmci-TW, GEE with a Gaussian distribution, identity link, robust standard errors, and an exchangeable working correlation structure were used with all available repeated observations. Treatment group, time, and the group-by-time interaction were included in the model to assess whether neurocognitive trajectories differed between groups. As a post hoc sensitivity analysis for missing 6-month Qmci-TW assessments, a complete-case ANCOVA was performed among participants with available baseline and 6-month Qmci-TW data. The 6-month Qmci-TW score was entered as the dependent variable, treatment group as the fixed factor, and baseline Qmci-TW score as a covariate. The homogeneity of regression slopes assumption was assessed using the group-by-baseline Qmci-TW interaction.

For secondary and exploratory outcomes, effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals are reported in addition to p values: Hodges–Lehmann median differences for continuous or ordinal outcomes and risk differences and odds ratios for categorical outcomes. All secondary outcomes were exploratory; no prespecified hierarchical testing strategy was applied, and their p values are unadjusted and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (25th–75th percentile), as appropriate. Exact p values are reported except where conventionally expressed as p<0.001. Item-level QoR-15 analyses were performed post hoc to explore which recovery domains contributed to the overall treatment effect; for these exploratory analyses, unadjusted p values are presented descriptively, and Bonferroni-adjusted p values and false discovery rate q values are provided for reference across the 15 individual items. A standardized difference <0.25 was considered indicative of acceptable balance.31 Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc Statistical Software version 23.3 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Participant Flow and Baseline Characteristics

Between March 14, 2022, and June 2, 2025, 132 patients scheduled for elective lumbar spine fusion surgery were screened. Of these, 100 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal general anesthesia group (MGA group; n=50) or the conventional balanced anesthesia group (control group; n=50) (Figure 2). All 100 randomized participants (50 per group) were included in the primary intention-to-treat analysis and in-hospital analyses. Exploratory 3-month pain outcomes were available in 48 participants in the MGA group and 50 participants in the control group; these 48 versus 50 participant counts refer only to the exploratory 3-month pain follow-up and not to the primary analysis. Six-month Qmci-TW assessments were completed in 46 participants in the MGA group and 45 participants in the control group (Figure 2). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups (Table 1). Age, sex, body mass index, ASA physical status, surgical approach, and preoperative pain intensity were comparable, with all standardized differences below the predefined threshold for acceptable balance. The low baseline pain intensity (median 0) reflects the elective, largely degenerative population, in whom preoperative pain was assessed at rest and many patients had intermittent or position-dependent radicular symptoms rather than continuous pain at the time of assessment; because randomization produced balanced groups, this low baseline applied equally to both arms and does not indicate a randomization failure. Both primary and revision procedures were eligible; revision surgery was performed in 10 (20%) MGA and 7 (14%) control patients. No patient was receiving regular scheduled opioid therapy before surgery. Occasional as-needed use of weak opioid–acetaminophen combination tablets was not systematically recorded. No crossover or major protocol deviation was identified, and no patient was excluded from the intention-to-treat analysis because of protocol adherence.

Primary Efficacy Outcome: 24-Hour Quality of Recovery

The primary efficacy outcome favored the MGA group (Figure 1). QoR-15 scores decreased from baseline to POD1 in both groups, but the decline was smaller in the MGA group than in the control group (−22.5 ± 27.6 vs −33.5 ± 23.8 points). In the primary unadjusted intention-to-treat analysis comparing 24-hour change scores using Student’s independent-samples t-test, the mean between-group difference was 11.0 points favoring MGA (MGA minus control; 95% confidence interval, 0.8 to 21.2; t=2.13, df=98, p=0.036). The equal-variance assumption was supported by the F-test (p=0.31), and normality of the change score was supported by the D’Agostino–Pearson test (p=0.93). The point estimate exceeded the established QoR-15 minimal clinically important difference of 6 points; however, the lower bound of the confidence interval (0.8 points) lay below this threshold, so the magnitude of benefit should be interpreted with corresponding uncertainty.

In the covariate-adjusted ANCOVA sensitivity analysis, with POD1 QoR-15 as the dependent variable and treatment group, baseline QoR-15 score, number of fused levels, and revision surgery included in the model, adjusted POD1 QoR-15 remained higher in the MGA group than in the control group. The adjusted between-group difference was 9.3 points favoring MGA (95% confidence interval, 0.2 to 18.4; p=0.045). The group-by-baseline QoR-15 interaction was not significant (p=0.411), supporting the homogeneity of regression slopes assumption for the preoperative QoR-15 covariate. Thus, the covariate-adjusted sensitivity analysis was consistent with the primary unadjusted change-score analysis and suggested that the treatment effect was not explained by baseline QoR-15 or the measured surgical covariates included in the model. A supportive repeated-measures GEE analysis also showed a significant group-by-time interaction (interaction coefficient for control versus MGA at POD1, −11.0 points; 95% confidence interval, −21.0 to −1.0; p=0.031), indicating that the temporal change in QoR-15 differed between groups and that the control group had a greater postoperative decline.

In a post hoc exploratory item-level QoR-15 analysis, most item-level treatment effects favored the MGA group, although no individual item remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1). The largest mean differences were observed for the ability to return to work or usual home activities, personal toilet/hygiene, emotional symptoms, enjoyment of food, and nausea or vomiting. However, after Bonferroni correction across the 15 individual items, none of the item-level comparisons reached the adjusted significance threshold. These findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, suggesting that the overall QoR-15 benefit may reflect modest improvements across several recovery domains rather than a single dominant item.

Safety and Tolerability Outcomes

No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in postoperative safety and tolerability outcomes (Table 3); however, the trial was not powered to establish equivalence or non-inferiority, and clinically relevant differences cannot be excluded. Postoperative delirium occurred in 5 patients (10%) in the MGA group and 3 patients (6%) in the control group [risk difference, 4.0%; 95% CI, −6.6% to 14.6%; odds ratio, 1.74; 95% CI, 0.39 to 7.71; p=0.715]. Gastrointestinal recovery, assessed using I-FEED categories, did not differ significantly between groups (p=0.443). No significant difference was observed in postoperative neurocognitive trajectories, as assessed by Qmci-TW scores over time (group-by-time interaction p=0.845). In a complete-case sensitivity analysis including 46 participants in the MGA group and 45 in the control group, the baseline-adjusted between-group difference in 6-month Qmci-TW was −0.22 points (95% confidence interval, −3.46 to 3.01; p=0.891). The group-by-baseline Qmci-TW interaction was not significant (p=0.819), and the result was consistent with the main longitudinal GEE analysis. ICU requirement [5 (10%) vs 3 (6%); p=0.715] and hospital length of stay [5.5 (4.0–7.0) vs 6.0 (4.0–9.0) days; p=0.875] were also not significantly different between groups. All patients admitted to the ICU were extubated postoperatively and were admitted primarily for overnight observation because of greater intraoperative blood loss; none required continuous postoperative sedation or mechanical ventilation.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial of older adults undergoing lumbar spine fusion, a dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal anesthetic regimen implemented with electroencephalographic spectrogram-guided titration improved 24-hour patient-centered recovery compared with conventional balanced anesthesia. The regimen reduced volatile anesthetic exposure, intraoperative fentanyl use, and post-anesthetic care unit morphine requirement, at the expense of higher intraoperative norepinephrine and atropine requirements. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in measured postoperative safety outcomes. Exploratory 3-month findings suggested lower average pain intensity and less interference with normal work, but not a higher pain-free proportion. Because the intervention combined dexmedetomidine, ketamine, spectrogram-guided titration, and reduced volatile anesthetic and opioid exposure, the trial cannot isolate the independent contribution of any single component. The results should therefore be interpreted as supporting the combined anesthetic strategy as implemented in this protocol.

The present findings support the concept that multimodal general anesthesia should be viewed not simply as the addition of adjunctive drugs, but as a pharmacologic strategy requiring careful titration to balance efficacy and tolerability.7 In the present study, the dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based regimen was associated with lower sevoflurane and fentanyl requirements, consistent with this dose-sparing concept. This is particularly relevant for older adults undergoing painful spine surgery, in whom both excessive anesthetic exposure and insufficient antinociception may adversely affect early recovery.4,32 However, previous clinical studies of ketamine- and dexmedetomidine-based multimodal or opioid-free anesthesia have yielded inconsistent results. In the SOFA trial, an opioid-free anesthesia protocol produced only modest improvement in early QoR-15 recovery after major surgery.11 Similarly, the POFA trial found that balanced opioid-free anesthesia with dexmedetomidine did not improve major postoperative outcomes and was associated with more adverse events, including bradycardia and hypoxemia.13 Other studies in enhanced recovery settings, including total knee arthroplasty and thoracic surgery, have not consistently shown improvements in opioid consumption, pain scores, or functional recovery when dexmedetomidine and ketamine were added to an already established multimodal analgesic pathway.12,33 These discrepant findings suggest that the clinical effect of multimodal anesthesia depends not only on drug selection, but also on patient population, surgical context, cointerventions, and intraoperative titration strategy.7,34

A key difference between the present trial and many prior studies is that the multimodal regimen was implemented with electroencephalographic spectrogram-guided titration. Ketamine and dexmedetomidine alter electroencephalographic dynamics in ways that may not be adequately represented by conventional processed indices.14–17 Ketamine may increase high-frequency activity and alter oscillatory signatures, while dexmedetomidine produces sleep-like spindle and slow-wave patterns; these drug-specific effects can make interpretation of a single processed index challenging.14–17 Spectrogram-guided titration provides a time–frequency representation of anesthetic brain states and may help clinicians recognize drug-specific patterns, avoid electroencephalographic suppression, and identify signatures suggestive of inadequate antinociception or noxious stimulation.14–17,20,35 Nevertheless, the present trial cannot isolate the independent contribution of spectrogram guidance from the pharmacologic effects of dexmedetomidine and ketamine. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as supporting a combined pharmacologic–titration strategy rather than proving that spectrogram monitoring alone is superior to conventional processed electroencephalographic index guidance.

The improvement in early postoperative recovery is clinically important because the between-group difference in 24-hour QoR-15 change exceeded the established minimal clinically important difference.25 Nonetheless, the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval (0.8 points) fell below this threshold, so the true magnitude of benefit remains uncertain and the effect should be regarded as clinically meaningful but not precisely estimated. The QoR-15 is a validated, responsive, and patient-centered recovery measure that captures multiple dimensions of early postoperative recovery, including pain, physical comfort, emotional state, and functional independence.25 The observed improvement occurred despite standardized perioperative care in both groups, including regional analgesia, non-opioid analgesics, postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis, goal-directed hemodynamic management, and early mobilization. These standardized cointerventions likely reduced between-group differences in downstream outcomes such as cumulative in-hospital morphine use, gastrointestinal impairment, and hospital length of stay.21,27,36 Thus, the early recovery benefit may reflect the combined effects of reduced intraoperative anesthetic exposure, reduced opioid exposure, and avoidance of excessive anesthetic depth rather than differences in postoperative care.

The opioid-sparing pattern deserves specific consideration. The MGA group received less intraoperative fentanyl and required less morphine in the post-anesthetic care unit, whereas cumulative in-hospital morphine consumption was similar between groups. This suggests that the dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based regimen had its clearest analgesic-sparing effect during the intraoperative and immediate post-anesthetic phases. Once patients entered the standardized ward analgesic pathway, scheduled acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and erector spinae plane block may have minimized subsequent between-group differences in opioid use. The absence of a difference in cumulative in-hospital morphine therefore tempers any claim of a sustained analgesic benefit and indicates that the early opioid-sparing effect did not extend into the later ward period. This interpretation is consistent with the concept that optimized intraoperative antinociception may influence immediate recovery and early pain experience, even when later ward analgesia is standardized.37 The hemodynamic findings are central to the benefit–risk interpretation of this regimen. Although MGA reduced volatile anesthetic and opioid exposure, it increased norepinephrine and atropine requirements. This trade-off is pharmacologically plausible because dexmedetomidine has sympatholytic and bradycardic effects. A recent systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis similarly reported that intraoperative dexmedetomidine improves patient-centered quality of recovery and may reduce chronic pain, but at the expense of increased clinically important hypotension and bradycardia.38 In the present trial, these hemodynamic effects were manageable under active arterial pressure monitoring, goal-directed hemodynamic management, and predefined treatment algorithms.9,24 Importantly, higher vasopressor and atropine requirements were not accompanied by longer post-anesthetic care unit stay, higher intensive care unit admission, longer hospitalization, or worse measured postoperative neurocognitive outcomes. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal anesthesia should be used with caution in older patients with limited hemodynamic reserve, and particularly in those with conduction system disease or sinus node dysfunction, severe autonomic dysfunction, unstable or significant coronary artery disease, hypovolemia, or high-grade atrioventricular block, in whom the sympatholytic and bradycardic effects of dexmedetomidine may be less well tolerated. Careful patient selection, invasive arterial pressure monitoring, dose titration, and readiness to treat bradycardia and hypotension are advisable when this regimen is used.

The 3-month pain findings should be interpreted cautiously and are best regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The MGA group had lower average pain intensity and less interference with normal work, but the proportion of pain-free patients was not significantly different, and most pain intensity and interference domains did not reach statistical significance. These findings therefore suggest, at most, a possible signal that the regimen may reduce average pain severity at 3 months rather than prevent persistent postsurgical pain; the study was neither designed nor powered for persistent pain endpoints, and confirmation in adequately powered trials is required. Spine surgery can induce peripheral and central sensitization, impaired descending modulation, and neuroinflammatory responses, all of which contribute to persistent postoperative pain.10,39,40 Ketamine may reduce central sensitization through N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonism, while dexmedetomidine may modulate sympathetic activation, inflammatory responses, and perioperative stress signaling.8,9,41 This mechanistic interpretation is consistent with prior literature suggesting that perioperative modulation of sensitization and neuroinflammation may influence the transition from acute to persistent postsurgical pain.10,39,40 Nevertheless, because chronic pain outcomes were secondary and the study was not powered specifically for persistent pain endpoints, these results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

We did not observe significant differences in postoperative delirium, neurocognitive recovery, or gastrointestinal impairment. Although ketamine and dexmedetomidine have been investigated for potential neurocognitive benefits, evidence remains heterogeneous across perioperative populations and clinical contexts.42,43 The absence of neurocognitive benefit in the present trial may partly reflect the very low electroencephalographic suppression time in both groups, suggesting that excessive anesthetic depth was largely avoided even in the control group.44 In this context, any additional neuroprotective effect of ketamine or dexmedetomidine would be difficult to detect. Because both groups already achieved minimal suppression, the trial may have had limited ability to demonstrate a between-group neurocognitive advantage. Similarly, the lack of gastrointestinal benefit may reflect the standardized multimodal analgesic pathway and low cumulative in-hospital opioid consumption in both groups. Dexmedetomidine has been reported to facilitate gastrointestinal recovery in older adults undergoing abdominal surgery,45 but the effect may be less apparent in the present setting because all patients received non-opioid analgesia and erector spinae plane block, which may independently promote gastrointestinal recovery after spine surgery.27,36 Because the postoperative safety outcomes were not statistically significantly different but the trial was not powered to establish equivalence or non-inferiority, smaller but clinically relevant differences in delirium, intensive care unit admission, gastrointestinal dysfunction, or neurocognitive decline cannot be excluded.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a single-center trial with a modest sample size, which may limit generalizability; however, the design allowed strict standardization of perioperative care and reduced heterogeneity in cointerventions that could influence recovery outcomes. Second, attending anesthesiologists could not be blinded to group allocation, although postoperative assessments were performed by blinded investigators independent of intraoperative care, and the primary endpoint was a patient-reported recovery measure.25 Third, because the intervention combined a dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based regimen with spectrogram-guided titration and intended reductions in volatile anesthetic and opioid exposure, the independent contribution of each component cannot be isolated. A factorial or dismantling trial would be required to determine the separate effects of the pharmacologic regimen and the monitoring/titration strategy. Fourth, the study was powered for the 24-hour QoR-15 outcome and was underpowered for secondary, safety, and 3-month persistent pain endpoints; these analyses were exploratory, unadjusted for multiplicity, and should be confirmed in larger trials. Fifth, the lower bound of the primary-outcome confidence interval fell below the MCID, so the magnitude of the recovery benefit is uncertain. Sixth, follow-up data were incomplete. Three-month pain outcomes were analyzed using available cases, and 6-month Qmci-TW trajectories were analyzed using GEE with all available repeated observations. Missingness was assumed to be non-informative after consideration of the observed data. Although missingness was small and broadly balanced between groups, no imputation-based sensitivity analysis was performed; therefore, residual bias cannot be excluded if missingness was related to unobserved pain or neurocognitive outcomes. Finally, several potentially relevant baseline factors, including detailed diagnostic categories, preoperative neuropathic medication use, depression or anxiety measures, frailty scores, pain duration, and disability indices, were not systematically collected, and residual imbalance in these unmeasured characteristics cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In older adults undergoing lumbar spine fusion, a dexmedetomidine–ketamine-based multimodal anesthetic regimen implemented with spectrogram-guided titration was associated with a smaller 24-hour decline in QoR-15 score and reduced immediate perioperative opioid requirements compared with conventional balanced anesthesia. These potential benefits occurred at the expense of increased intraoperative norepinephrine and atropine requirements. Exploratory 3-month pain findings require confirmation in trials adequately powered for persistent postsurgical pain, and the present trial was not powered to establish safety equivalence.
