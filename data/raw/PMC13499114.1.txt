Evaluation of subscapularis morphology following shoulder instability surgery: a retrospective magnetic resonance imaging-based comparison of open and arthroscopic techniques

Background

Open anterior shoulder stabilization with subscapularis takedown remains an important surgical option in selected cases, including revision surgery, significant capsular laxity, and contact athletes. However, concerns persist regarding potential long-term adverse effects on subscapularis muscle morphology, including atrophy and fatty infiltration, which have contributed to a preference for arthroscopic techniques.

Methods

Consecutive patients undergoing open or arthroscopic shoulder stabilization by a single surgeon between 2007 and 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were included if pre-operative and post-operative MRI scans were available at a minimum of 6 months following surgery. Subscapularis morphology was assessed on T1-weighted parasagittal magnetic resonance image (MRI) using established measurement techniques, including muscle dimensions and cross-sectional area at standardized levels. Fatty infiltration was graded using the Goutallier classification, and muscle atrophy was quantified using the upper-to-total cross-sectional area ratio. Two blinded observers independently reviewed all imaging, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients.

Results

Eighty-three patients met the inclusion criteria (22 open, 61 arthroscopic). No statistically significant differences were observed in subscapularis muscle dimensions, cross-sectional area, or atrophy indices between pre-operative and post-operative imaging in either group. Goutallier grade remained unchanged in the majority of patients, with minimal progression observed in isolated cases. Inter-rater reliability was good to excellent across all measurements.

Conclusion

In this selected cohort with post-operative imaging, no consistent MRI evidence of subscapularis atrophy or fatty infiltration was identified following either open or arthroscopic stabilization. These findings suggest that, when carefully handled and repaired, open subscapularis takedown may not inevitably result in adverse morphologic change.

Open stabilization with subscapularis takedown remains a reliable option for managing anterior shoulder instability, particularly in scenarios where arthroscopic repair may be insufficient. Current indications include critical and subcritical glenoid bone loss, revision after failed arthroscopy, and collision or contact athletes who face high recurrence risks and require greater capsular reinforcement.4,5,15 Despite a decline in its routine use, open repair has demonstrated durable long-term outcomes, and recent editorials have highlighted its value in patients with bony Bankart fractures or approximately 10% glenoid bone loss.1,15

In contrast, arthroscopic stabilization has become the predominant technique worldwide. Multiple randomized and comparative studies show that arthroscopy allows quicker recovery of motion and faster return to sport, with early post-operative functional advantages.5,7,10 However, in high-demand collision athletes, redislocation rates after arthroscopy remain higher than with open approaches, supporting the selective use of open repair and capsule plication in this cohort.4,5

The central debate around open stabilization concerns the potential compromise of the subscapularis tendon. Detachment for exposure may predispose to atrophy, shortening, or fatty infiltration, which could impair long-term function.6,9,22 Some series have demonstrated measurable changes in muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) and strength,14,20 while others using tendon-splitting or careful takedown-and-repair techniques have found preserved subscapularis integrity without clinically relevant dysfunction.18,21

Given these uncertainties, this study aimed to directly compare magnetic resonance image (MRI)-based measurements of subscapularis morphology before and after either open stabilization with takedown or subscapularis-sparing arthroscopic repair. We hypothesized that, when carefully repaired, the subscapularis would remain morphologically stable without significant deterioration in size or fatty infiltration.

Methods

Surgical technique—open stabilization

Open stabilization procedures were performed through a longitudinal incision from the coracoid process extending distally toward the axilla for approximately 7 cm using a standard deltopectoral approach. The superior and inferior margins of subscapularis were identified for the tenotomy. Superiorly, a longitudinal incision was made in the rotator interval. The anterior margin of the supraspinatus was loaded with #2 non-absorbable suture for later rotator interval repair. Inferiorly, the region just above the “3 sisters” anterior circumflex vessels was identified and marked above using diathermy.

Diathermy was used to start the tenotomy, joining the superior and inferior landmarks. This was positioned approximately 1 cm medial to the insertion. Superiorly, the diathermy carefully divided visible tendon fibers with partial thickness division. Inferiorly, the dissection progressed through the more muscular insertion to allow clearer exposure of the capsule. A McDonald dissector was passed beneath the inferior muscular portion of the subscapularis, outside the capsule. Careful scissor release then continued this plane proximally through the tendinous attachment. The capsule was opened, leaving a cuff of capsular tissue as required.

For repair, the superior aspect of the tenotomy was aligned with the arm slightly abducted and internally rotated to reduce tension within the field of repair. Usually, 4 #2 non-absorbable figure-of-8 configuration sutures were placed with secure purchase through both tendon and tendon stump.

Post-operatively, the arm was immobilized in a sling for 6 weeks. Passive pendular exercises were commenced at approximately 10 days. After 6 weeks, gentle passive followed by active range-of-motion exercises were introduced without forcing external rotation. Light use was permitted while protecting the subscapularis repair. Graduated strengthening exercises commenced from approximately 12 weeks post-operatively. Please see the accompanying video technique.

Patient selection

All patients who underwent shoulder stabilization surgery (open or arthroscopic) performed by a single fellowship-trained shoulder surgeon between 2007 and 2022 were identified from routine clinical records. As part of this retrospective clinical audit, cases were included where both pre-operative and post-operative MRI scans were available (with post-operative imaging performed at least 6 months following surgery) and where there was no history of neuromuscular disorder or prior subscapularis surgery. A minimum interval of 6 months between scans was used to minimize the influence of early post-operative edema and disuse-related changes, allowing assessment of more stable post-operative muscle morphology.12 Patients were excluded if their imaging was incomplete or of insufficient quality for analysis.

Imaging acquisition and measurements

Subscapularis morphology was assessed using T1-weighted parasagittal MRI images. A change in radiological protocol meant that early scans did not routinely include the scapular Y view. As a result, 2 anatomical levels were used for analysis:

Scapular Y view (standard technique): vertical muscle length, cranial transverse diameter, caudal transverse diameter, and CSA (upper, lower, and total) were measured at the scapular Y view, following the technique described by Seppel et al.23 Measurements at this level were performed only where paired scapular Y views were available (see Fig. 1).Figure 1 Imaging measurements at the Y cross-sectional view. Cranial transverse diameter (CrTD), caudal transverse diameter (CaTD), upper cross-sectional area (green), lower cross-sectional area (yellow), and vertical muscle length (VML). CSA, cross-sectional area.

Lateral coracoid level (novel technique): total CSA was measured at the most lateral base of the coracoid in cases where paired scapular Y were not available. This level was defined on both the axial and the en face view of the glenoid, as the most lateral image where the coracoid process is in continuity with the glenoid. The lateral coracoid level has not previously been described in the literature and represents a new measurement plane being tested in this study. See Figure 2.Figure 2 Lateral base of coracoid level defined in (A) parasagittal with total CSA measurement and (B) axial imaging as the most lateral part of the coracoid base where the coracoid is viewed inserting on the enface view. CSA, cross-sectional area.

Atrophy was assessed as the CSA ratio of the upper half to the total muscle area. Atrophy severity was further categorized: stage I (mild) < 0.40, stage II (moderate) < 0.35, and stage III (severe) < 0.30.23

Both pre-operative and post-operative images were reviewed for the presence of subscapularis tears.

Image review

All MRI scans were independently reviewed by 2 blinded observers: a fellowship-trained musculoskeletal radiologist and a shoulder fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon. Measurements were performed using InteleViewer (Intelerad Medical Systems Incorporated, Montreal, Quebec, Canada).

Reliability

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) calculated with a 2-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measures model.25 Interpretation of ICC values followed established thresholds: <0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and >0.90 excellent reliability.13

Statistical analysis

Pre- and post-operative morphologic values were compared using paired t-tests. For categorical data, chi-square testing was applied. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical statement

This project was conducted as a retrospective clinical audit of a single surgeon's private practice evaluating post-operative complications and radiological outcomes. In accordance with New Zealand Health and Disability Ethics Committee guidance, formal ethics review was not required, as the project involved analysis of existing clinical data without alteration to patient care. At the time of treatment, patients provided written consent permitting the use of de-identified clinical data for audit and research purposes.

Results

Between 2007 and 2022, a total of 238 open and 436 arthroscopic stabilization procedures were performed by the senior author. Of these, 83 patients had both pre-operative and post-operative MRI imaging at 6 months or more and were therefore included in the analysis (22 open, which included 5 humeral avulsion glenohumeral ligament repairs and 17 Bankart repairs with capsular plication and 61 arthroscopic Bankart repairs). The majority of patients were male (89.3%). The mean age at pre-operative imaging was 20.05 years in the open group and 22.33 years in the arthroscopic group (Table I). The mean interval between pre-operative and post-operative MRI scans was 6 years and 2 months for the open stabilization group and 4 years and 9 months for the arthroscopic stabilization group. Discussion

This audit did not demonstrate consistent MRI evidence of subscapularis atrophy or fatty infiltration following either open or arthroscopic shoulder stabilization. Across both groups, quantitative measurements of muscle size and qualitative assessments of muscle quality remained largely unchanged on paired imaging. These findings suggest that structural deterioration of the subscapularis is not an inevitable consequence of stabilization surgery when the tendon is carefully handled and repaired. The results challenge the longstanding assumption that open subscapularis takedown inherently compromises muscle integrity.

Early concerns regarding post-operative dysfunction, particularly in high-demand populations, contributed to the shift toward arthroscopic techniques as the dominant surgical approach in recent decades.8,9,11,16,21 Arthroscopic stabilization avoids direct violation of the subscapularis and has been associated with earlier recovery of motion and return to function, particularly in younger athletic populations, reinforcing its widespread adoption.5,19,24 However, subsequent studies have reported variable findings, and it has remained unclear whether adverse morphologic changes of the subscapularis are an inherent consequence of open procedures or instead reflect surgical technique, patient selection, or injury-related factors. In this context, the present data are consistent with previous reports in which no consistent MRI evidence of atrophy or fatty degeneration was identified following open procedures when meticulous repair was undertaken.10,22

Open stabilization continues to have an important role in selected clinical scenarios, including patients with critical bone loss, collision athletes, and revision cases where a more robust capsular repair may be required.2,4,15 In this cohort, concerns regarding subscapularis compromise were not consistently supported by post-operative MRI findings and should not automatically preclude consideration of open procedures when clinically indicated.

Muscle morphology and quality were largely preserved in this cohort, with progression in Goutallier grade observed only in isolated cases. This aligns with prior studies reporting that fatty infiltration of the subscapularis is not an inevitable consequence of open takedown and may instead reflect patient-specific or injury-related factors.6,18,23,26 Atrophy grading also remained stable for most patients, further supporting the observation that adverse morphologic change was not consistently observed following stabilization surgery.

The robustness of these findings is supported by the high inter-rater reliability observed across all MRI-based measurements, with intraclass correlation coefficients consistently in the good-to-excellent range.13,25 The use of established grading systems and quantitative assessment methods further strengthens confidence in the reproducibility of the observed findings. In particular, the upper-to-total CSA ratio proved to be a reliable metric for assessing subscapularis atrophy and remained stable in the majority of patients.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. This was a retrospective audit, and only a small proportion of the total operative cohort underwent post-operative MRI, typically for clinical indications such as pain or recurrent symptoms. As a result, selection bias is likely, with patients experiencing uncomplicated recoveries under-represented.1,3,17 The findings, therefore, cannot be generalized to all patients undergoing shoulder stabilization. The sample size, while representing the largest comparative cohort with paired pre- and post-operative imaging to date, remains modest relative to the total number of stabilization procedures performed during the audit period and may be underpowered to detect small but clinically relevant differences, raising the possibility of a Type II error. Preservation of muscle morphology on MRI should therefore be interpreted with caution, as the absence of statistically significant change does not exclude the presence of subtle or functionally relevant differences that were not detectable within this sample. In addition, preservation of muscle morphology on MRI does not necessarily equate to preserved strength or functional outcome, and the timing and indication for post-operative imaging were not standardized.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, the absence of consistent structural deterioration even within a symptomatic imaging subgroup may be considered reassuring. Larger prospective studies with systematic post-operative imaging and functional correlation are required to more definitively characterize the impact of open and arthroscopic stabilization on subscapularis integrity. Until such data are available, these findings support a balanced, indication-driven approach to surgical decision-making rather than avoidance of open techniques based solely on concerns regarding subscapularis morphology.
