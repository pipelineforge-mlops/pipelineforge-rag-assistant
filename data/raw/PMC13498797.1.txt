Background

Circulating collagen turnover markers, including reC1M, PRO-C3, C3M, C4G, PRO-C8, TUM, PRO-C11, and PRO-C17, may serve as non-invasive biomarkers in gastric cancer (GC).

Methods

Eight serum collagen turnover markers were quantified in 74 pretreated GC patients from the SUNCASE trial and compared to 50 healthy donors. Markers were assessed at baseline and longitudinally after the first and second chemotherapy cycle. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated by AUROC analysis; prognostic value was assessed for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) using Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses.

Results

All markers except C4G were significantly elevated in GC patients vs. controls (p < 0.001). C3M showed the strongest diagnostic performance (AUROC 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.95; sensitivity 78%, specificity 77%). Elevated reC1M (HR 2.70), C3M (HR 2.33), PRO-C11 (HR 2.14), and TUM (HR 2.09) were independently prognostic of shorter OS. None of the established tumor markers (CA 19-9, CEA, CA 72-4) retained prognostic significance. Longitudinally, a >20% reduction in PRO-C3 and PRO-C11 after the first chemotherapy cycle was associated with longer OS.

Conclusion

Collagen turnover markers reC1M, C3M, TUM, and PRO-C11 are independent prognostic markers for OS in advanced GC. C3M demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing GC patients from healthy controls.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) arises from the epithelial lining of the stomach and is a prevalent form of gastrointestinal cancer. It is commonly categorized into subtypes based on location, such as cardia and non-cardia gastric cancer, while adenocarcinoma is the histological most common type. GC ranks fifth for incidence and fourth for mortality with over 1 million new cases and 768,000 deaths worldwide in 2020 and numbers are predicted to rise by 2040 with 1.77 million new cases and 1.27 million deaths worldwide. Acknowledged risk factors are carcinogen agents such as tobacco, meat, alcohol consumption but also obesity. 1 Survival rate strongly depend on the stage, while patient in an advanced stage have a dismal prognosis with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%. Unfortunately, the majority of patients are diagnosed in advanced stages (Union for International Cancer Control, UICC stage III and IV), when patients show symptoms. 2 Surgical resection combined with perioperative chemotherapy according the FLOT regime (5-Fluorouracil, Leucovorin, Oxaliplatin, Docetaxel) is the only curative treatment for GC; however, only patients up to stage III are eligible. Recurrence rates for gastric cancer after curative surgery typically range around 36.8%. For those metastatic disease, systemic therapy, including chemotherapy, is the standard approach, and immunotherapy has recently shown potential to improve outcomes. The addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g. pembrolizumab) or HER2 inhibitors (e.g. trastuzumab), depending on the PD-1 (programmed cell death protein 1) or HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2) status respectively, can extend survival.2,3

There is an urgent need for biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity to guide diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment decisions. Commonly studied tumor markers for GC include carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) and CA 72-4, which are elevated in a subset of patients and are associated with prognosis. 4 However, the sensitivity and specificity of these markers vary widely, and certain genetic factors, such as the absence of Lewis antigen, limit the utility of CA 19-9 for some individuals. 5

Recently, the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly the extracellular matrix (ECM), has gained attention in the search for novel biomarkers due to its significant role in tumor progression and response to immunotherapy.6,7 In GC, the ECM undergoes extensive remodeling, characterized by a desmoplastic response, which includes various types of collagens with specific structural and biochemical roles. 8 The collagens in the ECM are distinct, serving specialized structural, biochemical, and biomechanical functions within the tissue. 9 Type IV and type VIII collagen are predominantly located in the basement membrane zone, where they play key roles in maintaining epithelial cell polarity and supporting angiogenesis. In contrast, type I, type III, and type XI collagen are found in the interstitial matrix zone, produced by fibroblasts within the underlying stroma. Type XVII collagen is anchored in the epithelial cell membrane, directly linking the epithelium to the basement membrane (Figure 1). Under normal physiological conditions, collagen synthesis and degradation are tightly regulated to maintain tissue homeostasis.10,11 However, excessive collagen turnover and remodeling contribute to the formation of a desmoplastic tumor stroma, which acts a physical barrier, limiting the penetration of therapeutic agents and reducing their efficacy. 12 Collagen remodeling and degradation is done by various proteases, including matrix metalloproteases (MMP) produced by tumor cells and macrophages, as well as serine proteases such as neutrophil elastase and granzyme B (GzB), which are primarily released by T cells and NK cells. Another important player in the tumor stroma are cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), which promote tumor progression by enhancing cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis and also mediate immunosuppression (Figure 1).13,14Figure 1. The schematic illustration depicts the distribution of various collagens within the tumor microenvironment (TME) of gastric cancer (GC). The collagen turnover markers analyzed in this study include reC1M, a fragment generated by MMP-mediated degradation of type I collagen; C3M, a fragment produced by MMP-driven degradation of type III collagen; PRO-C3, the N-terminal pro-peptide of type III procollagen released during collagen synthesis and fibrogenesis; TUM (Tumstatin), the NC1 domain of type IV collagen; C4G, a fragment generated by GzB-mediated degradation of type IV collagen; PRO-C8 (Vastatin), the NC1 domain of type VIII collagen; PRO-C11, the N-terminal pro-peptide of type XI collagen released during synthesis and fibrogenesis; and PRO-C17, the ectodomain of type XVII collagen. Collagen fragments generated during ECM remodeling are released into the bloodstream and can be measured as potential non-invasive biomarkers. In this study, non-invasive serum biomarkers related to the remodeling of type I, III, IV, VIII, XI, and XVII collagen were quantified in GC patients. Specifically, we analyzed reC1M (a fragment of MMP-degraded type I collagen),15,16 C3M (a fragment of MMP-degraded type III collagen), PRO-C3 (the amino terminal pro-peptide of type III procollagen released during synthesis and fibrogenesis), TUM (Tumstatin, the non-collagenous carboxyterminal NC1 domain of type VIII collagen), C4G (a fragment of GzB-degraded type IV collagen), PRO-C8 (Vastatin, the NC1 domain of type VIII collagen), PRO-C11 (the amino terminal pro-peptide of type XI procollagen released during synthesis and fibrogenesis), and PRO-C17 (the ectodomain of type XVII collagen) (Figure 1). We then assessed their diagnostic, prognostic, and therapy monitoring value in a clinically well-characterized cohort of GC patients enrolled in a multicenter, randomized clinical trial.17–21

Material and methods

Patient and healthy donor samples

Serum samples from 74 patients with gastric cancer were obtained from the biobank of the SUNCASE trial (“Sunitinib (SUN) to FOLFIRI versus FOLFIRI in patients with chemorefractory advanced adenocarcinoma of the stomach or lower esophagus: a randomized, placebo-controlled phase II AIO trial with serum biomarker program”). All patients had an indication for palliative chemotherapy with no option for curative-intent surgery. Blood was collected prospectively at three time points: prior to treatment initiation (baseline), following the first chemotherapy cycle, and following the second chemotherapy cycle. Patients were recruited between November 2009 and July 2013. Paired serum samples were available for 48 patients after the first and for 33 patients after the second chemotherapy cycle.

For oncologic therapy, patients were randomized to receive 6-week cycles including FOLFIRI plus sodium folinate (Na-FOLFIRI) once every two weeks and SUN or placebo continuously for four weeks followed by a 2-week rest period. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from study inclusion to death, while progression-free survival (PFS), progressive disease (PD), partial response (PR), and stable disease (SD) were defined according to RECIST version 1.1 (Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumors, 2009). The study was approved by 17 German independent ethics committees, met all regulatory requirements, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki).

Serum samples of healthy donors were obtained from the blood donor center at the University Medical Center of the Johannes Gutenberg-University, while only age and sex of the donors were reported in line with the German data privacy act. Every human participant consent to study biomarkers in their sera. We have previously published this cohort as a reference 22.

Quantification of the collagen turnover markers by ELISA

All collagen turnover biomarkers were measured using competitive ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Nordic Bioscience, Herlev, Denmark). The technical evaluation and assay development for each biomarker have been described elsewhere: reC1M (cat. no. 1000AG01, NordicC1MTM), a fragment generated by MMP-degraded type I collagen; C3M (cat. no. 1200AG01, NordicC3MTM), a fragment generated by MMP-degraded type III collagen; PRO-C3 (cat. no. 1700AF01, NordicPRO-C3TM), the pro-peptide of type III collagen released during synthesis and fibrogenesis; TUM (cat. no. 4800AG01, NordicTUMTM), Tumstatin, the NC1 domain of type VIII collagen; C4G (cat. no. 1027CE01, NordicC4GTM), a fragment generated by GzB-degraded type IV collagen; PRO-C8 (cat. no. 0106AE01, NordicPRO-C8TM), Vastatin, the NC1 domain of type VIII collagen; PRO-C11 (cat. no. 1020AE01, NordicPRO-C11TM), the pro-peptide of type XI collagen released during synthesis and fibrogenesis; PRO-C17 (cat. no. 1084AD01, NordicPRO-C17TM), the type XVII collagen ectodomain.

In brief, the ELISA procedure followed these general steps: A 96-well streptavidin-coated microtiter plate was coated with a biotinylated peptide dissolved in assay buffer and incubated for 30 minutes at 20°C in the dark. The plates were then washed five times with washing buffer (20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.2). Next, 20 μl of calibrator peptide, quality control samples, or serum samples were added in duplicate to the appropriate wells, followed by 100 μl of horseradish peroxidase-conjugated monoclonal antibody solution in assay buffer. The plate was incubated for 1–20 hours at either 4°C or 20°C, depending on the assay, followed by another washing step. Then, 100 μl of tetramethylbenzidine (Kem-En-Tec, Taastrup, Denmark; cat. #438OH) was added, and the plate was incubated for 15 minutes at 20°C before stopping the reaction with 100 μl of 1% H2SO4. Throughout all incubation steps, the plates were shaken at 300 rpm. Finally, optical density was measured at 450 nm with a 650 nm reference, and a four-parameter mathematical fit model was applied to generate the calibration curve.

Statistics

Group comparisons of collagen turnover marker levels between patients with GC and healthy controls, as well as before and after each CTX cycle, were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Since the analysis was exploratory, no adjustments for multiple testing were applied. The diagnostic accuracy of the markers was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) to assess their ability to distinguish individual cancer types from healthy donors. Sensitivity and specificity-balanced cutoffs were determined using the Youden Index. In the absence of established clinical cut-off values for these novel biomarkers, a median split was applied as a data-driven approach to stratify patients into ‘low’ and ‘high’ groups for OS and PFS analyses, while survival differences were assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to determine the independent prognostic value of the markers, adjusting for age, sex, height, weight, baseline CA 19-9 and CEA levels, and presence of metastases. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 and reported as follows: * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The cohort included 74 patients with GC and 50 healthy controls (Table 1). The healthy controls were younger, with a median age of 46 years (IQR 38–54), compared to the patients, who had a median age of 59 years (IQR 49–67). Sex distribution was similar between the groups, with men comprising 56% of the patient group and 52% of the healthy controls. Most patients were diagnosed with gastric cardia adenocarcinoma (47%), followed by gastric non-cardia adenocarcinoma (38%), while the tumor location was unspecified in 11 patients (15%). All patients had advanced disease, with the majority presenting with distant metastases (M1, 84%). The median OS was 204 days (IQR 97–396), and the median PFS was 108 days (IQR 42–192). It is plausible that deregulation of collagen turnover markers occurred primarily in patients who responded to treatment. To investigate this, patients were stratified into PD versus SD and PR after the first chemotherapy cycle. With the exception of PRO-C11, PRO-C17 and C4G, biomarker levels were significantly lower in patients with SD or PR compared to those with PD as seen before for patients with biliary tract cancer (BTC) (Figure 3(b)). 22

Since collagen turnover markers originate from specific collagen fragments and contribute to the remodeling of distinct ECM subdomains, we examined the extent to which these markers correlate with each other.

A strong correlation was observed between TUM and PRO-C8 (r = 0.93), as well as between C3M and TUM (r = 0.89), while minor correlations were found between PRO-C8 and PRO-C11 (r = 0.49) as well as PRO-C11 and C3M (r = 0.5). Notably, no correlation was found between the established tumor biomarkers CA 19-9, CEA, and CA 72-4 and the collagen turnover markers (Figure 4). Collagen turnover markers are predictors of overall survival

Given that the biomarkers were elevated in tumor patients, we assessed their prognostic value for OS and PFS in our cohort (Figure 5). Patients were stratified into “low” and “high” groups based on a median split of marker levels. Kaplan-Meier analysis identified reC1M as the strongest prognostic marker for OS (log-rank p = 0.0005), followed by C3M, TUM, PRO-C8, PRO-C11, CA 19-9, and CEA. Patients with low reC1M levels had a median OS of 355 days (95% CI: 284–426), whereas those with high reC1M levels had a significantly shorter median OS of 136 days (95% CI: 85–187). Given the strong prognostic performance of reC1M, we assessed whether combining reC1M with the established biomarker CA19-9 (both classified as high by median split) could enhance predictive accuracy. Discussion

The tumor stroma of solid cancers gains successively attention in oncological research as it represents a distinct tumor microenvironment which regulates tumor growth and metastasis. 10 Desmoplastic stroma with a high abundancy of collagens is a hallmark of gastric adenocarcinoma. 25

In this study, we assessed serum markers associated with stromal turnover of type I collagen (reC1M), type III collagen (PRO-C3, C3M), type VIII collagen (PRO-C8, TUM), type XI collagen (PRO-C11), and type XVII collagen (PRO-C17) in patients with GC for the first time. All collagen turnover markers were significantly elevated in patients compared to healthy donors, whereas the marker for type IV collagen (C4G) showed no elevation (Figure 2). This aligns with our previous study, in which the same collagen markers were evaluated in a cohort of patients with BTC, revealing C4G as the only marker that was not elevated. 22 Interestingly, only half of the gastric cancer patients exhibited elevated levels of the established tumor markers, with CA 19-9 detected in 55%, CA 72-4 in 42%, and CEA in 53% of cases.

For diagnostic purposes, novel biomarkers should be independent of established ones to provide additional diagnostic value. Therefore, we examined whether collagen turnover markers correlated with each other and with the established tumor markers CA 19-9, CEA, and CA 72-4. Notably, none of the collagen turnover markers correlated with the established tumor markers, which suggests a potential additional diagnostic benefit. However, since all collagen markers are released during extracellular matrix remodeling, we observed strong correlations among some of them, such as PRO-C8 with TUM and TUM with C3M. These findings prompted us to further evaluate the diagnostic potential of individual collagen turnover markers in distinguishing gastric cancer patients from healthy individuals.

C3M demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance in distinguishing gastric cancer patients from healthy individuals, achieving an AUROC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.81–0.95) (Table 2).

Using a balanced cut-off of 12.1 ng/mL, it reached a sensitivity of 78% and a specificity of 77%. However, this performance was lower than that of PRO-C3 in patients with BTC, which exhibited an AUROC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.95–0.99). 22

We investigated whether the markers were influenced by the first and second cycles of CTX (Figure 3). Overall, no consistent changes in serum markers were observed, except for PRO-C17 after the first chemotherapy cycle and C4G after the second cycle. These observations may be influenced by selection bias, as serum markers could not be assessed in patients who had died. Survival data indicated that patients with lower serum marker levels had longer overall survival. Therefore, it is possible that only patients with relatively low and stable serum marker levels—showing little to no dynamic changes following CTX —were available for assessment after the first and second treatment cycles. Especially C4G should be interpreted with caution, as its levels did not differ between GC patients and healthy controls. This was once more in line with findings in cohort of patients with BTC who also received two CTX cycles. There is plausibility that only biomarker levels of patients who responded to CTX (SD and PR) would have decreased (Figure 3). Except PRO-C11, PRO-C17 and C4G, all collagen turnover markers were lower in patients who were diagnosed with SD or PR after first CTX compared to patients with PD. Whether collagen turnover markers provide diagnostic value for evaluating therapeutic response in patients with GC should be assessed in an appropriate external control cohort.

Although data on their circulating half-life are not yet available to the best of our knowledge, the half-life is expected to be relatively short, as these markers are predominantly cleared via the kidneys. In line with this, Genovese et al. demonstrated that urinary clearance of PRO-C3 and C3M decreases with advancing chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage. 26 Consequently, collagen markers released during desmoplastic stroma degradation by e.g. CTX are likely cleared rapidly, thereby reflecting tumor burden more directly than stromal degradation.

One of the key roles of biomarkers in oncology is their prognostic value in predicting outcomes or interim events. 27 Elevated serum levels of biomarkers associated with the turnover of type I, III, VIII, and XI collagens (reC1M, C3M, TUM, PRO-C8, PRO-C11) were found to be prognostic OS in patients with GC (Figure 5). Among these markers, reC1M, followed by C3M, demonstrated the strongest prognostic value for OS, with p-values of 0.0005 and 0.0021, respectively. In contrast, the prognostic value of PRO-C11 for OS was borderline (p = 0.0484), which may be attributed to the high variability in its measurements, as indicated by the comparatively high coefficient of variation across all PRO-C11 values (S3 Table).

In addition to collagen turnover markers, the well-established biomarkers CA 19-9 and CEA also showed prognostic significance for OS. However, combining reC1M with CA 19-9 did not enhance prognostic accuracy.

In our previous study on patients with BTC, PRO-C8 demonstrated the strongest predictive performance for OS, with a p-value of 0.0002. Notably, PRO-C8 was also a significant predictor of PFS, a capability not observed for any of the biomarkers in the present study. However, in this study, PRO-C8 ranked fourth in prognostic performance, following reC1M, C3M, and TUM. These findings highlight the need to evaluate collagen turnover markers individually for each solid tumor type, as their prognostic value may differ across cancer entities.

Another important question was whether patients who experienced a decline in biomarker levels had longer OS after the first cycle of CTX. Patients with a ≥20% reduction in PRO-C3 and PRO-C11 levels had significantly longer OS compared to non-responders without such reductions. However, in patients with BTC, none of the assessed biomarkers could distinguish between responders (≥20% reduction of biomarkers after first cycle CTX) and non-responders. Interestingly, recent findings indicate that PRO-C3 is produced by CAF in pancreatic cancer patients, whereas pancreatic fibroblasts from non-malignant tissue do not express it. 28 This suggests that PRO-C3 could serve as a marker of CAF activity.

The associations of the assessed biomarkers with OS remained significant for reC1M, C3M, PRO-C11 and TUM, adjusting for key risk factors that may influence patient survival, including age, gender, BMI, levels of established tumor markers (CA 19-9 and CEA), the presence of metastases and T and N category. Notably, the established biomarkers CA 19-9 and CEA, as well as PRO-C8, lost significance following adjustment. This is interesting as PRO-C8 proved to be the most robust marker in the last study. 22

Since reC1M and C3M stand out for their diagnostic and predictive performance, it is important to first examine their pathophysiological mechanisms. reC1M is a collagen fragment and neoantigen released into the serum when type I collagen is degraded by MMP-2 and MMP-13.29–31 MMP are a family of zinc-dependent enzymes essential for ECM remodeling. While MMP-2 is predominantly upregulated in advanced stages of liver fibrosis, MMP-13 plays a counter-regulatory role by breaking down collagen fibers, facilitating ECM turnover, and potentially aiding fibrosis resolution.32,33 Consequently, reC1M is released during both fibrogenic and fibrolytic processes within the ECM. To the best of our knowledge, reC1M has not yet been quantified in patients with GC and has only been investigated in our recent study on patients with BTC. In BTC patients, its diagnostic performance (AUROC 0.88, 95% CI 0.95; 0.99) and prognostic value (p-value 0.002) were moderate, ranking below PRO-C3 and C3M. There is only one study in which reC1M has been quantified in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD). Increased degradation of type I collagen and excessive formation of type IV collagen are strongly associated with stricturing (Montreal B2) Crohn’s disease and can effectively distinguish it from inflammatory (Montreal B1) disease. 34

C3M is generated by the degradation of type III collagen by MMP-9 during ECM remodeling. Its levels were assessed in a cohort of patients with gastric cancer and were significantly elevated compared to healthy controls. Additionally, C3M demonstrated a pharmacodynamic response to therapy, aligning with our findings. 35 In patients with BTC, among the other collagen turnover markers also assessed in this study, C3M showed the second-best prognostic performance for diagnosis (AUROC: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.78–0.93) and overall survival (p = 0.002), following PRO-C8. 22 In metastatic breast cancer, elevated C3M levels are associated with shorter time-to-progression and reduced overall survival, supporting its role as a prognostic biomarker. There is also evidence for its diagnostic value in colorectal cancer, where C3M levels are elevated in tumor tissues compared to non-neoplastic tissues. 36

This suggests its potential to identify patients at risk of developing colorectal cancer within the adenoma-carcinoma sequence. Beyond oncology, C3M has been implicated as a biomarker in non-malignant diseases. It significantly differentiates Crohn’s disease from ulcerative colitis, with higher levels correlating with more severe phenotypes such as penetrating Crohn’s disease. 37 Moreover, in patients undergoing infliximab treatment, C3M levels distinguish responders from non-responders, highlighting its potential for monitoring treatment efficacy. 38 C3M also holds promise beyond gastrointestinal diseases. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), its levels are elevated and correlate with disease activity measures such as DAS28 and VAS pain, suggesting its utility as a marker of synovial turnover and disease severity. 39

A decline of PRO-C3 and PRO-C11 levels (≥20%) after first CTX was prognostic for OS (Figure 6), suggesting that the dynamic changes in collagen markers may have prognostic value beyond their absolute levels. However, PRO-C11 levels did not differ after the first CTX when comparing patients with SD or PR to those with PD, which may be attributed to the high variability in PRO-C11 measurements, which could obscure potential differences between the groups (S3 table).

Given the retrospective nature of the study design, several limitations must be acknowledged. Clinical data on chronic parenchymal damage, such as liver cirrhosis/fibrosis, lung fibrosis, scleroderma, or chronic kidney disease, were not available for the included patients, which may have influenced collagen turnover marker levels. Additionally, the healthy donors were younger than the tumor patients (46 years [IQR 38; 54] vs. 59 years [IQR 49; 67]), potentially complicating comparisons, as biomarker levels can increase with age. Furthermore, the cohort consisted exclusively of patients with advanced or metastatic gastric cancer receiving palliative chemotherapy. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to earlier disease stages or to the broader gastric cancer population. Additionally, no formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was a retrospective, exploratory study using an existing biobank cohort. The sample size was therefore determined by the availability of suitable archived serum samples from the SUNCASE trial.

Due to data privacy regulations, we cannot provide further characteristics of the healthy donors beyond sex and age, as only these details were reported by the blood bank. Furthermore, given the large number of statistical tests performed for group comparisons, the reported p-values should be interpreted with caution. Most analyses were conducted as pairwise comparisons, and no adjustments for multiple testing were applied.

Since sunitinib (SUN) showed no additional benefit to chemotherapy, no subgroup analysis was performed for patients treated with this tyrosine kinase inhibitor. 21 Furthermore, we did not have access to tumor biopsies of the included patients for mechanistic investigations regarding the origin of the markers within the TME. Given the relatively small sample size of 74 gastric cancer patients and 50 healthy controls, the findings of this study should be considered exploratory. External validation in a larger, independent cohort is warranted before any clinical conclusions can be drawn.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the collagen type I and III derived biomarkers reC1M and C3M had the best diagnostic performance to discriminate between patients with GC and healthy individuals and were prognostic for OS in patients with GC. The prognostic performance of reC1M for OS did not improve when combined with the established tumor marker CA19-9. Further studies, including external validation in larger independent cohorts, are needed before these markers can be considered for clinical application.
