Background

Ganglion cell + inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) atrophy reflects global neurodegeneration and disability in multiple sclerosis (MS). The effect of ocrelizumab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody and highly-effective disease modifying therapy (DMT), on rates of GCIPL atrophy in people with MS remains understudied.

Objectives

To assess if ocrelizumab attenuates retinal atrophy rates, particularly GCIPL atrophy, and change in clinical measures in people with relapsing MS (PwRMS), and how this compares to natalizumab (another highly-effective DMT), as well as the related anti-CD20 therapy rituximab.

Methods

PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab, natalizumab, or rituximab, untreated people with progressive MS (PwPMS), and healthy controls underwent Cirrus HD-OCT, 100% and 2.5%-contrast letter acuity (LA) assessments longitudinally. MS participants underwent Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) assessments. Statistical analyses used linear mixed-effects regression models adjusting for age, sex, race, disease duration, optic neuritis history, and interval between DMT initiation and OCT monitoring; accounting for within-subject, inter-eye correlations.

Results

In ocrelizumab-treated PwRMS, GCIPL atrophy rate was −0.15 µm/year (SE = 0.03, p < 0.001, n = 88 [168 eyes]), similar to GCIPL atrophy in natalizumab- or rituximab-treated PwRMS, and healthy controls, although slower than in untreated PwPMS (−0.35 µm/year, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001, n = 40 [78 eyes]). EDSS and LA scores remained stable in ocrelizumab-treated PwRMS, and similar to natalizumab- or rituximab-treated PwRMS.

Conclusion

GCIPL atrophy is attenuated and clinical function relatively stable in PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is primarily considered to be an autoimmune demyelinating disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), 1 with axonal and neuronal degeneration regarded as underlying disability accumulation. 2 Disease modifying therapies (DMTs) are utilized to modulate or suppress immune function in people with multiple sclerosis (PwMS), thereby decreasing neuroinflammatory activity, related neuroaxonal degeneration and subsequent disability accumulation. Ocrelizumab is a humanized anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that is approved for use as a disease modifying therapy (DMT) in relapsing multiple sclerosis (RMS) and primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS).3–5 Previous studies have found that ocrelizumab attenuates rates of brain atrophy and clinical progression in PwMS.4–6 However, the effect of ocrelizumab on rates of retinal atrophy in PwMS remains understudied.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a noninvasive and reliable imaging technique allowing the visualization and quantification of discrete retinal layers. 7 The inner retina represents an opportune location to study the effects of DMTs in PwMS since optic nerve demyelinating lesions occur in almost all PwMS, regardless of optic neuritis (ON) history.8,9 Ganglion cell + inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) atrophy of the inner retina mirrors whole brain atrophy, and in particular cortical and subcortical gray matter atrophy over time, reflecting global neurodegeneration and disability accumulation in PwMS.10,11 Furthermore, rates of GCIPL atrophy have been shown to be differentially attenuated by DMTs in PwMS. 12 It has been previously shown that PwMS treated with natalizumab or rituximab, high efficacy DMTs, exhibited rates of GCIPL atrophy that were comparable to healthy controls (HCs). 13 Investigating rates of retinal atrophy, particularly GCIPL atrophy, in PwMS treated with ocrelizumab may offer further insight into the degree of neuroprotection that ocrelizumab may provide to PwMS.

The objectives of this study were to (1) assess whether, and the degree to which, ocrelizumab attenuates or reduces rates of retinal atrophy, particularly GCIPL atrophy, in people with RMS (PwRMS), and how this effect compares to natalizumab (another highly effective DMT), as well as the related chimeric anti-CD20 therapy rituximab, and (2) to assess whether, and the degree to which, ocrelizumab modulates or reduces clinical progression, as measured by changes in expanded disability status scale (EDSS), 100% high-contrast letter acuity (LA), and 2.5% low-contrast LA scores, in PwRMS.

Methods

Participants

PwMS were recruited from the Johns Hopkins MS Center and MS diagnosis was confirmed by the treating neurologist based on the 2017 revised McDonald criteria. 14 Participants were classified as having RMS, secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS), or PPMS. 15 PwRMS due to commence ocrelizumab, existing PwRMS attending the center who were already receiving ocrelizumab and undergoing all study procedures, or people with SPMS or PPMS not receiving any DMT (collectively referred to as people with progressive multiple sclerosis [PwPMS]; as a comparator cohort) were recruited. A prospective healthy control (HC) cohort undergoing OCT scans, and also cohorts of PwRMS treated with natalizumab or rituximab undergoing all study procedures were recruited retrospectively for additional comparative purposes. OCT scans and EDSS evaluations by Neurostatus-certified raters were performed at Time 0 (defined as the time of first OCT scan and clinical evaluations after re-baselining participants following 6 months of DMT treatment in the ocrelizumab, natalizumab, and rituximab cohorts [to allow adequate time for DMT optimization] or as the time of first OCT scan and clinical assessment in the untreated PwPMS and HC cohorts) and every 6 months (±14 days). 100% high-contrast LA assessments evaluated with retro-illuminated 100% high-contrast Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study charts at 4 m, and 2.5% low-contrast LA assessments, evaluated with 2.5% low-contrast Sloan letter charts at 2 meters, were also performed during study visits. Disease subtype (classified as RMS, SPMS, or PPMS), disease duration (from first reported symptom onset to Time 0), history of ON (including the date and side of occurrence), and type of DMT treatment were recorded. Participants with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia, glaucoma, refractive errors greater than ±6 diopters, acute ON within 6 months of first OCT scan or during study follow-up, other confounding neurological or ophthalmological disorders, follow-up time less than 6 months, or who were <18 or >70 years old at enrollment were excluded.

OCT

All study participants underwent Cirrus HD-OCT scans (Carl Zeiss, Dublin, CA, USA; Model 5000) by experienced technicians under low light conditions without pupillary dilation. Optic disc scans were acquired with the standard 200 × 200 Optic Disc Cube protocols, as described previously. 7 Macular scans were acquired using the 512 × 128 Macular Cube protocols, as described elsewhere. 7 Segmentation of the GCIPL, inner nuclear layer (INL), and outer nuclear layer (ONL) was performed using a validated Johns Hopkins University developed segmentation algorithm,16,17 while segmentation of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) utilized standard Cirrus HD-OCT segmentation. 7 All OCT scans underwent quality control assessments according to OSCAR-IB criteria, 18 with scans violating these criteria being excluded. Current reporting adheres to the APOSTEL 2.0 guidelines. 19

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab, natalizumab, or rituximab were re-baselined following 6 months of DMT treatment in order to allow adequate time for DMT optimization. Comparisons of age at Time 0 (the time of first OCT scan and clinical assessments after re-baselining participants following 6 months of DMT treatment in the ocrelizumab, natalizumab, and rituximab cohorts [to allow adequate time for DMT optimization] or at the time of first OCT scan and clinical assessment in the untreated PwPMS and HC cohorts) between study cohorts were performed using a one-way ANOVA test with Bonferroni pairwise comparisons. The Chi-square test was used to determine differences between cohorts for sex and race. The Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's pairwise comparisons was used to assess differences between the study cohorts in disease duration at Time 0, follow-up time (interval between initiation of OCT monitoring at Time 0 and final OCT scan), gap time (interval between the initiation of DMT treatment and OCT monitoring from Time 0), retinal layer thicknesses at Time 0, and clinical measures at Time 0. Fisher's exact test was used to assess differences in ON history. Linear mixed-effects regression models adjusting for age, sex, race, disease duration, ON history, and gap time; accounting for within-subject, inter-eye correlations were used to examine annual retinal atrophy rates and annual changes in monocular 100% high-contrast LA and monocular 2.5% low-contrast LA. A linear mixed-effects regression model adjusting for age, sex, race, disease duration, and gap time, accounting for within-subject correlations was used to examine annual changes in EDSS scores. Participants remained in their defined cohorts, with no overlap of the same participant being in more than one cohort. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethical approval and data availability

This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Review Board and written informed consent was provided by all participants. Data related to this study are available based upon reasonable requests, and subject to appropriate inter-institutional data-sharing agreements.

Role of the funding source

The funders of this study had no role in study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, drafting or reviewing this manuscript, or in the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Eighty eight PwRMS (168 eyes) receiving ocrelizumab, 90 PwRMS (168 eyes) receiving natalizumab, 24 PwRMS (46 eyes) receiving rituximab, 40 untreated PwPMS (78 eyes), and 45 HCs (83 eyes) were included (Figure 1, Table 1). Demographic information at Time 0, including age and disease duration, race, sex, ON history, follow-up time per eye (interval between initiation of OCT monitoring at Time 0 and final OCT scan), and gap time (interval between DMT treatment initiation and commencement of OCT monitoring at Time 0) are detailed in Table 1. At Time 0 age and disease duration were lower and proportion of eyes with ON history was higher in PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab relative to untreated PwPMS (age = 40.6 vs. 58.6 years [p < 0.001], disease duration = 10.1 vs. 18.0 years [p < 0.001], eyes with ON history = 33.3% vs. 16.7% [p = 0.01]). Race differed between PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab (63.6% White American, 31.8% Black American, 4.6% Other race) and PwRMS receiving natalizumab (84.4% White American, 15.6% Black American, 0.0% Other race [p = 0.003]), untreated PwPMS (85.0% White American, 12.5% Black American, 2.5% Other race [p = 0.048]), and HCs (68.9% White American, 13.3% Black American, 17.8% Other race [p = 0.01]). Follow-up time per eye was higher in PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab relative to PwRMS receiving rituximab (3.1 vs. 2.6 years [p = 0.02]). Gap time between DMT treatment initiation and Time 0 OCT was shorter in PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab (1.2 years) as compared to PwRMS receiving natalizumab (2.2 years [p < 0.001]) and PwRMS receiving rituximab (1.7 years [p = 0.02]).

Rates of retinal layer atrophy

Of note, *p-values denote p-values for retinal layer atrophy within cohorts, while **p-values denote p-values for differences in retinal layer atrophy between cohorts.

GCIPL and other retinal layer atrophy rates in the different study cohorts are detailed in Table 3 and illustrated in Figure 2. The rate of GCIPL atrophy in PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab was −0.15 µm/year (SE = 0.03, *p < 0.001). This was similar to the rates of GCIPL atrophy of PwRMS treated with natalizumab (−0.20 µm/year, SE = 0.03, *p < 0.001), rituximab (−0.26 µm/year, SE = 0.07, *p < 0.001), as well as HCs (−0.11 µm/year, SE = 0.04, *p = 0.01). However, it was slower (**p < 0.001) than the rate of GCIPL atrophy of the untreated PwPMS cohort (−0.35 µm/year, SE = 0.04, *p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study we found that the rate of GCIPL atrophy in PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab was comparable to HCs, PwRMS treated with natalizumab, as well as PwRMS treated with rituximab. Ocrelizumab, natalizumab, and rituximab likely impart a high degree of neuroprotection in PwRMS by suppressing focal neuroinflammatory disease activity. Given the relationships between GCIPL atrophy, global neurodegeneration, and whole brain, particularly gray matter atrophy, our findings add further support that ocrelizumab may impart neuroprotection in PwRMS. This is further supported by a previous study which demonstrated that people with relapsing MS treated with ocrelizumab exhibit global and regional brain volume loss similar to HCs. 20 Furthermore, a 3-year open-label extension of the combined OPERA I and OPERA II phase 3b trials demonstrated that PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab maintained near complete suppression of new brain MRI lesion activity and those who initiated ocrelizumab earlier exhibited less whole brain volume loss, as compared to those who were initially treated with interferon-β-1a. 6 Additionally, the phase 3b ENSEMBLE trial found that most PwRMS exhibited no evidence of disease activity over 4 years of ocrelizumab treatment, as well as serum neurofilament light chain levels that were reduced to healthy control ranges. 21 Our findings also provide further support that the high efficacy DMTs, natalizumab and rituximab, similarly might impart neuroprotection in PwRMS, as demonstrated by the comparable rates of GCIPL atrophy in PwRMS receiving natalizumab or rituximab, as compared to PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab, as well as HCs. These findings align with prior studies which found that PwRMS receiving natalizumab or rituximab had similar rates of GCIPL atrophy to HCs.12,13 Both natalizumab and rituximab have previously exhibited that they are highly effective DMTs for suppressing MS disease activity in relapsing forms of MS.22–27 Although the rate of GCIPL atrophy in PwRMS receiving rituximab was not significantly different to PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab, it was numerically higher. However, for contextual interpretation it should be noted that the cohort of PwRMS treated with rituximab was retrospectively recruited, was smaller, and the PwRMS in the rituximab cohort may have had fewer treatment options at the time or prior to the initiation of rituximab. Additionally, EDSS at Time 0 was numerically higher in the rituximab treated PwRMS cohort, as compared to the ocrelizumab and natalizumab treated PwRMS cohorts, perhaps reflecting a greater degree of neurodegenerative disease burden. The untreated PwPMS cohort exhibited faster rates of GCIPL atrophy when compared to the ocrelizumab treated PwRMS cohort, likely reflecting a greater degree of neurodegeneration in this untreated cohort.

PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab exhibited rates of INL atrophy that were comparable to HCs, and to PwRMS treated with natalizumab and rituximab. However, the rate of INL atrophy of the untreated PwPMS cohort was faster than the PwRMS treated ocrelizumab cohort. INL (and ONL) atrophy rates have been shown to be faster in progressive forms of MS as compared to RMS, are thought to reflect neurodegeneration that is potentially more prominent in progressive forms of the disease than RMS, and have been noted to be generally unmodulated by conventional, anti-inflammatory (including high-potency) DMTs in the past. 28 The faster rates of INL atrophy in untreated PwPMS, as compared to PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab, natalizumab, or rituximab may be due to differences in the prevailing pathobiology operative in progressive MS (PPMS and SPMS), relative to RMS. Interestingly, a prior study investigating INL volume atrophy rates in people with PPMS treated with ocrelizumab deemed that people with PPMS who clinically responded to ocrelizumab treatment exhibited slower INL volume atrophy rates as compared to those who did not clinically respond to treatment. 29 However, this area remains largely understudied and was not investigated in the current study. The ONL atrophy rate in PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab was comparable to PwRMS treated with natalizumab, rituximab, and to untreated PwPMS, although borderline faster than in HCs. ONL atrophy rates have also been found to be generally unmodulated by conventional anti-inflammatory DMTs. 28 While ONL atrophy rates have also been suggested to be faster in progressive forms of MS as compared to RMS, this was not a notable finding in the current study. 28 Annual pRNFL atrophy of the ocrelizumab treated PwRMS cohort was borderline slower than in HCs. Similar to GCIPL atrophy, pRNFL atrophy is reflective of global neurodegeneration in PwMS 30 (although GCIPL atrophy may be more sensitive to disability accumulation and visual dysfunction 31 ). The comparable rates of pRNFL atrophy in PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab and HCs further supports a likely imparted neuroprotection in this cohort. Furthermore, the findings of this study highlight that examining OCT-derived retinal thinning, particularly GCIPL thinning, may have utility as a sensitive neuroaxonal outcome measure when assessing therapies in MS. OCT-derived retinal thinning potentially has useful applications as a treatment outcome measure both in future clinical trials and in real-world clinical practice.

Importantly, PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab exhibited no significant clinical progression, as estimated by annual changes in EDSS, 100% high-contrast LA, and 2.5% low-contrast LA scores. In this study, similar findings were also observed for the most part in PwRMS receiving natalizumab, PwRMS receiving rituximab, as well as untreated PwPMS. While annual change in 100% high-contrast LA trended toward being lower in PwRMS receiving rituximab as compared to ocrelizumab, this finding was not observed with 2.5% low-contrast LA. The ocrelizumab treated PwRMS cohort was the only cohort in this study to exhibit significant increases in 2.5% low-contrast LA, although did not meet thresholds at a cohort level for clinically meaningful improvement. The stability in clinical outcomes further supports neuroprotection in PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab, as well as other high efficacy DMTs such as natalizumab and rituximab.

This study has several limitations. The comparative cohort of untreated PwPMS had several clinical characteristics that differed from the cohort of PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab. The untreated PwPMS had a longer disease duration, higher disease burden (as reflected by higher EDSS and lower LA scores at Time 0), higher age at Time 0, and fewer eyes with a prior history of ON as compared to the PwRMS treated with ocrelizumab. For true comparative purposes it would be ideal if the untreated cohort consisted of PwRMS rather than PwPMS. However, this is unrealistic in current day clinical practice since the vast majority of PwRMS receive treatment with DMTs in order to lower the risk of inflammatory disease activity. Additionally, the sample size of the rituximab cohort was substantially smaller than the other cohorts. This was due to the fact that the cohort was retrospectively recruited and had limited data available for inclusion. Furthermore, our study did not include a cohort of people with PPMS being treated with ocrelizumab. Finally, as this was an observational study, it is important to note that the DMT (or lack of DMT) selection in PwMS was selected by the treating neurologist based on clinical indications and therefore participants could not be truly randomized between cohorts.

In summary, the findings of this study support that ocrelizumab is associated with slower rates of retinal atrophy, particularly GCIPL atrophy, in PwRMS. The findings of our study have important clinical implications as they support stability of global disability and visual function measures in PwRMS receiving ocrelizumab, as measured by annual change in EDSS scores, 100% high-contrast LA scores, and 2.5% low-contrast LA scores. Overall, our study provides additional support that ocrelizumab may impart secondary neuroprotection in PwRMS, similar to other high potency DMTs, and that OCT-derived GCIPL thinning may be a useful neuroaxonal outcome measure in PwMS.
