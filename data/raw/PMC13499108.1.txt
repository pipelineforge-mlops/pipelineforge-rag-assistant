Objective

This study aimed to externally validate the ninth edition TNM staging system for thymic carcinoma (TC) in an independent Chinese cohort.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed 245 patients with TC who underwent complete surgical resection between 2010 and 2023. Tumors were staged according to the eighth and ninth TNM editions. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), Akaike Information Criterion, R2, and the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC).

Results

Compared with the eighth edition, the ninth edition exhibited improved discrimination for OS, with a higher C-index (0.724 versus 0.701), lower Akaike Information Criterion (565.81 versus 568.34), higher R2 (0.131 versus 0.121), and superior 5-year AUC (0.693 versus 0.674). Significant differences in OS and DFS were observed between most adjacent stage groups (all p < 0.05), except between stages III and IV. Lung or phrenic nerve invasion was associated with more favorable DFS (5-year DFS: 47.3% versus 27.1%, p = 0.032), supporting reclassification from T3 to T2. Among patients with stage I disease, the Masaoka-Koga staging system provided additional prognostic stratification for DFS, distinguishing between stage I and stage II or higher (5-year DFS: 85.8% versus 56.2%, p = 0.021), whereas no significant difference was observed in OS.

Conclusions

The ninth TNM edition provides improved prognostic discrimination after complete resection, although differentiation between stages III and IV remains limited. The Masaoka-Koga system offers complementary value in early stage disease, particularly for DFS.

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are the most common primary neoplasms of the anterior mediastinum.1,2 Among them, thymic carcinoma (TC) exhibits significantly higher malignancy than thymoma, characterized by an aggressive clinical course with a greater tendency for local invasion and distant metastasis.3 Clinically, patients often present with heterogeneous symptoms because of tumor infiltration of adjacent mediastinal structures, reflecting the biologically aggressive nature of the disease.

The TNM staging system was central to prognostic evaluation and treatment planning in TC.4,5 The eighth edition of the TNM classification, jointly developed by the American Joint Committee on Cancer and the Union for International Cancer Control, marked the first standardized staging framework for TETs, offering a consistent basis for clinical assessment and management. Building on this foundation, the ninth edition was developed on the basis of a comprehensive analysis of a multinational, multicenter database.6, 7, 8 This updated edition introduced key revisions to the T descriptor: T1 was subdivided into T1a (tumors ≤5 cm) and T1b (tumors >5 cm); mediastinal pleural invasion, previously classified as T1 in the eighth edition, was reclassified. In addition, phrenic nerve and pulmonary invasion were downstaged from T3 to T2.7,9 In contrast, the N and M descriptors remained largely unchanged in the ninth edition.7,10

Given the rarity of TC, external validation of the newly proposed ninth edition TNM staging system remains limited. Moreover, most of the data informing the ninth edition originated from Japan, Europe, and South Korea,6 whereas large-scale cohort studies from the People’s Republic of China remained notably scarce. In this study, we analyzed a Chinese independent surgical cohort of patients with TC to systematically evaluate the prognostic performance of the ninth edition TNM classification. We also assessed the complementary value of the Masaoka-Koga staging system.11 Our goal was to provide evidence-based insights to improve staging accuracy and support more informed clinical decision-making in the management of TC.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent surgical resection for TC at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital between January 2010 and December 2023. Exclusion criteria included receipt of preoperative therapy, synchronous lung cancer resection, perioperative death within 30 days (excluded from long-term survival analysis), and a lack of follow-up data. Patients with pleural or pericardial dissemination (M1a) were excluded to ensure a relatively homogeneous surgical cohort. Stage IV cases were limited to patients with pulmonary metastases (M1b) and/or N1/N2 nodal involvement who underwent complete resection of all detectable lesions. No patients overlapped with the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer database. The final 245 patients with TC were included in the analysis. The patient selection process is detailed in Supplementary Figure 1. Clinicopathological data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, and the need for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design.

Surgical and Pathologic Assessment

All patients underwent surgical resection with curative intent, with the extent determined by tumor invasion and intraoperative findings. Complete resection (R0) was defined as a microscopically margin-negative resection. In selected patients with limited pulmonary metastases, metastasectomy was performed.

All specimens were reviewed by experienced pathologists and classified according to the WHO criteria. Masaoka-Koga staging was determined on the basis of histopathological evidence of invasion into the surrounding mediastinal tissues. In the absence of a well-defined capsule, staging relied on microscopic invasion into adjacent tissues, and tumors were not classified as stage II or higher solely on the basis of infiltrative growth patterns without histologic confirmation of invasion.

Lymph node evaluation was selectively performed on the basis of preoperative assessment and intraoperative findings at the discretion of the surgeon, rather than being routinely applied to all patients. Nodal status was classified as N0, N1, or N2 according to the TNM staging system. The extent of lymph node assessment, including evaluation of N1 and N2 regions, was recorded.

Follow-Up

Postoperative surveillance consisted of routine outpatient visits or structured telephone interviews. Follow-up was scheduled at 3, 6, and 12 months during the first year and annually thereafter. Imaging included chest computed tomography and abdominal ultrasound. When recurrence was suspected, additional diagnostic procedures, such as positron emission tomography–computed tomography or image-guided biopsy, were performed as clinically indicated. The final follow-up date was June 30, 2025.

Outcome Measures and Statistical Analyses

The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS), measured from the date of surgery to the date of death, recurrence, or last follow-up. Survival outcomes by TNM stage (eighth and ninth editions) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test.

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges. Hazard ratios were derived using Cox proportional hazards models, with adjustments made for sex, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, surgical approach, and receipt of adjuvant therapy. The prognostic performance of the eighth and ninth edition TNM staging systems was compared using several metrics: Harrell’s concordance index (C-index),12 Akaike information criterion (AIC),13 coefficient of determination (R2),14 and the area under the curve (AUC). All reported p values are two-sided, with statistical significance defined as p less than 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 23.0, IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp., Armonk, New York) and R software (version 4.0.3, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

As a sensitivity analysis, we expanded the cohort to include 321 patients with all types of resections (R-any). Similar trends were observed, although the discriminatory performance of the ninth edition was somewhat attenuated, likely because of confounding by residual disease. Nevertheless, the ninth edition continued to demonstrate more consistent stage-wise prognostic separation than the eighth edition (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Subgroup Analysis Results of Staging System

Among patients classified as T3 under the eighth edition TNM staging system, a subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of lung or phrenic nerve invasion. Patients with lung or phrenic nerve involvement exhibited a significantly improved DFS compared with those without such invasion (p = 0.032) (Fig. 1C), whereas the difference was not statistically significant in OS (p = 0.108) (Fig. 1D). These findings were confirmed by multivariable Cox regression analysis (Supplementary Table 2), suggesting a favorable prognostic association with lung or phrenic nerve involvement.

According to the ninth edition TNM staging system, patients with stage I were reclassified into T1a (tumor size ≤5 cm) and T1b (tumor size >5 cm) subgroups. Although DFS did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 3A), patients with T1a exhibited a trend toward improved OS compared with those with T1b (p = 0.089) (Fig. 3B).Figure 3 Survival curves of OS and DFS for patients with T1 tumors according to the ninth edition TNM staging system (A,B) and the Masaoka-Koga staging system (C, D). OS, overall survival; DFS, disease-free survival.

To further explore prognostic stratification in early-stage disease, we applied the Masaoka-Koga staging system within the stage I subgroup. Patients with Masaoka-Koga stage I disease exhibited significantly better DFS (p = 0.021) (Fig. 3C) than those with stage II or higher disease. In contrast, no statistically significant difference in OS was observed, although a favorable trend was noted (p = 0.320) (Fig. 3D). These findings were supported by multivariable Cox regression analysis (Supplementary Table 3). These findings indicate that the Masaoka-Koga system provides complementary prognostic value in early-stage TC, specifically for DFS rather than OS.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first and largest cohort to validate the ninth edition TNM staging system in TC. Given that complete (R0) resection is a key determinant of long-term outcomes, we focused our primary analysis on patients who underwent complete resection to minimize the confounding effect of margin status on prognostic evaluation. Within this cohort, the ninth edition exhibited superior prognostic performance compared with the eighth edition, as reflected by improved separation of survival curves and better model discrimination (C-index, AIC, R2, and time-dependent AUC), indicating enhanced risk stratification. To further assess the robustness of these findings, we performed sensitivity analyses that included patients with incomplete resection (R1–R2). The results remained consistent, with the ninth edition exhibiting improved discriminatory ability for OS. These findings support the broader applicability of the ninth edition TNM system across different surgical settings and underscore its potential as a more reliable tool for prognostic stratification in TC.

We believe that this improvement is partly attributable to revisions in T classification, particularly the downstaging of lung and phrenic nerve invasion from T3 to T2. In our cohort, patients with lung or phrenic nerve invasion had more favorable DFS than those without such involvement, supporting this reclassification. In contrast, major vascular invasion remained associated with worse outcomes, consistent with previous studies,15 indicating that it represents a distinct high-risk feature even after complete resection.

In addition, the eighth edition of the TNM system defines pericardial invasion as T2, but in our complete resection cohort, the proportion of patients with simple pericardial invasion was very low (17/245, 6.9%), resulting in a lack of representation in the T2 category, which may limit its stratification ability in clinical practice. In the ninth edition, some original T3 patients were reclassified as T2, which enriched the case composition of the T2 stage and improved the recognition ability of the model in the intermediate stage, which may be one of the reasons for its better performance in statistical indicators. Therefore, we believe that the ninth edition of the TNM staging has higher prognostic efficacy and clinical applicability in patients with completely resected TC.

Furthermore, the ninth edition of TNM proposed that the maximum tumor diameter was an important parameter affecting T staging, and on the basis of this, T1 was further divided into T1a (≤ 5 cm) and T1b (>5 cm).9 This modification is on the basis of previous research results.16,17 However, in this study, tumor size did exhibit a certain differentiation trend in OS, but it failed to effectively distinguish the prognosis risk of patients in DFS. Similarly, a study from the National Cancer Database found that tumor size is not an independent risk factor for TC.18 We speculate that this result may be related to the highly invasive biological characteristics of TC. Even if the tumor is small, lymph node metastasis or invasion of adjacent structures may occur, which may affect the clinical prognosis.

Within T1 disease, the Masaoka-Koga staging system provided additional prognostic stratification for DFS, distinguishing between stage I and stage II or higher. However, no significant difference in overall survival was observed. Although TC often lacks a well-defined capsule, this system may remain clinically relevant when interpreted on the basis of histopathological evidence of local invasion rather than capsular structure alone. Consistent with our findings, Litvak et al.19 also reported improved survival in stage I compared with stage II TC. These findings suggest that, despite the absence of a well-defined capsule in many TCs, histopathological evidence of local invasion remains clinically informative. This further indicates that invasion-related features may better capture tumor aggressiveness than size alone in early-stage disease.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection and information bias. Second, although multiple end points were assessed and subgroup analyses were performed, the inherently low incidence of TC and the small sample size of certain subgroups (such as patients with pericardial or major vascular invasion) may limit the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Then, in this cohort, lymph node dissection was performed selectively rather than being routinely applied. Although this reflects real-world practice, the lack of uniform lymph node assessment may introduce potential staging bias and should be considered when interpreting the TNM staging results. Finally, the study was conducted at a single center; therefore, external validation in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts is required to confirm the applicability of the ninth edition TNM staging system across different populations and clinical settings.

In summary, the ninth edition TNM classification provides improved prognostic discrimination compared with the eighth edition in surgically treated TCs. The Masaoka-Koga staging system offers complementary prognostic value within T1 disease, particularly for DFS.
