Background

In China, women’s psychosocial health is shaped by social, cultural, family, economic, and healthcare-related factors that extend beyond biomedical care. Community nurses often encounter hidden emotional distress, caregiving burden, loneliness, family pressure, and unmet social needs during routine practice. Social prescribing, a structured process that connects people with non-clinical community resources, may help address these needs. However, the role of nurses in gender-responsive social prescribing remains underexplored in China.

Objective

This study explored how community nurses in China can serve as gender-responsive social prescribing facilitators to support women’s psychosocial health and well-being.

Methods

An exploratory qualitative descriptive design was used. Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted with 27 purposively selected community nurses working in community health centers, community health stations, township health centers, family doctor teams, and other community-based settings in Yiwu City, Zhejiang Province, China. Because the participants did not consent to audio recording, the interviewer prepared detailed contemporaneous notes and expanded them immediately after each interview. This approach limited independent verification of exact wording and is acknowledged when interpreting participant excerpts. Anonymized notes were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis. Ethical approval and written informed consent were obtained.

Results

Six themes were identified. Women’s psychosocial distress was often hidden beneath physical complaints, fatigue, insomnia, emotional withdrawal, caregiving exhaustion, and indirect idioms of suffering. Help-seeking was suppressed by face-saving norms, family reputation, caregiving obligations, economic dependence, migration-related exclusion, and hukou-related barriers. Nurses recognized hidden distress through repeated consultations, non-verbal cues, family dynamics, indirect comments, and long-term patient relationships. They already provided informal psychosocial support through listening, reassurance, family engagement, and ad hoc signposting, but this work remained unsupported and invisible. Women’s engagement depended on culturally acceptable framing, trust, warm referrals, privacy, accessibility, and family acceptability. Participants emphasized that nurse-led social prescribing requires formal role recognition, protected time, training, supervision, referral directories, privacy safeguards, and multidisciplinary collaboration.

Conclusion

In this single-city study, community nurses perceived themselves as potentially well positioned to facilitate gender-responsive social prescribing for women. Developing and testing such a model would require formal role recognition, protected time, culturally sensitive communication, referral infrastructure, community partnership, and direct involvement of women service users.

Introduction

Women’s psychosocial health is shaped by the interaction of biological, social, relational, and structural factors rather than by symptoms or diagnoses alone. Gender roles, caregiving responsibilities, family expectations, employment pressures, economic insecurity, stigma, violence, and access to social support can influence how women experience distress and whether they seek help.1–12 In China, rapid urbanization, population ageing, migration, changing family structures, and unequal access to health-promoting resources have altered the conditions in which women manage work, family responsibilities, caregiving, and health. Chinese studies have associated women’s mental health with livelihood resources, relative deprivation, urban-rural inequalities, and combined individual, family, and social influences.13–15 These findings support a community-based approach that recognizes psychosocial needs before they develop into or are interpreted solely as clinical disorders.

China has expanded primary healthcare and community health services to strengthen prevention, chronic disease management, health education, and population health. Nevertheless, variations in service quality, workforce capacity, and equitable access remains.16,17 Within brief and predominantly biomedical consultations, non-medical concerns such as loneliness, caregiving strain, family conflict, financial stress, and limited social support may receive insufficient attention. Social prescribing offers one possible response by linking people with non-clinical community resources, including peer support, welfare assistance, physical and cultural activities, counselling, volunteering, and local social networks.1–4 Evidence from the Western Pacific region indicates that social prescribing is increasingly used for mental health concerns, social isolation, chronic illness, and social determinants of health, although implementation is often constrained by fragmented referral systems, limited resources, and inconsistent evaluation.5

Social Prescribing and Gender-Responsive Community Care

Social prescribing extends care beyond medical treatment by addressing the practical, relational, and social conditions that affect well-being. Reviews report potential benefits in mental well-being, social connectedness, confidence, self-management, and access to support, although the strength and consistency of the evidence remain variable.1–5 In settings without dedicated link workers, existing primary care professionals may identify non-medical needs, discuss available options, connect individuals with community services, and follow up. This is relevant to China, where early social prescribing initiatives have focused on healthy ageing and community support, and where general practitioners have begun to consider arts-, nature-, and activity-based referrals for psychosocial health.6,7

A gender-responsive approach is required because the same referral may not be equally accessible, acceptable, or safe for all women. Gendered caregiving expectations, reproductive and family roles, economic dependence, interpersonal violence, stigma, mobility restrictions, privacy concerns, and unequal control over time and resources can shape both psychosocial distress and service use.9–12 Community referral decisions should therefore consider safety, affordability, transport, family context, cultural acceptability, confidentiality, and the woman’s preferred form of support. This approach also avoids treating social prescribing as a substitute for action on poverty, discrimination, unsafe relationships, or inadequate welfare protection.8 Chinese evidence showing urban-rural differences, resource-related inequalities, and varied help-seeking pathways among women further supports the need for locally adapted and gender-sensitive assessment.13–15

Community Nurses in China as Potential Social Prescribing Facilitators

Community nurses frequently interact with residents and families through health education, chronic disease follow-up, preventive services, home visits, family health support, and community outreach. Chinese research describes primary healthcare nurses as protectors of family health, early identifiers of health risks, and promoters of health behavior, while also documenting unclear responsibilities, limited resources, uneven professional preparation, restricted authority, and organizational barriers.18–21 These activities overlap with core social prescribing functions: recognizing non-medical concerns, providing information and emotional support, coordinating care, signposting services, and linking families with community resources. However, the ability to perform these functions depends on the workload, professional recognition, communication skills, interprofessional collaboration, and availability of reliable referral pathways.

Recent China-specific studies provide a more direct basis for examining community nurses’ psychosocial roles. A mixed-method study developed a psychological care competence framework for Chinese nurses that included knowledge, communication, and crisis-management skills, professional ethics, personal attributes, and motivation, while noting that application in community settings requires further evaluation.22 Qualitative research found that community nurses providing home-based hospice and palliative care encountered inadequate preparation and organizational and resource constraints when addressing complex patient and family needs.23 Another study showed that community nursing interactions included informational, emotional, and practical support, but that workload, limited participation, and weak collaboration reduced the depth and continuity of that support.24 Together, these studies show that Chinese community nurses already undertake relational and supportive work, but the evidence remains concentrated on specific diseases or populations. It does not explain how nurses recognize women’s gender-related psychosocial needs, how informal community linkage could be developed into a structured social prescribing role, or what safeguards and organizational support such a role would require.

Rationale of the Study

Social prescribing is receiving growing international attention, but its development in China remains at an early stage, particularly in relation to community nursing and women’s psychosocial well-being. Existing studies have largely examined general social prescribing models, older adults, chronic illness, social isolation, and broad primary care infrastructure.2–7 Chinese community nursing research demonstrates potential roles in family support, health promotion, emotional interaction, information provision, and care coordination, but it has not specifically examined gender-responsive social prescribing for women. Consequently, little is known about how community nurses understand women’s psychosocial needs, what related practices they already perform, which cultural and institutional factors shape those practices, and what training, referral systems, and ethical safeguards are needed. A qualitative thematic approach is appropriate because these questions concern meanings, experiences, routine practices, barriers, and contextual influences that cannot be adequately captured through quantitative measures alone. Therefore, this study asks: In what ways can community nurses serve as gender-responsive social prescribing facilitators to enhance women’s psychosocial health and well-being in China? Exploring nurses’ perspectives can clarify current practices, identify feasible community resources and referral relationships, and reveal requirements for confidentiality, risk recognition, trauma-informed communication, interprofessional collaboration, and follow-up. The findings may inform training, community resource mapping, referral pathway design, service governance, and future evaluation of a culturally appropriate nurse-led model.

Theoretical and Conceptual Framework

This study is guided by an integrated gender-responsive social prescribing framework (see Figure 1). The social determinants of health perspective explains how emotional distress, loneliness, stress, and help-seeking may be shaped by living conditions, social relationships, material resources, and access to services rather than by individual psychological factors alone.24 A gender-responsive and intersectional lens adds attention to unequal gender relations and differences associated with age, marital status, income, education, migration background, reproductive experiences, and caregiving responsibilities.25–28 These perspectives direct the study toward the social and cultural contexts in which community nurses encounter women’s psychosocial concerns. Social prescribing positions community nurses as potential connectors between healthcare and non-clinical community support.1,22 In this study, nurse-led social prescribing refers to identifying women’s non-medical psychosocial concerns, discussing what matters to them through sensitive and person-centred communication, jointly selecting appropriate support, facilitating referral or community linkage, and following up where feasible. Potential resources include peer and caregiver support, health education, counselling, women’s groups, welfare services, exercise, and cultural or social activities. Person-centered nursing theory supports this process through its emphasis on trust, privacy, empathy, shared decision-making, and respect for individual preferences.29 Implementation is examined through the COM-B model and Normalization Process Theory (See Figure 1A and B). COM-B focuses on whether nurses have the capability, opportunity, and motivation to perform gender-responsive social prescribing.30 Normalization Process Theory focuses on how the practice could be understood, accepted, enacted, and sustained within routine community healthcare.31 Together, these theories guide the analysis of barriers and facilitators at individual, organizational, community, cultural, and policy levels and support the development of a preliminary practice model for safe, feasible, and context-sensitive nurse-led social prescribing. Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed an exploratory qualitative descriptive design utilizing semi-structured individual interviews to investigate how community nurses in China perceive, interpret, and enact their potential role as gender-responsive social prescribing facilitators for women’s psychosocial health and well-being. A qualitative descriptive design was deemed appropriate as the study aimed to generate a practice-near account of community nurses’ professional experiences, perceived responsibilities, implementation concerns, and recommendations for service development. This design is particularly suitable when the objective is to remain close to participants’ everyday language and professional realities while producing findings that can inform healthcare practice, service planning, and policy development.32,33

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval (K2023034) for this study was secured from the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University prior to the commencement of data collection. The research adhered to ethical principles, including voluntary participation, informed consent, confidentiality, respect for individuals, and protection from harm, in alignment with the Declaration of Helsinki.34 Participants were fully informed about the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their involvement, the anticipated interview process, and their right to refuse any question or withdraw from the interview without any repercussions. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the interviews commenced. The participants informed consent included publication of anonymized responses/direct quotes. No personal identifiers, such as names, workplace details, patient names, or identifiable community-service information, were collected or disclosed. Participants were assigned anonymous codes ranging from CN01 to CN27, which were utilized throughout transcription, data management, analysis, and reporting.

Study Participants and Sampling

The participants in this study were community nurses employed in Yiwu City, Zhejiang Province, China. A purposive criterion-based sampling method was employed to select individuals with direct professional experience relevant to the phenomenon under investigation. This sampling technique is extensively utilized in qualitative health research when the objective is to recruit participants who are information-rich and can offer direct insights into a specific practice, service, or implementation issue.35

Eligible participants were community nurses working in community-based nursing roles in Yiwu, including direct nursing care, health education, chronic disease management, maternal and child health, elderly care, family doctor services, or mixed community health roles. Participants were required to have regular contact with women in community healthcare settings and professional experience with women’s everyday health, psychosocial, family, or social support concerns. Formal social-prescribing experience was not required because structured nurse-led social prescribing remains underdeveloped in the local setting. Therefore, the study also explored informal practices, such as listening, signposting, family engagement, and ad hoc referrals. Nurses with exclusively administrative roles, those without direct contact with community residents, and those unable to complete the interview were excluded. The sample was occupationally focused because all participants were community nurses. At the same time, recruitment aimed to achieve variation in age group, gender, nursing qualification, professional title, total nursing experience, community nursing experience, type of community setting, location of practice, main population served, weekly number of women seen, previous exposure to mental health support training, previous exposure to gender-sensitive or women-centered care training, and main work role. These demographic domains were incorporated into the interview guide to facilitate a comprehensive description of participants’ characteristics and practice context.

The final sample comprised 27 community nurses from Yiwu. Sample adequacy was assessed iteratively using information power and meaning saturation rather than statistical power or numerical representativeness.36,37 A statistical power calculation was not applicable because the study used a qualitative design and did not seek statistical representativeness of the sample. After each interview, the researchers compared the expanded notes with the research questions, interview domains, emerging codes, contradictory cases, and implementation issues. Recruitment continued until the interviews provided sufficient depth to address the research question, and no substantively new domain-level issues emerged regarding women’s psychosocial needs, nurses’ current practices, barriers, facilitators, ethical concerns, and implementation requirements. The findings are context-bound to the Yiwu setting and are intended to support analytical interpretation and assessment of transferability, not statistical generalization to all regions of China.

Development and Validation of the Data Collection Tool

A semi-structured interview guide was developed in accordance with established guidance.38,39 Seven community nurses pilot-tested the guide for clarity, relevance, sequencing, cultural appropriateness, and non-leading wording. The pilot led to minor revisions: technical wording was simplified, practice-based questions were placed before the formal explanation of social prescribing to reduce priming, and optional probes on privacy, family influence, workload, referral resources, and critical or negative views were clarified. No new substantive interview domains were added. Pilot participants were not included in the final sample or the thematic analysis.

The guide comprised four principal components. Section A gathered demographic and professional information. Section B included an introductory script elucidating the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the absence of right or wrong answers. Section C encompassed the main interview questions, organized around six thematic areas. Section D contained closing questions that allowed participants to offer additional reflections and recommendations (See Figure S1).

Data Collection

Data were collected through semi-structured individual interviews with 27 community nurses in private locations in Yiwu. The data collection were started on 15th June 2025 and completed on 20th December 2025. The total duration of the study was two years, from designing and developing the research plan to the implementation of data collection. The mean length of the interview per participant was 65 minutes. The interviewer used the guide flexibly and probed women’s psychosocial needs, recognition of emotional or social difficulties, sensitive communication, available community resources, referral practices, barriers to engagement, and organizational requirements.40,41 Participants declined audio recording. The interviewer therefore prepared detailed contemporaneous notes and expanded them immediately after each interview to retain participant wording, contextual information, non-verbal observations, and reflexive comments. Quotation marks are used only for wording recorded as near-verbatim in the field notes; content that could not be confirmed as exact wording is presented as paraphrased analytic summary. Because no audio files or verbatim transcripts exist, exact quotation accuracy cannot be independently verified, nuance may have been lost, and note selection may have been influenced by the interviewer. All notes were anonymized and stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team.

Data Analysis

The expanded interview notes were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.37,38 The analysis was recursive and interpretive rather than a rigid sequence or a search for coder agreement. It examined both participants’ explicit accounts and the researchers’ interpretation of patterned meaning while retaining a clear distinction between reported experience and analytic inference.

First, both researchers read and re-read the complete dataset and the reflexive field records. Familiarization notes documented recurring issues, unusual cases, tensions, and initial assumptions. The researchers then coded the dataset line by line and segment by segment, remaining open to content that did not fit the interview guide or preliminary conceptual framework.

Coding included semantic codes such as repeated visits, sleep problems, caregiving burden, informal signposting, privacy concerns, and lack of referral pathways, as well as more interpretive codes concerning gendered expectations, professional boundaries, emotional labor, and organizational recognition. The codes were treated as analytical tools rather than objective variables or frequency counts.

Related codes were grouped into candidate themes based on shared meaning and relevance to the research question. For each candidate theme, the researchers documented the central organizing concept, supporting excerpts, boundaries with other themes, contradictory or less common accounts, and the relationship between participant reports and author interpretation.

Candidate themes were reviewed against the full dataset for internal coherence, distinctiveness, evidential support and alternative explanations. Themes were revised, combined, separated, or discarded when the available notes did not support the proposed interpretations. Particular attention was given to tensions, including nurses’ perceived suitability for psychosocial work, alongside concerns about workload, competence, authority, privacy, and referral capacity.

The final themes were defined and named using concise, descriptive labels. The researchers then selected a limited number of near-verbatim field note excerpts to illustrate, rather than prove, each theme. Interpretive statements were written using cautious language and checked against the source notes and documented theme definitions.

Inter-coder reliability statistics were not calculated because reflexive thematic analysis does not conceptualize coding as a mechanical exercise requiring numerical agreement.37,38 Instead, both researchers used reflexive discussions to challenge assumptions, review coding and theme-development decisions, examine negative or contradictory cases, and agree on a coherent account while recognizing that the final themes are researcher-generated interpretations.

Research Team and Reflexivity

The research team comprised two authors with nursing experience. During the study, RM was affiliated with a university nursing school, and LR was affiliated with a hospital nursing department. Neither researcher was a formal social prescribing link worker within the participating community services. Their interest in community-based, person-centered, and gender-sensitive care may have sensitized them to the potential contribution of nurses and encouraged favorable interpretations of nurse-led social prescribing.

To reduce premature endorsement of the proposed model, interviews began with participants’ routine experiences, women’s psychosocial needs, current support practices, and existing referral patterns before introducing the term social prescribing. The interviewer used a neutral, non-evaluative stance and explicitly invited concerns, negative views, ethical risks, and examples in which nurse involvement might be inappropriate.42,43

Both researchers maintained reflexive notes documenting assumptions, emotional responses, coding choices, theme development, uncertainty, and competing interpretations. During the analysis, they examined whether supportive accounts might reflect professional aspiration, social desirability, or dissatisfaction with current services rather than evidence that a nurse-led model would necessarily be effective. These positional considerations informed the cautious presentation of the findings.

Analytic Orientation

The analytical approach adopted was both experiential and critically interpretive. Participants’ narratives were regarded as significant depictions of community nursing practice, influenced by broader institutional, cultural, gendered, and organizational contexts. This methodology facilitated an examination as to whether community nurses endorsed nurse-led social prescribing and the conditions under which such a role would be feasible, ethical, acceptable, and sustainable.

Consequently, the analysis concentrated on the interplay between individual nursing practice and system-level implementation. It investigated how nurses identify women’s psychosocial needs, the impact of gender norms and family expectations on women’s willingness to seek assistance, the current provision of informal psychosocial support by nurses, and the obstacles preventing these practices from evolving into a structured social prescribing pathway. Furthermore, the analysis explored how community health centers, women’s organizations, family doctor teams, neighborhood committees, mental health services, social workers, and digital communication tools could facilitate a gender-responsive referral and follow-up model.

Trustworthiness and Reporting Quality

Trustworthiness was supported through purposive recruitment of information-rich participants, open-ended questioning, clarification probes, immediate note expansion, systematic documentation of analytic decisions, reflexive memoing, comparison across interviews, attention to contradictory cases, and review by both researchers.44 A detailed description of the setting, sample, interview domains, and analytic process supports the assessment of transferability. Nevertheless, note-based data cannot provide the same quotation fidelity as audio-recorded verbatim transcripts; this limitation was not fully correctable by the trustworthiness procedures and is carried forward into interpretation and reporting.

Analytic Overview

Throughout the corpus, participants’ narratives coalesced around a distinct storyline. Chinese community nurses did not characterize women’s psychosocial distress as an isolated clinical symptom amenable to pharmacological intervention. Instead, it emerged as a gendered and culturally embedded phenomenon, manifesting through bodily complaints, emotional withdrawal, and relational tension, which remained undetectable by conventional biomedical assessments. Nurses frequently encountered women whose suffering was articulated through culturally specific idioms, somatized complaints, domestic silence, and despair related to their roles. However, they operated within a structural environment lacking both the diagnostic language and referral infrastructure necessary to address such needs. Their narratives reveal a workforce acutely aware of gendered distress, adept at discerning its subtle manifestations, yet largely unsupported by the systems within which they operate. The final analysis identified six themes (See Table S1). The quoted material below is identified as a near-verbatim field-note excerpt. Because interviews were not audio-recorded, excerpts illustrate the analysis but should not be read as independently verifiable verbatim transcripts.

Final Findings

Theme 1: Hidden Psychosocial Distress Expressed Through Bodily and Indirect Signs

Participants reported that women often reported sleep problems, fatigue, headaches, dizziness, chest discomfort, digestive symptoms, repeated unexplained complaints, emotional withdrawal, or reduced self-care rather than naming depression or anxiety. Nurses linked these presentations to loneliness, caregiving pressure, marital or family conflict, migration, bereavement, and limited social support. They described the variation across life stages and social positions, particularly among postpartum women, widows, migrant women, left-behind women, and middle-aged caregivers (See Table S2).

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: Older women commonly present with what they describe as “heart stuffiness”, feeling agitated, poor sleep, and body aches that do not have a clear cause. Many are lonely; their children have moved to the city, their spouses may have passed away, and they spend days without meaningful conversation. Some are depressed but would never use that word they say they’re just “not in the mood” or “can’t be bothered”. The body speaks what the mouth cannot. (CN04)

Nurses observe that women often lack culturally acceptable words for emotional suffering, so the body communicates distress through insomnia, chest discomfort, or “heart stuffiness.” These bodily complaints are not simple medical puzzles but gendered idioms of distress that nurses must interpret as hidden emotional and social pain.

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: They rarely use the word “depression”; instead, they talk about headaches, dizziness, or digestive problems. We must learn to read between the lines. (CN07)

Nurses described women’s distress as shaped by a broader gendered structure of family and community life, not just by bodily complaints. This distress is linked to caregiving duties, loneliness, marital conflict, patriarchal family roles, and pressure on children’s education. Women’s suffering is therefore not purely individual but is rooted in the social expectations attached to being a wife, mother, daughter-in-law, and caregiver.

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: Older women are isolated, physically exhausted, and feel forgotten in their fifties and sixties who are caring for grandchildren while their children work in the city (CN02). Middle-aged women who are sandwiched between caring for children and elderly parents seem to struggle the most. (CN24)

Certain groups of women experience deeper psychosocial distress because different forms of disadvantage overlap and intensify each other. Postpartum mothers, elderly widows, migrant women, left-behind rural women, and members of the “sandwich generation” face combined pressures linked to caregiving, isolation, discrimination, family roles, and limited support. Their suffering is not caused by a single problem. It emerges from the interactions of gender, age, motherhood, migration, widowhood, and social expectations.

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: Left-behind women are definitely the most vulnerable group in our community. Their husbands work away in the city, sometimes for years. They carry the entire burden of the farm, elderly parents, and children (CN25). Migrant women in our suburban station come in with a lot of stress-related complaints. Many work long hours in factories or service jobs, live far from their families, and have no social support here. (CN11)

Intersectional vulnerability demonstrates that women’s distress is shaped not only by gender but also by migration status, age, class, marital status, and location. Migrant women, postpartum mothers, and widowed elderly women face different forms of suffering, as social systems and cultural expectations intensify their burdens. These patterns suggest that women’s psychosocial distress is diverse and requires context-specific support rather than general responses.

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: These women carry the heavy weight of being far from home and are separated from their family support networks. Loneliness is almost unbearable for some of them (CN27). First-time mothers in their late twenties to mid-thirties seem to need the most psychosocial support. They are dealing with huge hormonal changes, sleepless nights, and often feel very isolated (CN19). Widowed women are the most vulnerable group I serve. After losing their husbands, many women lose their social identity and purpose in life. They withdraw, stop eating properly, and their health declines rapidly. (CN04)

Theme 2: Cultural and Structural Barriers to Disclosure and Help-Seeking

Participants described face-saving, fear of family shame, expectations of endurance, caregiving obligations, economic dependence, limited privacy, rural isolation, migration-related exclusion, and hukou-linked service barriers as factors that could inhibit disclosure or the use of support. Nurses also reported that some women prioritized the health and schedules of their children, spouses, or older relatives over their own needs (See Table S3).

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: The “face” culture is huge; women feel that they cannot show weakness or admit that they are struggling emotionally because it would bring shame to their family. (CN01)

The familial dimension is critical, as it operates not as individual reputation but as collective honor; a woman’s emotional disclosure threatens not only her own standing but also that of her entire family unit. Nurses witness the devastating effects of this prohibition on a daily basis.

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: One patient, aged 72 years, whose daughter-in-law told her that seeking counseling would bring shame on the whole family. Thus, she suffered in silence. (CN04)

An elderly, widowed woman’s distress is silenced when her daughter-in-law prioritizes family reputation and cultural “face” over her emotional suffering. This reflects the broader architecture of silence, in which “eating bitterness” normalizes the endurance of silence as a feminine virtue. Nurses frequently report that women have internalized the belief that suffering is not merely to be endured but is indicative of moral character:

Near-verbatim field note excerpt The concept of “eating bitterness” and silently enduring hardship is deeply embedded in Chinese culture, particularly for women (CN03). Women in this town have been taught since childhood to endure hardship without complaint. (CN25)

Many women begin to see emotional struggles as weakness or failure in their roles as wives and mothers. This creates internalized silence, in which women censor their own needs before others do. This phenomenon is not paradoxical but rather a structural feature of patriarchal social organization. Nurses describe how women’s caregiving obligations serve as practical, moral, and economic constraints on help-seeking:

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: Women also prioritize their family’s needs over their own. For instance, one patient canceled their follow-up appointment because they needed to take their child to extracurricular activities (CN01). Women often view their emotional needs as secondary to their caregiving responsibilities (CN03). Women often put themselves last when seeking help. They will bring their children or husbands for care but downplay their own needs. (CN24)

What nurses articulate is not merely a pattern of self-sacrifice but rather a systematic devaluation of women’s needs in comparison to those of their family members, a devaluation so pervasive that women engage in it without conscious awareness. The legacy of the one-child policy intensifies this dynamic by concentrating sole caregiving responsibilities on women.

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: Women prioritize their family’s health over their own; they’ll bring their husband or child to appointments but neglect their own medication reveals how this devaluation is embodied, manifesting in concrete health behaviors that endanger women’s well-being (CN10). The one-child policy legacy means many women are now the sole support for two parents and four grandparents (CN20)

Structural marginalization through the hukou system, economic dependence, and rural isolation constitutes the third pillar of this architecture. The hukou (household registration) system, a uniquely Chinese form of internal migration control, engenders profound inequities in access to healthcare, education, and social services, disproportionately affecting women.

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: The household registration system creates profound inequity. Migrant women without local residency cannot access the same healthcare benefits as local women. (CN05). The hukou system means that their children may not have equal access to education and healthcare. (CN11)

For migrant mothers, this situation necessitates a harsh calculation in which their personal needs must be subordinated to their children’s tenuous access to essential services. This results in a form of structural silencing, in which the pursuit of healthcare poses the risk of revealing documentation vulnerabilities that could jeopardize the entire family.

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: In some families, the husband controls the finances, so a woman might not have the money or permission to seek services even if she wants to (CN01). Economic dependence on husbands limits women’s autonomy. (CN02)

The emerging picture suggests that women’s ability to seek assistance is not primarily dictated by their needs but rather by their structural position within the family economy. Specifically, women who do not have an independent income are deprived of the practical means to advocate for themselves, even when they are aware of their distress.

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: There’s a belief that if you’re poor or struggling, you don’t have the luxury of emotional needs. (CN05)

This observation elucidates that the architecture of silence is influenced by gender and class dynamics.

Theme 3: Nurses’ Tacit Recognition of Hidden Distress

Nurses described relying on repeated consultations, changes in usual behavior, pauses, tone of voice, eye contact, family interactions, indirect comments, and long-term knowledge of residents to decide when a psychosocial conversation was appropriate. Participants valued experience but also reported uncertainty, limited mental health training, fear of causing harm, and a lack of supervision (See Table S4).

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: Nurses noticed indirect comments, repeated vague symptoms, normal tests, and signs such as fatigue or changes in tone. A woman might come for a blood pressure check and then casually mention that she has been having trouble sleeping or that her husband does not understand her (CN01, CN02). Experience and long-term relationships help them read silence and subtle cues (CN04). Withdrawal or reduced communication may also signal distress (CN05). They used familiar phrases instead of clinical terms and asked gentle and indirect questions (CN04; CN02). I rely on my extensive experience and relationship with these women. I have known many of these women for over a decade. Asking about the patient’s hometown and family helped to build trust. (CN27)

The competence systems paradox crystallizes in nurses’ reflections on their own practice. Those who have received even minimal training report transformative effects on their confidence; however, most have had no preparation at all.

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt Honestly, I think most of us feel quite uncomfortable discussing sensitive emotional topics, especially those of us who have not received any mental health training (CN19). We care deeply about our patients, and many are like family after all these years (CN04). None of us had formal training in this area, and we were expected to resolve this issue. There were no protocols, guidelines, or supervision (CN04). Younger nurses, such as myself, tend to be more comfortable because we have had more psychosocial content in our education than older nurses. Some of my older colleagues find it uncomfortable because they see nursing as purely physical care (CN05). I worry about my own emotional well-being; hearing difficult stories affects me, and I do not have the supervision or support to process that. (CN07)

Nurses have developed sophisticated, culturally attuned clinical intuition not because the system supported this development but because the gap between women’s needs and institutional responses was too wide to ignore. Their expertise represents a form of adaptive professionalism, the creative expansion of practice beyond formal boundaries to meet needs that the system does not acknowledge.

Theme 4: Informal Psychosocial Support Without Formal Infrastructure

Participants reported listening, reassurance, family engagement, health education, occasional signposting and personal follow-up. They also described fragmented community information, no standardized referral or feedback process, limited access to social workers or mental health support, heavy workloads, privacy concerns, and little recognition of psychosocial work within routine performance systems (See Table S5).

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: Many nurses do not see this as legitimate nursing work. They said, we are not psychologists, we are nurses (CN07). Currently, we do not formally connect these two. I might mention an activity or give a phone number, but there are no referral forms, follow-ups, or feedback. I have started doing informally is keeping a personal notebook of community contacts: dance instructors, support group leaders, and social workers I have met. However, this should not depend on individual initiative (CN09). At present, we do not connect women with non-medical services. I am unaware of the community resources available in my area. (CN11). Our workloads are already overwhelming; we see 80 to 100 patients a day, and each consultation is supposed to take five minutes. Where is the time for psychosocial assessment? (CN07). Time is the most significant barrier. We see so many patients each day that we barely have time for basic care, let alone in-depth psychosocial assessment (CN01). There is no reimbursement or recognition for social prescribing work in our current healthcare system; it is invisible labor (CN19). Some nurses do not see psychosocial support as “real nursing” and do not take it seriously (CN01). I worry about privacy constantly. In our small community, confidentiality is nearly impossible to ensure. I am also concerned about my own emotional well-being; carrying these heavy stories day after day takes a toll. (CN04)

Theme 5: Conditions Shaping Women’s Acceptance of Community Support

Participants believed that women were more likely to consider support when it was introduced through a trusted relationship, framed without stigmatizing mental health labels, available in familiar and private settings, affordable, practical, and compatible with their family and caregiving responsibilities. Warm introductions, peer recommendations, women-only options, and follow-ups were also described as potentially helpful (See Table S6). Referral success depends on aligned cultural framing, trust, family approval, and practical access to care.

Near-verbatim field-note excerpt: Trust is everything if she trusts me, she’ll at least consider what I suggest (CN04). Another explained, Women here often feel guilty if they are just “taking time for themselves” but if they are learning a useful skill, exercising for health reasons, or volunteering to help others, that feels acceptable (CN07). Therefore, nurses avoid clinical labels and frame activities as health, skills, enjoyment, or cultural participation. One nurse noted, “Activities that have a clear purpose, exercise for health, crafts for enjoyment, singing for cultural preservation are more acceptable than anything framed as ‘mental health support (CN04). Peer approval and follow-up also matter: Word of mouth from other women in the community is incredibly powerful. If her neighbor says a group helped her, she will try it (CN02). Relational care continues after referral: Follow-up is critical. When we check in and show we care about her progress, she is more likely to stick with it (CN01). Ultimately, gender-responsive care protects dignity and social standing, as captured in the nurse’s words: The key is that it should not make them lose face. (CN01)

Theme 6: Envisioning a Gender-Responsive Social Prescribing System

The final theme presents nurses not as passive workers but as frontline architects of change who clearly understand what women need. They call for a gender-responsive social prescribing system built into routine community healthcare (See Table S7). This system would include nurse-led contact, brief psychosocial screening, personalized referrals, digital support, and multidisciplinary teamwork. Nurses stress that change cannot happen through one intervention alone; protected time, training, leadership support, funding, and recognition must work together.

Near-verbatim field note excerpt: Having dedicated time for psychosocial support would transform everything. If our workload allowed even 15 min per patient for a deeper conversation, that would help (CN01). If we are going to take on social prescribing, we need the training, the time, the tools, and the recognition. It is not about adding another task to our list. It is about fundamentally rethinking what community nursing means in the context of women’s health (CN10). Leadership must walk the talk, allocate budget, champion the work, and publicly recognize nurses. If it’s just lip service, nothing will change (CN06). These women deserve better than what we are able to give them right now. (CN21)

Their vision is practical because it comes from years of working within a system that leaves psychosocial care informal and unsupported. They also insist that leadership must provide real resources and not empty promises. Ultimately, nurses see themselves as essential links between clinical care, family life and community support.

Discussion

Participants described women’s psychosocial distress as being communicated indirectly through bodily complaints, caregiving exhaustion, emotional withdrawal, and culturally familiar expressions such as “heart stuffiness,” rather than through explicit labels of depression or anxiety. These accounts suggest that nurses often encountered distress in forms that could be interpreted as ordinary physical illness. However, the reported symptoms, including headache, insomnia, dizziness, chest tightness, poor appetite, fatigue, and repeated attendance, are not specific to psychosocial distress and may also reflect undiagnosed physical conditions, chronic disease, or the combined effects of physical and emotional strain. Therefore, the findings should not be read as evidence that somatic complaints are inherently psychological. Rather, they indicate that community nurses perceived patterns across repeated encounters that prompted them to consider psychosocial explanations alongside biomedical ones. This interpretation is consistent with transcultural psychiatry literature showing that somatic idioms can make distress communicable in settings where direct emotional disclosure is difficult.45 It also accords with evidence that depression among Chinese patients may be expressed more somatically than in North American samples.46 The present study adds a gendered interpretation: the authors infer that bodily expressions may provide women with a socially acceptable way to communicate suffering when open disclosure could conflict with expectations of endurance, caregiving, family harmony, or the preservation of face. The prominence of new mothers and postpartum women in participants’ accounts is also compatible with evidence of a substantial burden of perinatal and postpartum depressive experiences in China.47,48 Nevertheless, because women were not interviewed, this gendered interpretation remains an analytic explanation of nurses’ reports rather than a direct account of women’s own meanings.

Participants also reported that women frequently minimized, delayed, or indirectly disclosed their emotional difficulties. Nurses linked this pattern to concerns about face, shame, filial expectations, family reputation, caregiving responsibilities, economic dependence, rural isolation, migration, and hukou-related exclusion. The authors interpret these interconnected influences as an “architecture of silence,” but this formulation should not imply that all women were unwilling to speak or that culture alone determined help-seeking behavior. Some apparent silence may have reflected uncertainty about confidentiality, limited consultation time, lack of trusted services, previous negative experiences, or rational judgment that available support would not address practical problems. Concern for face has been associated with Chinese women’s willingness to seek help for postnatal depressive symptoms,49 while systematic evidence identifies stigma, limited mental health knowledge, fear of social judgment, and service barriers as important influences on professional help-seeking among Chinese adults.50 Qualitative evidence among pregnant women in China likewise shows that help-seeking is shaped by personal, family, sociocultural, and health-system factors.51 The present study extends this literature by illustrating how community nurses perceived these pressures during routine encounters rather than in specialist mental health settings. Participants’ descriptions of migrant women, left-behind women, widows, and economically dependent women further suggest that social disadvantage may intensify barriers; this interpretation is consistent with evidence of migration-related health vulnerability, exclusion, and restricted access among rural–urban migrants and their families.16 However, this study cannot establish that these groups experience distress in the same way or that any single structural factor caused non-disclosure.

A further contribution concerns the role of community nurses in recognizing possible psychosocial needs. Participants did not identify themselves as mental health specialists, yet they reported attending repeated consultations, changing symptom narratives, pauses, downcast eyes, family interactions, changes in self-care, and seemingly incidental “by the way” comments. Participants reported a form of relational and longitudinal noticing developed through repeated contact. The authors infer that this may represent an under-recognized component of primary care expertise, particularly where formal mental health screening and referral are limited. This interpretation is relevant to Chinese primary care research, which has identified persistent difficulties in detecting and managing common mental disorders due to stigma, limited training, and weak integration between primary care and mental health services.52 Broader assessments of China’s primary healthcare system also report gaps in workforce capacity, coordination, quality improvement, and comprehensive person-centered care.53,54 Nevertheless, nurses’ confidence in recognizing distress should not be equated with diagnostic accuracy. Informal pattern recognition may support sensitive inquiry, but it may also lead to over-attribution of psychosocial causes, inconsistent responses, or missed physical illness if it is not supported by assessment guidance, supervision, and clear escalation procedures. Participants’ reports that they lacked protected time, formal authorization, supervision, and dependable referral pathways therefore point to both unrealized capacity and potential clinical risk. This interpretation is consistent with continuing concerns about mental health service-delivery and workforce challenges in China.55

The findings also revealed a mismatch between nurses’ reported recognition of psychosocial needs and the limited infrastructure available to respond. Internationally, social prescribing is commonly described as a mechanism for connecting people with non-clinical community resources that may support health and well-being.1 Compared with more formalized international models, the practices described in this study were fragmented and relational: nurses listened, suggested activities, relied on personal knowledge of local resources, maintained informal contact lists, and occasionally referred women to community organizations. These activities resemble elements of social prescribing, but they should not be assumed to constitute a complete or effective social prescribing system. International reviews caution that the evidence remains uneven, particularly when programs lack clear models, rigorous evaluation, sufficient community capacity, and explicit attention to equity.22 Implementation research further indicates that effective delivery depends on trained link workers or equivalent coordinating roles, up-to-date service directories, intersectoral communication, sustained funding, provider engagement, and clear referral procedures.56 Realist evidence also emphasizes that outcomes vary according to context, relationship quality, acceptability, participant circumstances, and the availability of meaningful community assets.57 In this respect, the Chinese accounts are consistent with international evidence that referral alone is insufficient. They also add a context-specific insight: where formal infrastructure is weak, community nurses may become de facto navigators, but such dependence on individual initiative can produce variable access, fragile continuity, and inequity between communities.

Participants reported that women appeared more willing to engage when support was framed as health promotion, exercise, skills development, traditional arts, interest-based classes, or community participation rather than as a mental health intervention. The authors interpreted this as evidence that acceptability may improve when support is embedded in familiar and non-stigmatizing forms. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that culturally adapted interventions for people of Chinese descent can be effective when they respond to language, explanatory models, stigma, and family or cultural expectations.58 This is also supported by the cultural adaptation and field-testing of the Thinking Healthy Programme for perinatal depression in China, which demonstrates the importance of translating intervention content and delivery into locally meaningful forms.59 However, alternative explanations should be considered. A greater willingness to join such activities may reflect convenience, affordability, social opportunity, or interest in the activity itself rather than reduced stigma alone. Similarly, attendance should not be considered evidence of improved psychosocial outcomes. The reported importance of group activities, companionship, and community connection is compatible with evidence linking loneliness and social isolation with poorer health among older adults, including longitudinal findings from Shanghai connecting loneliness and social isolation with depression and anxiety.58,59 However, the present study did not evaluate whether any particular activity reduced distress, for whom it worked, or whether benefits were sustained.

Conclusion

This single-city qualitative study suggests that community nurses have a potential facilitative role in gender-responsive social prescribing for women’s psychosocial health issues. Community nurses can support women’s psychosocial health in China through gender-responsive social prescribing. However, this role is underdeveloped in the current community health system. In this single-city qualitative study, community nurses perceived that they encountered women’s psychosocial needs during routine care and that they may be able to support culturally sensitive conversations and connections with non-clinical community resources. Structural drivers of distress, including gender inequality, economic dependence, migration-related exclusion, caregiving demands, family control, and limited welfare or community resources cannot be resolved through healthcare referrals alone. Participants also identified substantial prerequisites, including training, protected time, supervision, privacy safeguards, referral infrastructure, multidisciplinary collaboration and leadership support. This study shows that social prescribing for women in China must be culturally adapted to be effective. Support is more likely to be accepted when it is framed as health promotion, social connection, learning, exercise, or community participation rather than as a mental health treatment. A sustainable model requires formal training, protected time, leadership support, community resource mapping, referral pathways, multidisciplinary collaboration, and policy recognition to be effective. Future studies should co-design the pathway with women and local community partners, test feasibility and safety, and evaluate access, referral completion, psychosocial outcomes, equity, workload, and unintended consequences before wider implementation.

Strength and Limitations

The strength of the study is its focused examination of an underexplored nursing role using participants with varied experience, titles, community settings, and service populations. The analysis considered supportive, critical, and contradictory accounts and linked practice-level observations with implementation conditions. Several limitations require caution. The study was conducted in one city and included only community nurses. Therefore, the findings should not be generalized to all regions, health systems, or professional groups in China. Women service users, family members, community organizations, physicians, social workers, and managers were not interviewed. Participants who agreed to take part may have had greater interest in psychosocial care, and accounts may be affected by recall and social-desirability bias. Most importantly, the absence of audio recording meant that analysis relied on contemporaneous and expanded notes; exact wording cannot be independently verified, conversational nuance may have been lost, and the interviewer’s selection and interpretation may have shaped the dataset. The theoretical framing and the researchers’ nursing orientation may also have favored a nurse-facilitator interpretation. The findings therefore offer context-specific hypotheses and implementation considerations that require triangulation, co-design with women, and prospective evaluation.
