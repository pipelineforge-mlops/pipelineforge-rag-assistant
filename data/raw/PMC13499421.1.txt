Background

Augmented reality (AR)-based cognitive aids provide real-time guidance during resuscitation, but their impact on clinicians’ visual attention is not well understood. This study aimed to describe visual attention distribution among clinicians using an AR-based decision support system during simulated pediatric cardiac arrest.

Methods

This descriptive study was a secondary analysis of data from the intervention arm of a randomized clinical trial. Pediatric resuscitation teams from two centers participated in a standardized cardiac arrest simulation. The AR-based decision support system provided role-specific, real-time visual guidance, including task prompts, timers, and medication information displayed within the clinician’s field of view. Visual attention data from team leaders and medication nurses were collected using AR devices (HoloLens 2) with built-in eye tracking. Predefined areas of interest (AOIs) were established, and visual attention was quantified using fixation percentage, fixation count, and fixation duration.

Results

Nine simulation sessions were analyzed. AR components accounted for a substantial proportion of visual attention (Mean percentage (SD) team leaders: 41.9% (14.9); medication nurses: 59.0% (14.6)). Team leaders demonstrated distributed attention across multiple AOIs, whereas medication nurses showed concentrated attention on medication-related tasks. Distinct fixation patterns were observed, with some AOIs characterized by frequent, brief fixations and others by less frequent, longer fixations. A substantial proportion of attention occurred outside predefined AOIs (team leaders: 46.0% (15.2); medication nurses: 22.9% (10.7)).

Conclusion

Visual attention during use of the AR-based decision support system by both team leaders and medication nurses was characterized by substantial engagement and distinct role-specific attention patterns.

Introduction

Cardiac arrest is one of the leading causes of preventable death, with unacceptably low survival rates despite decades of research and evidenced-based clinical guidelines.1, 2, 3 Guideline-compliant basic life support (BLS) and advanced life support (ALS) improve patient outcomes following cardiac arrest.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 However, deviations from resuscitation guidelines are common in clinical practice.11, 12, 13 Leading resuscitation organizations, such as the American Heart Association (AHA) and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR), have called for strategies to improve the implementation of guideline-compliant care.14, 15

Cognitive aids are decision support tools that provide clinical information or prompts to promote the performance of desired behaviors and have been used to support resuscitation teams during cardiac arrest.16, 17 The use of cognitive aids has been associated with improved adherence to guidelines and enhanced clinical performance during simulated neonatal, pediatric, and adult cardiac arrest.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 However, traditional cognitive aids, such as pocket cards or apps, are often static and may require clinicians to shift attention away from the clinical environment without dynamically adapting to evolving patient conditions. Augmented reality (AR) uses head-mounted devices to overlay interactive holograms onto the user’s real-world environment.19, 20 AR-based cognitive aids enable fast decision making by providing clinically relevant prompts and supporting anticipatory behaviors by listing upcoming tasks. Simulation-based studies have shown that use of an AR device providing decision support for the team leader improves compliance with pediatric19 and neonatal resuscitation guidelines.20 Understanding how clinicians visually interact with AR-based cognitive aids is therefore important because visual attention reflects how limited visual resources are allocated between the decision-support system and surrounding clinical environment during resuscitation. Despite these promising results, AR devices introduce additional visual stimuli (e.g. holograms) that may compete for visual attention during high-stakes clinical events, raising concerns about potential distraction and increased cognitive demands. Specifically, it is unknown how AR-based cognitive aids influence the distribution of visual attention between the cognitive aid and the clinical environment, and how this may differ across team roles during resuscitation. Although eye tracking provides an objective measure of gaze allocation, visual attention should not be interpreted as a direct measure of clinical performance or cognitive processing. A better understanding of this issue will inform future efforts to design and implement AR-based cognitive aids during cardiac arrest.

The ‘InterFACE-AR’ (Interconnected and Focused mobile Applications in the patient Care Environment with Augmented Reality-based guidance) system is a multi-faceted clinical decision support system that provides role-specific, AR-based guidance to the team leaders and medication nurses during pediatric cardiac arrest.21, 22 In this descriptive study, we aimed to characterize the distribution of visual attention and fixation patterns amongst team leaders and medication nurses while using the augmented reality device components of the InterFACE-AR system during a simulated pediatric cardiac arrest scenario.

Methods

Study design

This descriptive study was a secondary analysis of data collected from a previously published randomized controlled trial comparing the InterFACE-AR system with the AHA PALS pocket card during simulated pediatric cardiac arrest.23 Only data collected from participants randomized to the intervention arm (InterFACE-AR system) were included in this study. Ethical approval was obtained from both study sites and all participants provided informed consent before participation.

Participants

We recruited participants from the inpatient or acute care units (i.e. intensive care, emergency department) of Alberta Children’s Hospital (Calgary, Canada) and Geneva Children’s Hospital (Geneva, Switzerland). Team leaders were attending physicians or senior trainees (i.e. fellows, senior residents) in pediatric intensive care, emergency medicine, anesthesia, general pediatrics, or adult emergency medicine. Medication nurse roles were filled by nurses in pediatric intensive care, emergency medicine, anesthesia, surgery or general pediatrics. All participants were certified in pediatric advanced life support (PALS). During the preceding year, participants had been involved in a mean of 1.2 (SD 1.5) resuscitation events and 4.1 (SD 5.3) simulated resuscitation events. Most participants were naïve to augmented reality technologies (92.6%). Additional participants’ characteristics have been reported previously.23

The InterFACE-AR system

InterFACE-AR is a multi-faceted system designed to provide real-time clinical guidance and decision support for resuscitation teams during cardiac arrest.21, 22, 23, 24 The system is comprised of three core components: (a) a tablet-based Guiding Pad app used by the documenting nurse, designed to collect and track patient and event data (e.g. patient weight, time to clinical interventions, medication orders)24, 25, 26; (b) a large 65-in LCD TeamScreen designed to provide a clinical roadmap for the entire resuscitation team.24 The TeamScreen offers three columns of information: the active cardiac arrest PALS algorithm that tracks clinical progress, CPR and epinephrine timers with a list of current tasks and next steps, and an updated list of medications (i.e. ordered and delivered) and completed tasks24, 25, 26, 27; and (c) Augmented reality devices (Microsoft Hololens 2) for the physician team leader and medication nurse, designed to provide role-specific clinical guidance via holograms projected into the clinical space.22 The team leader’s AR display has multiple holograms providing the PALS algorithm tracking clinical case progression, current tasks, next steps, CPR and epinephrine timers, medication reference, and reversible causes. The medication nurses AR display is comprised of two holograms: one with current tasks, next steps and an epinephrine timer, and the other displaying relevant resuscitation drugs, pre-calculated doses (based on patient weight) and preparation and administration instructions (Fig. 1).Fig. 1 Predefined Areas of Interest.

Study procedure and simulated scenario

Participants were recruited in teams consisting of three people to fill the roles of team leader, medication nurse, and documenting nurse. The two nurses recruited determined which role they wanted to play: medication nurse or documenter. To fill out the rest of the resuscitation team, trained research actors played the highly standardized roles of airway provider, CPR provider, and CPR coach. Those actors assigned to the CPR provider and CPR coach roles switched roles every 2 min under the direction of the team leader. The CPR coach was also responsible for performing defibrillation when ordered by the team leader. All research actors were either physicians or nurses and were previously trained not to provide suggestions or clinical prompts during the simulation.

Participants assigned to the InterFACE-AR group received a video-based orientation to the simulation environment and manikin features, followed by a second video describing the functionality of the entire InterFACE-AR system.23 After watching the videos, participants received hands-on training (5 min) with the AR devices. This training involved following a checklist of tasks to complete for each device. Finally, the entire team participated in a brief, 4-min verbal walk-through cardiac arrest scenario, where the team leader verbalized intended actions, providing an opportunity for the other team members to use their respective devices. No actual tasks were performed on the manikin during the verbal walk-through scenario. Training was concluded after answering any remaining questions from the participants.

For the study scenario, participating teams were asked to manage a simulated cardiac arrest, depicting an 8-year-old child presenting with hyperkalemia-induced cardiac arrest secondary to hemolytic uremic syndrome. The patient presented in pulseless electrical activity, progressed to pulseless ventricular tachycardia and finally to ventricular fibrillation as the scenario progressed. Rhythm changes occurred every four minutes regardless of clinical interventions, and the diagnosis of hyperkalemia was disclosed to the team leader 6.5 min into the scenario. A Zoll R Series feedback defibrillator (Zoll Medical Corporation) was used to provide CPR feedback. Clinical tasks (e.g. intubation, defibrillation, medication administration) were only performed at the direction of the team leader.

Areas of interest

Areas of interest (AOI) were defined a priori and mapped using digital markers within the HoloLens devices. The predefined AOIs corresponded to the functional components of the InterFACE-AR interface, which was developed through an iterative user-centered design process involving frontline healthcare providers.21, 22 A different set of AOIs was defined for the team leader and medication nurse. Team leader AOIs included holograms visible within the AR device (i.e. PALS algorithm, medication reference, reversible causes, current task, next steps, cardiac rhythm, CPR timer, and epinephrine timer) and other objects in the room: TeamScreen LCD display, patient monitor, and the patient (i.e. simulator on stretcher). Medication nurse AOIs included holograms visible within the AR device (i.e. medication reference card, medication orders, advanced preparation and epinephrine timer), the TeamScreen LCD display and the working surface of the medication cart (i.e. countertop). Eye tracking data was collected from the AR devices of the team leaders and medication nurses using the built-in eye-tracking capability of the HoloLens devices (Fig. 1).

Outcome measures

Three key metrics were used: (1) fixation percentage, calculated as the total duration of fixations within an AOI divided by the total duration of the scenario; (2) fixation count: the number of fixations within an AOI during the 12-min scenario; and (3) mean fixation duration, calculated as the total duration of fixations within an AOI divided by the fixation count.

Sample size

This study is a secondary, exploratory analysis of data collected from a published randomized trial; therefore, a formal sample size calculation was not performed for the present analysis. Findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Statistical analysis

The unit of analysis for this descriptive study was the simulation session. Analyses were conducted to characterize visual attention patterns and fixation behavior across predefined AOIs for team leaders and medication nurses. For each AOI, fixation percentage, mean fixation duration, and fixation count were summarized at the session level. Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation). No inferential comparisons were performed.

Results

A total of 9 simulation sessions using the InterFACE-AR system were analyzed. Visual attention data were available for 8 team leaders and 9 medication nurses, with one team leader dataset excluded due to technical issues. On average, each 12-min session included approximately 973 fixation events for team leaders and 1180 for medication nurses. Results are presented as mean (standard deviation) below.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the visual attention patterns of team leaders and medication nurses using an AR-based decision support system during a simulated cardiac arrest. A substantial proportion of visual attention was allocated to AR components in both roles; however, the distribution and patterns of fixations differed. Team leaders demonstrated a more distributed pattern of attention across multiple AOIs, whereas medication nurses exhibited highly concentrated attention on medication-related tasks. To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide insight into how clinicians interact with AR-based cognitive aid during resuscitation.

The observed visual attention patterns appeared consistent with both the role-specific responsibilities of clinicians during resuscitation and the design of the InterFACE-AR system, which was developed with input from frontline emergency physicians and nurses to align with real-world workflow and cognitive demands.21, 22, 23, 24 Key AR components were tailored to the information needs of different team members.22 For medication nurses, drug-related elements were prioritized with non-essential components removed based on user feedback. This design approach was reflected in the observed fixation patterns, with medication nurses directing most of their attention toward the “Medication Order” and “Medication Reference” holograms, which contained time-sensitive medication instructions, dosing information and preparation guidance. The relatively prolonged fixation duration observed for the countertop may similarly reflect sustained attention during the medication preparation tasks.

For team leaders, AR components were designed to support situational awareness, task coordination and real-time decision making and oversight of resuscitation flow. Consistent with this intended function, the “Current Tasks” and “Next Steps” holograms received the greatest proportion of attention, likely because they displayed the most time-sensitive and action-oriented information during the scenario. In contrast, relatively little attention was directed towards the “TeamScreen” display, potentially because much of the same information was already available within the AR interface. Comparatively low attention toward the “CPR Timer” may also reflect the delegation of CPR quality monitoring and compressor switching to the CPR coach role. Distinct fixation patterns were observed across AR components, with some elements characterized by frequent but brief fixations and others by less frequent but more sustained attention, suggesting differing patterns of visual engagement with specific AR elements.28 In general, shorter, more frequent fixation may reflect rapid monitoring of dynamic or frequently updated information, whereas long fixations may indicate more sustained visual processing of complex or detailed information. However, there are no universally accepted threshold defining “short” or “long” fixation duration in resuscitation, and these patterns should be interpreted within the context of the specific clinical tasks. Importantly, findings from our prior randomized trial demonstrated that use of the InterFACE-AR system improved adherence to guideline-recommended epinephrine dosing intervals without compromising other performance metrics23 and was associated with reduced cognitive and overall workload and improved teamwork performance.29 Together, these findings suggest that clinicians were able to incorporate AR-based information into their visual attention during simulated resuscitation. These observations are consistent with, but do not directly demonstrate, cognitive offloading or improved situational awareness.

Prior eye-tracking studies have demonstrated that cognitive aids can draw substantial visual attention during resuscitation. In neonatal resuscitation without cognitive aids, the majority of attention is typically directed toward the patient (approximately 60%), with smaller proportions allocated to monitors and team members.30 Similar results were observed in a pediatric trauma resuscitation study, where the patient and monitor accounted for 35% and 16% of visual attention, respectively.31, 32 However, when feedback devices are introduced, a large proportion of visual attention may shift toward the device itself, accounting for approximately 40–50% of total gaze,33 with reduced attention directed toward the patient and surrounding environment. Previous studies have raised concerns that AR overlays may contribute to attentional tunneling or inattentional blindness by drawing excessive attention toward digital information displays.34, 35 In our study, medication nurses allocated a substantial proportion of visual attention toward AR components, whereas team leaders demonstrated broader distribution of attention across multiple AOIs and the surrounding clinical environment. Importantly, unlike prior studies where increased attention to cognitive aids has been associated with increased workload or potential distraction,16, 28 our findings, together with previous trials within this research program,23, 29 suggest that clinicians were able to interact with AR-based cognitive support during simulated resuscitation without obvious evidence of visual domination by the interface. However, whether similar gaze patterns would be observed during real clinical resuscitation remains unknown.

Attention classified as outside predefined AOIs should not be interpreted as noise. In our study, the use of a head-mounted AR device and built-in eye-tracking capability limited our ability to capture gaze directed toward dynamic elements such as moving team members during resuscitation. In a pediatric trauma simulation eye-tracking study, approximately 34% of team leaders’ gaze was directed toward other team members.31 Previous eye-tracking studies have similarly suggested that a proportion of gaze is directed toward team members, reflecting communication and coordination processes during resuscitation.30, 36, 37 The high proportion of gaze outside predefined AOIs likely reflects a limitation of using the HoloLens built-in eye-tracking system to study dynamic team interactions. While the system enabled objective mapping of gaze to predefined, functionally relevant interface components, gaze directed toward moving team members or other dynamic visual targets could not be reliably classified within predefined AOIs. These findings suggest that attention categorized as outside predefined AOIs may represent meaningful visual attention that was not explicitly captured. Consequently, gaze classified as outside predefined AOIs should not necessarily be interpreted as distraction of irrelevant visual behavior, but rather as part of the complex visual demands of team-based resuscitation. Even in an immersive AR-supported environment, human interaction remained central to resuscitation care.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, gaze data were collected using an AR headset rather than a dedicated eye-tracking device, which may have lower spatial and temporal precision and could result in measurement imprecision. However, this approach enabled direct mapping of gaze to predefined, functionally relevant AOIs, facilitating interpretation of attention toward specific AR components. Second, eye-tracking measures describe visual attention allocation but do not directly assess cognitive processing, cognitive offloading, situational awareness, or distraction. Participants also received only brief familiarization with the AR system before the study. Consequently, the observed gaze patterns likely reflect clinicians during the early stages of interacting with a novel AR-based cognitive aid and may differ as users gain experience. Given the exploratory nature and limited sample size of this descriptive study, we did not examine associations between gaze patterns and other variables. Therefore, the relationship between visual attention and outcomes such as decision-making, cognitive load, and team performance remains unclear. Third, the absence of a control group limits our ability to determine how AR devices influence visual attention. Thus, it is unclear whether the observed patterns are attributable to the AR system or to underlying tasks and role characteristics. Finally, this study was conducted in a simulated pediatric cardiac arrest scenario. Visual attention patterns may differ during real resuscitation, where environmental complexity, emotional stress, competing priorities and interaction with patients and family members may further influence gaze allocation.

Conclusion

Resuscitation team leaders and medication nurses demonstrated substantial allocation of visual attention toward AR-based cognitive aids during simulated pediatric cardiac arrest, with distinct role-specific patterns of visual attention. These findings provide descriptive insights into how clinicians interact with role-specific AR-based cognitive aids during resuscitation. Further studies integrating eye-tracking measures with clinical performance, cognitive workload and implementation outcomes are needed to better understand the role of AR-based decision support during resuscitation.
