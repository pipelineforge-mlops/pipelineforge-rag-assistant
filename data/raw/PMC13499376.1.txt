Highlights

• Lower pre-arrest glucose was associated with greater resuscitation difficulty.

• Lower pre-arrest glucose was associated with more defibrillation shocks and longer CPR duration.

• Sustained ROSC rates were similar between the 36-h and 12-h fasting groups.

• Fasting-related metabolic status may contribute to resuscitation difficulty.

Objective

To investigate whether prolonged fasting-induced lower pre-arrest glycemia above the hypoglycemia alert threshold of 70 mg/dL is associated with defibrillation requirements, CPR duration, and sustained ROSC in a porcine model of ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest.

Methods

Twenty male Landrace pigs were randomized to 36 h or 12 h of solid-food fasting with free access to water before ventricular fibrillation induction. After successful ventricular fibrillation induction, animals underwent 8 min of untreated no-flow cardiac arrest followed by standardized mechanical CPR.

Results

Animals in the prolonged fasting group exhibited significantly lower blood glucose levels at all time points. The 36-h fasting group required more defibrillation shocks than the 12-h fasting group, with median values of 2 shocks (2–3.75) and 1 shock (1–1.75), respectively (P = 0.035). CPR duration was also longer in the 36-h fasting group, with median durations of 344 s (238–784.25) and 191 s (145.75–275), respectively (P = 0.043). Sustained ROSC was achieved in 70% and 90% of animals respectively, with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.582). In univariable Poisson regression, higher pre-arrest blood glucose was associated with fewer defibrillation shocks required, with an incidence rate ratio of 0.980 (95% CI, 0.968–0.993; P = 0.002). During CPR, the 36-h fasting group demonstrated significantly lower MAP (P = 0.035) and mixed venous oxygen saturation (P = 0.030) than the 12-h fasting group.

Conclusion

In this prospective longitudinal porcine model of ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest, prolonged fasting-induced lower pre-arrest glycemia within the physiological range was associated with increased defibrillation requirements and longer CPR duration, despite similar rates of sustained ROSC. These findings indicate that pre-arrest glycemic and metabolic status may be relevant to resuscitation difficulty.

1 Introduction

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is the cornerstone intervention for patients experiencing cardiac arrest.1 While contemporary research has predominantly focused on optimizing mechanical procedural metrics, including chest compression quality and the exact timing of electrical defibrillation,2 the impact of pre-arrest blood glucose levels on resuscitation outcomes remains largely underexplored. Specifically, the role of baseline glycemia in dictating electrophysiological responsiveness has not been adequately addressed.

During cardiac arrest (CA), myocardial tolerance to profound ischemia relies heavily on pre-arrest energy reserves.3 As glucose represents the primary circulating metabolic substrate, the impact of the low pre-arrest glycemic state on immediate electrophysiological responsiveness remains inadequately addressed. While retrospective data highlight the danger of overt hypoglycemia,4 it is currently unknown whether baseline blood glucose at the low-normal limit compromises myocardial oxygen extraction and defibrillation efficacy during CA. While existing studies have explored the relationship between blood glucose and cardiac arrest outcomes, they predominantly focus on long-term prognosis. For instance, Wongtanasarasin et al.5 demonstrated that blood glucose levels below 150 mg/dL were associated with poor neurological recovery. Furthermore, retrospective analyses suggest a correlation between glucose <100 mg/dL and lower rates of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC),6 and case reports indicate that rapid correction of intra-arrest hypoglycemia can facilitate ROSC.7 Notably, the glucose thresholds identified in these studies substantially exceeds the conventional definition of hypoglycemia (typically <70 mg/dL or <3.9 mmol/L).8 However, whether lower pre-arrest glycemia within the physiological range is associated with defibrillation requirements and resuscitation outcomes remains unclear. Consequently, the primary objective of this study was to determine whether prolonged fasting-induced lower pre-arrest glycemia within the physiological range is associated with defibrillation requirements and resuscitation outcomes in a porcine model of ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest.

2 Materials and methods

All animals received humane care in strict compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, National Academies Press, 2011) and the 'Principles of Laboratory Animal Care' formulated by the National Society for Medical Research. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Capital Medical University (Approval No. 2025-Dong-179) and conducted at its Laboratory Animal Center. All procedures adhered to relevant institutional policies, national guidelines, and the fundamental principles of EU Directive 2010/63/EU regarding the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. Reporting follows the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

2.1 Experimental animal preparation

Twenty healthy male Landrace pigs (10–12 weeks, 30 ± 4 kg) were purchased. Based on preliminary observations and related physiological literature,9, 10, 11 we estimated that a sample size of 10 animals per group would provide 80% power to detect a mean difference of 3 defibrillation shocks between the groups, assuming a standard deviation of 2.4 and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Utilizing a random number sequence, the animals were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either a standard fasting group (n = 10) or a prolonged fasting group (n = 10). Investigators responsible for data acquisition and statistical analysis were strictly blinded to the group assignments. Because exogenous insulin-induced hypoglycemia can prolong the QT interval and confound defibrillation thresholds,12 we deliberately used a physical-fasting protocol with ad libitum water, to induce glycemic stratification without pharmacological manipulation.

A 36-hour fast was employed to achieve low-normal fasting blood glucose levels without inducing overt hemodynamic instability,13 a 12-hour fast served as the control. Both groups were housed at 26℃.

Animals were initially sedated with midazolam (0.5 mg/kg, intramuscular) and subsequently induced with propofol (2 mg/kg, intravenous) via the ear vein. Following induction, each animal was secured in the supine position. General anesthesia was maintained through a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol (2.0 mg/kg/h). Once an adequate depth of anesthesia was achieved, the animals were intubated with a 6.5-mm endotracheal tube and placed on mechanical ventilation (Hamilton-C1, Switzerland) in a volume-controlled mode. The ventilation parameters were established as follows: tidal volume at 8 mL/kg, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) at 5 cmH2O, and a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) at 21%. The respiratory rate was dynamically adjusted to maintain the end-tidal partial pressure of carbon dioxide (ETCO2) within a physiological range of 35–45 mmHg. Furthermore, isotonic saline was continuously administered to all animals at a rate of 5 mL/kg/h to maintain fluid balance.

2.2 Experimental procedures

After 15 min of anesthesia and stabilization, the body surface area (BSA) was measured and equipment was checked. A triple-lumen central venous catheter (Arrowguard™ Blue Plus™, Arrow, American) was inserted through the right external jugular vein into the right atrium to monitor intra-atrial pressure, collect mixed venous blood, and administer fluids. A PiCCO arterial catheter (Pulsion Medical Systems, Munich, Germany) was inserted into the right femoral artery for continuous arterial pressure and blood temperature monitoring and arterial blood sampling. A 7F catheter sheath (Radifocus Introducer II H, Terumo, Japan) was inserted into the right femoral vein, an electrode catheter was placed. Ventricular fibrillation (VF) was induced by applying 50 V/25 Hz alternating current stimulation through a pacing electrode positioned in the right ventricle. Successful VF induction was confirmed by a characteristic VF waveform on the ECG and a decrease in mean arterial pressure to <25 mmHg. Mechanical ventilation was then discontinued, and the endotracheal tube was disconnected from the ventilator. Following 8 min of untreated VF, precordial compressions were performed with a mechanical chest compressor at 100 compressions/min.14 Coincident with the initiation of compressions, animals were mechanically ventilated in an asynchronous, volume-controlled mode with a target tidal volume of 8–10 mL/kg, an FiO2 of 1.0, a respiratory rate of 10 breaths/min, a PEEP of 5 cmH2O, and an I:E ratio of 1:2. Epinephrine (1 mg IV) was first administered at 2 min after CPR initiation. If ROSC was not achieved, CPR was continued with intravenous administration of 1 mg epinephrine every 4 min. Each resuscitation cycle consisted of 2 min of continuous CPR, followed by a rigorously timed 8-second interruption in chest compressions immediately prior to attempted defibrillation.9 If a shockable rhythm was confirmed, a single 150 J biphasic shock was delivered (Zoll R-Series, Zoll Medical, USA). If ROSC was not achieved, or if it was achieved but failed to persist for 1 min, the identical 2-minute CPR algorithm was immediately resumed before any subsequent rhythm analysis or defibrillation attempts. Parallel to the resuscitation efforts, arterial blood was collected for blood gas analysis after 3 min of CPR, and the blood glucose level was recorded as Intra-CPR GLU.

CPR was terminated upon the achievement of sustained ROSC. Sustained ROSC was defined as the restoration of an organized supraventricular rhythm with effective hemodynamic output, evidenced by MAP ≥50 mmHg sustained for at least 10 consecutive minutes without ongoing chest compressions. Resuscitation was performed for a maximum of 15 min. Failure to achieve ROSC within this window was defined as resuscitation failure. After ROSC, mechanical ventilation and anesthesia were maintained during the 15-min observation period. No vasopressors, antiarrhythmic drugs, glucose-containing solutions, or other resuscitation medications were administered during this period, except for maintenance fluid required for anesthesia and catheter patency. At the end of the experiment, animals that remained alive were euthanized under deep anesthesia by intravenous administration of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg). Death was confirmed by the sustained absence of spontaneous respiration, an arterial pressure waveform, and cardiac electrical activity.

2.3 Physiological recording

Arterial and mixed venous whole-blood samples were collected into heparinized blood gas syringes at three predefined time points. Pre-arrest measurements were defined as those obtained after anesthesia, instrumentation, and stabilization and before VF induction. The preparation time, defined as the interval from anesthesia induction to VF induction, was recorded for each animal. Post-VF samples were collected immediately after confirmation of VF induction and before the 8-min untreated VF period. Intra-CPR samples were collected at 3 min after CPR initiation during ongoing mechanical chest compressions. All samples were analyzed immediately without storage or centrifugation using a GEM Premier 3000 blood gas analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, Bedford, MA, USA). Arterial blood glucose concentration was measured using the analyzer’s amperometric glucose oxidase electrode method. Femoral arterial pressure and right atrial pressure (RAP) were continuously monitored through the PiCCO arterial catheter and right atrial catheter, respectively. For the intra-CPR assessment, aortic pressure and RAP were recorded during the first minute after CPR initiation while mechanical chest compressions were ongoing and before the administration of epinephrine or delivery of defibrillation shocks. For each compression cycle, minimum-pressure coronary perfusion pressure (CPP) was calculated as the minimum femoral arterial pressure minus the time-coincident RAP, and the resulting values were averaged over the 1-min recording period for each animal. Cardiac output, cardiac index, cumulative epinephrine dose, number of defibrillation shocks, and CPR duration were also recorded (Fig. 1).Fig. 1 Experimental protocol and measurement timeline.

2.4 Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was the defibrillation burden, defined as the total number of shocks required during resuscitation. Secondary outcomes included the total duration of CPR, the cumulative dose of epinephrine administered, the rate of sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), and key hemodynamic parameters.

2.5 Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed via Q-Q plots and histograms. Given the small sample size (N = 20) and the non-normal distribution of several key physiological parameters, all continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test with Hodges–Lehmann median differences and 95% confidence intervals.

A Poisson regression model was employed to evaluate the independent predictive value of pre-arrest blood glucose levels for the number of defibrillation shocks required.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3.4 Hemodynamic parameters

Prior to the initiation of CPR, the mean arterial pressure (MAP) did not differ significantly between the two groups with a median difference of 11 mmHg (95% CI, 0–23; P = 0.075). However, during the active CPR phase, the MAP in the 36-h fasting group was significantly lower than that in the 12-h fasting group with a median difference of 14.5 mmHg (95% CI, 1–24; P = 0.035). RAP did not differ significantly between the groups before VF induction, after VF induction, or during CPR. CPP did not differ significantly between the groups during CPR (P = 0.739) (Table 2).

4 Discussion

In this porcine model of cardiac arrest, prolonged fasting-induced lower pre-arrest glycemia was associated with a greater number of defibrillation shocks and a longer duration of CPR, despite comparable rates of sustained ROSC. Higher pre-arrest blood glucose was also associated with fewer defibrillation shocks in the univariable Poisson regression analysis. These findings suggest an association between pre-arrest glycemic status and resuscitation difficulty.

The prognostic significance of pre-arrest blood glucose in the context of cardiac arrest remains a subject of ongoing clinical debate. A previous retrospective analysis demonstrated that elevated glucose levels do not effectively improve the rate of sustained ROSC.15 In the context of CPR, an increased defibrillation requirement may reflect the refractoriness of ventricular fibrillation and myocardial electrophysiological instability. Importantly, repeated electrical shocks not only exacerbate direct electrical and thermal injury to the myocardium, but also inevitably prolong the overall duration of resuscitative efforts.16 As extensively documented, prolonged CPR duration is a critical, independent risk factor for both decreased survival to hospital discharge and poor neurological prognoses.17, 18 These findings collectively suggest that even when ROSC is ultimately achieved, patients resuscitated under conditions of low-normal glycemia are exposed to a substantially greater ischemic and mechanical burden during the resuscitation process itself. The present study did not extend observation beyond ROSC. Therefore, post-resuscitation hemodynamic recovery, myocardial function, and neurological outcomes were not assessed. Whether the increased resuscitative burden incurred in the low-normal glycemic cohort translates into measurably worse post-resuscitation outcomes therefore remains to be determined, and represents an important direction for future investigation.

Previous studies examining the association between blood glucose levels and cardiac arrest outcomes have predominantly relied on retrospective clinical data and may therefore have been affected by residual confounding and variations in CPR quality.19, 20, 21, 22 Current resuscitation guidelines emphasize early recognition, high-quality chest compressions, and early defibrillation, but do not specifically address the potential influence of pre-arrest glycemic status on resuscitation difficulty.23, 24, 25 The present controlled porcine study extends the existing evidence by examining this association under a standardized mechanical CPR protocol. Although sustained ROSC rates did not differ significantly between the groups, animals with prolonged fasting-induced lower glycemia required more defibrillation shocks and a longer duration of CPR. These findings complement previous outcome-based clinical studies by suggesting that pre-arrest glycemic status may contribute to variability in the resuscitation process even when its association with ROSC is not statistically apparent. However, the results should not be interpreted as supporting glucose administration during CPR, because no glycemic intervention was evaluated in this study.

The effectiveness of CPR depends on both the blood flow generated by chest compressions and the balance between systemic oxygen delivery and tissue oxygen consumption during ischemia.26, 27 During CPR, MAP was lower in the 36-h fasting group, whereas no significant between-group difference was observed in minimum-pressure CPP. Mixed venous oxygen saturation was also lower in the 36-h fasting group. This finding may reflect reduced systemic oxygen delivery, increased tissue oxygen extraction.28 Accordingly, the combined hemodynamic findings are consistent with an altered systemic oxygen delivery–consumption balance during CPR but do not establish impaired myocardial substrate utilization or contractile function.

Some perspectives argue that lower blood glucose levels is merely an epiphenomenon of severe underlying illness or a marker of disease severity, rather than a direct contributor to cardiac arrest pathophysiology.29 This view is reflected in the removal of hypoglycemia from the list of reversible causes in current ACLS guidelines.30 The use of otherwise healthy animals reduced confounding from chronic disease, organ dysfunction, and concomitant medications. Nevertheless, prolonged fasting produces metabolic changes beyond lowering blood glucose, including alterations in glycogen stores, insulin and counter-regulatory hormones, lipolysis, free fatty acids, and ketone-body availability.31, 32, 33 Because these variables were not measured, the observed differences cannot be attributed specifically to blood glucose. Ketone bodies and free fatty acids can serve as alternative myocardial substrates and may influence myocardial energy production during ischemia.34 Therefore, lower blood glucose may represent a measurable marker of the broader fasting-induced metabolic state, and its specific contribution independent of other fasting-related metabolic changes remains to be determined.

5 Limitations

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, the study was not designed to determine the cellular or molecular mechanisms underlying the association between glycemic status and resuscitation outcomes. The mechanistic interpretations therefore remain inferential and require further investigation. Second, because femoral arterial pressure may not accurately reflect central aortic pressure during chest compressions, the resulting CPP values may not be directly comparable with those reported in previous studies or with the approximately 15 mmHg threshold associated with ROSC.28, 35 Third, glycemic stratification was achieved through prolonged fasting rather than pharmacological manipulation to avoid potential insulin-related metabolic confounding.36 However, fasting may alter metabolic factors beyond blood glucose, including glycogen stores, hormonal responses, free fatty acids, and ketone bodies. As these variables were not measured, the observed differences cannot be attributed specifically to blood glucose. Future studies using controlled dietary or pharmacological interventions, together with serial measurements of ketone bodies, free fatty acids, hormonal responses, and myocardial metabolic markers, are required to distinguish the specific contribution of blood glucose from the broader metabolic effects of prolonged fasting.

6 Conclusion

In this prospective longitudinal porcine model of ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest, prolonged fasting-induced lower pre-arrest glycemia within the physiological range was associated with increased defibrillation requirements and longer CPR duration, despite similar rates of sustained ROSC. These findings indicate that pre-arrest glycemic and metabolic status may be relevant to resuscitation difficulty.
