Daniela Correia and colleagues estimated the potential reduction in cancer incidence in the European Union (EU) if all countries had implemented comprehensive and effective alcohol control measures in 2012.1 Given the very high levels of alcohol use and attributable health burden in the EU, coupled with insufficient progress in alcohol control policy, this study is a valuable addition to the field.

To estimate the impact of alcohol control policies, the authors refer to studies identified in the IARC Handbook 20B.2 With regard to marketing bans, the Handbook identified four studies from which the authors selected a study from Norway, where marketing was banned in 1975.3 However, the studies in the Handbook do present conflicting evidence on the impact of marketing bans, so it is unclear why the Norwegian study was chosen. For example, the Handbook identified a more robust multi-country study controlling for concurrent policies and confounders which found that marketing bans were linked to a 10.7% increase in consumption (model 3 of Table 5 in 4). As evidence of the effectiveness of marketing bans at a population level is inconclusive (see comprehensive reviews in 5,6), the current approach gives the impression of cherry-picking.

Based on our understanding of the literature, we would like to suggest a humbler approach with more realistic assumptions for future modelling studies.

Firstly, the short-term impact of marketing bans may be restricted to adolescents. There is robust evidence that alcohol marketing influences adolescents' alcohol consumption.7 This is mirrored by recent evidence of a short-term reduction of drinking to intoxication among adolescents following a comprehensive marketing ban in Lithuania.8 However, the same marketing ban in Lithuania was not linked to short-term changes in per capita consumption.9 Therefore, short-term modelling of marketing bans should focus on adolescent drinking behaviours rather than population-level consumption.

Secondly, the population-level effects of marketing bans may only unfold over time. We expect two key mechanisms for a population-level impact of marketing bans: over time, new generations of young people who drink less will make up a growing share of the population; and less marketing exposure and reduced drinking among younger populations will shape social norms relating to alcohol consumption in the population as a whole.10 However, it may take years or decades before such an impact is reflected in per capita consumption. To account for such nuanced changes in modelling studies, however, more empirical data is required.

Based on the available evidence, the estimates of avoided cancer incidence due to marketing bans are likely to be substantial overestimates. Furthermore, the modelling undertaken creates a misleading impression that the selected alcohol control policies have a similar health impact. However, the evidence for a short-term impact on health burden from taxation policies and availability restrictions is much more robust. This evidence gap should be clearly communicated to the public in order to avoid creating false expectations and to facilitate evidence-based policymaking.
