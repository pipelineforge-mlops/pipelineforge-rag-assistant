Drug identification and screening are crucial in precision medicine and pharmaceutical research, yet they face challenges such as interference from complex biological matrices, insufficient sensitivity for trace detection, and poor specificity. Multifunctional metal-organic framework (MOF) composites, leveraging their tunable pore structures, high specific surface area, and modular functional integration capabilities, have emerged as promising solutions to address these issues. Despite rapid progress, a systematic review comprehensively consolidating these advances remains absent. This review systematically summarizes the design strategies and performance advantages of four representative types of MOF composites (carbon dots (CDs)-MOFs, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs)-MOFs, biomacromolecules-MOFs, covalent organic frameworks (COFs)-MOFs), along with their applications in multimodal sensing (fluorescence, electrochemical, colorimetric, and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)) and high-efficiency screening platforms (offline ligand fishing (LF) and immobilized enzyme microreactors (IMERs)). Collective findings demonstrate that rationally engineered multifunctional MOF composites can significantly meet the stringent requirements of drug analysis regarding sensitivity, selectivity, and stability. This review also provides an in-depth discussion of current challenges and future directions in the field, including stability, structure-activity relationships, biocompatibility, and scalable fabrication, aiming to offer design insights for the development of drug identification and screening technologies and to fill the gap in systematic reviews in this area.

1 Introduction

Drug identification aims to determine the chemical structure and presence of drug components, whereas drug screening focuses on the rapid discovery of candidate compounds with targeted biological activity from complex matrices. In the era of precision medicine, advanced technologies for drug sensing and identification are pivotal for ensuring drug quality and clinical safety, while the efficiency and accuracy of drug screening critically determine the success of new drug development. Consequently, advancing highly efficient, sensitive, and selective platforms for drug sensing, identification, and screening represents an urgent imperative for safeguarding public health and driving innovation [1]. Although conventional methods such as chromatography and spectroscopy meet the fundamental requirements in specific contexts, they exhibit inherent limitations when analyzing complex biological matrices like blood and tissue fluid, including insufficient sensitivity for trace drug detection, laborious and time-intensive sample pretreatment, low throughput for large-scale screening, and compromised selectivity in complex biological environments [[2], [3], [4]]. These shortcomings significantly constrain the application of traditional techniques in modern high-precision pharmaceutical research and clinical practice. With the continuous emergence of novel drug molecules and advanced therapies, the demand for high-end drug identification and screening technologies continues to rise, especially when processing complex biological samples or screening lead compounds from vast compound libraries. There is an urgent need for methods with rapid response, ultra-high sensitivity, and excellent specificity.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) offer a highly promising avenue to address these challenges. Leveraging their ultrahigh specific surface area, highly tunable and ordered pore structures, and programmable chemical microenvironments, MOFs exhibit distinctive merits in targeted drug enrichment, enhanced sensing signal transduction, and the construction of biocompatible detection interfaces [5]. However, intrinsic challenges associated with MOFs-namely poor aqueous stability, limited functionalization versatility, and inadequate recyclability-pose significant barriers to their practical implementation in real-world pharmaceutical analysis.

To overcome the limitations inherent in single-component MOFs, such as restricted signal dimensionality, challenging recovery, and stability issues, researchers have engineered multifunctional MOF composites through sophisticated synergistic integration with auxiliary functional materials, including fluorescent probes, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), biomacromolecules (e.g., enzymes, antibodies, and aptamers (Apt)), and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. These composites not only mitigate the intrinsic shortcomings of pristine MOFs but also substantially expand and enhance their applicability in drug sensing, identification, and screening through complementary advantages and synergistic amplification (a “1 + 1 > 2” effect) among constituent materials. For example, fluorescent probes confer high-sensitivity multimodal optical signaling [10,11]; MNPs enable efficient magnetic separation/enrichment and facile sensor regeneration [12,13]; biomacromolecule modifications provide superior biorecognition specificity and targeting [14,15]; while COFs’ integration optimizes pore environments and introduces novel functionalities for refined molecular discrimination and signal modulation [16,17]. Consequently, MOF composites exhibit markedly superior overall analytical performance compared to single-component systems or conventional methods, demonstrating lower limit of detection (LOD), enhanced resistance to complex matrix interference (high selectivity), accelerated response kinetics, and streamlined workflows (e.g., magnetic separation simplifying pretreatment). Crucially, their high degree of integration, modularity, and programmable design endow them with excellent adaptability to diverse analytical needs, making them transformative platforms for developing a new generation of intelligent drug sensing and screening systems with real-time monitoring, dynamic response, and closed-loop control capabilities.

Although numerous reviews have covered MOF composites in environmental remediation and food safety [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]], to our knowledge, a systematic analysis of their applications in the field of drug recognition and screening remains notably absent. This article fills this critical gap by systematically integrating research advances from the past decade and providing a comprehensive evaluation of the innovative applications of MOF composites in pharmaceutical analysis. This review first outlines the development, classification, and key characteristics of MOF composites. It then elaborates on the preparation strategies and performance characteristics of MOF composites. Subsequently, it highlights their applications in multimodal sensing platforms, i.e. fluorescence, electrochemical, colorimetric, surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) and screening platforms ligand fishing (LF) and immobilized enzyme microreactors (IMERs). Through representative case studies, it demonstrates the practical efficacy of various composites in drug identification and screening via these platforms. Finally, it assesses current challenges related to sensitivity, selectivity, and scalability and proposes future research directions. As the first review dedicated to synergistic mechanisms and innovative applications of multifunctional MOF composites in drug recognition and screening, this work analyzes material design strategies, platform architectures, and practical case studies, thereby contributing innovative perspectives to advance pharmaceutical analysis and guide the design of future high-performance drug analysis platforms.

2 Development of MOF composites for pharmaceutical analysis

MOFs are porous coordination polymers formed through the self-assembly of metal ion clusters or metal ions with organic ligands. They have rapidly emerged as promising materials in drug recognition and screening due to the following distinctive characteristics:(1) Structural diversity and tunability. MOFs possess remarkable structural diversity. Their pore size, channel shape, and dimensions can be precisely tailored through the selection of different metal ions and organic ligands. Metal ions not only serve as the central nodes of the MOF framework but also act as functional carriers. The diversity and multifunctionality of metal ions facilitate the construction of MOFs with rich topological structures and functionalities. Furthermore, the diversity in the configuration and coordination modes of organic ligands endows MOFs with a wealth of structural features. This structural tunability enables MOFs to adapt to the size and shape of diverse drug molecules, thereby achieving efficient drug recognition and screening.

(2) High specific surface area and porosity. The inherently high specific surface area and porosity of MOFs significantly increase the contact area between drug molecules and the material, enhancing the sensitivity of drug recognition and screening processes.

(3) Amenability to functionalization. The structural framework of MOFs allows for extensive functional modification. Through post-synthetic modification (PSM) strategies, specific functional groups can be introduced into the pore channels, enabling selective and specific drug recognition capabilities.

(4) Potential for biocompatibility and stability under controlled conditions. Certain MOFs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and stability. These properties minimize interference with biological systems during drug recognition and screening, thereby improving accuracy. Concurrently, robust structural stability allows MOFs to maintain integrity under chemically harsh conditions, making them suitable for analytical detection in challenging environments. This stability also contributes to extending the material's operational lifespan and reducing usage costs.

However, it is crucial to note that these advantages are highly dependent on the MOF composition and the operating environment. MOFs possess several intrinsic limitations that hinder their broader application. Firstly, a critical limitation for biomedical applications is their inherent susceptibility to aqueous environments. Many prominent MOFs, including south china university of technology crystal-19 (SCUTC-19), MOF-74, and amino-functionalized matériaux de l'institut lavoisier (MIL-125-NH2), readily and irreversibly decompose upon exposure to aqueous conditions encountered in physiological environments, leading to structural collapse [23]. Significant efforts have focused on enhancing hydrolytic stability, primarily through (1) De novo synthesis using high-valent metal ions (e.g., Cr3+, Fe3+, Al3+, Zr4+): that form strong M–O bonds and high coordination numbers. (2) PSM with hydrophobic groups to shield frameworks from water [24]. However, these strategies face inherent trade-offs: (1) High-valent metal synthesis is complex that severely restricts the diversity of obtainable MOF structures. (2) Hydrophobic PSM compromises interfacial interactions in aqueous media, quenches intrinsic fluorescence, and reduces biocompatibility, thus fundamentally limiting their utility in biological sensing and in vivo applications.

Secondly, the synthesis of MOFs often requires expensive organic ligands, such as 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate (or terephthalic acid, H2BDC), which significantly increase production costs and diminish their cost-effectiveness. To address this, researchers have employed two primary strategies: (1) Alternative ligand sourcing. Researchers employed organic ligands recovered from recyclable municipal solid waste instead of commercial sources. For example, H2BDC has been successfully recovered from polyethylene terephthalate waste bottles via ultrasound-assisted, phase-transfer-catalyzed alkaline hydrolysis under mild conditions. The high-purity H2BDC obtained was then used as the organic ligand in the synthesis of an iron-based MOF-derived magnetic porous carbon (α-Fe/Fe3C) composite for the removal of tetracycline (TC) hydrochloride [25]. (2) Performance enhancement. The high cost of MOF materials also impacts their overall performance metrics, often quantified as a figure of merit (FOM). To improving FOM, researchers have employed strategies like metal doping and semiconductor coupling [26]. For instance, combining TiO2 with MOFs to prepare MIL-101(Fe)/TiO2 composites has shown excellent photocatalytic activity, achieving an FOM of 1.62 × 10−10 mol·L/(g·J·h) and degrading 92.76% of TC within 10 min under sunlight [27]. However, these approaches exhibit critical limitations: (1) Waste-derived ligands are restricted to simple commodity linkers (e.g., H2BDC), failing to reduce costs for complex functional ligands essential for advanced drug screening; (2) Derived composites largely bypass direct MOF utilization (e.g., carbon composites are end-products, not functional MOFs) (3) Performance-enhanced systems (e.g., MOF/TiO2); introduce additional synthesis complexity and cost, counteracting the original cost-reduction goals.

Thirdly, MOFs prepared by conventional synthesis methods are typically in powder form, exhibiting weak mechanical strength. Issues such as limited functionality and complex recovery also restrict their application in drug recognition and screening. Therefore, MOF composites emerge as a transformative solution. By integrating MOFs with functional materials, MOF composites not only address the inherent chemical instability of MOFs but also integrate advantageous properties from other materials, such as high porosity, large specific surface area, optical, electrical, magnetic, and catalytic properties. MOF composites can even exhibit novel synergistic properties unattainable with single components.

By integrating MOFs with other functional nanomaterials (such as MNPs, carbon dots (CDs), and biomacromolecules), not only are the limitations of single-component MOFs addressed, but also a “1 + 1 > 2” synergistic effect is achieved, which significantly enhancing the performance of drug analysis across multiple dimensions. This synergistic effect is primarily manifested through four key mechanisms:(1) Enhanced stability. The structural characteristics of MOFs allow them to act as a protective matrix, encapsulating guest materials. This encapsulation effectively enhances the stability and contaminant resistance of the guest components under harsh conditions, ensuring the long-term performance and reliability of the composite in practical applications [28].

(2) Improved selectivity. The incorporation of recognition elements (e.g., antibodies, Apts, molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs)) provides high-affinity binding sites for target drug molecules, while the size-selective porosity of MOFs contributes a molecular sieving effect. Together, these features establish a dual screening mechanism that markedly reduces interference from complex sample matrices [29].

(3) Simplified operation and enrichment. The introduction of MNPs enables the composite to achieve rapid separation and enrichment via an external magnetic field, which greatly simplifying the sample pretreatment process and improving analysis throughput [30,31].

(4) Amplified sensing signal. Integration of fluorescent materials such as CDs with MOFs facilitates signal enhancement or ratiometric output, while the incorporation of conductive components (e.g., graphene) improves charge transfer within the composite. These enhancements collectively LOD and support ultra-sensitive detection capabilities [32].

To date, researchers have successfully developed functional MOF composites integrated with various guest materials, including nanoparticles (e.g., metal nanoparticles), carbon materials (e.g., graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), CDs), silicon-based materials, polymers, and functional molecules (e.g., dyes, biomacromolecules, metal porphyrins) [33]. Among these, the following four categories have shown significant application potential in the field of drug identification and screening: CDs, MNPs, biomacromolecules, and COFs. With advances in precision design strategies (e.g., biomimetic mineralization, in situ growth), such materials hold great potential to break through the sensitivity and selectivity boundaries of traditional drug analysis technologies. This review focuses specifically on these four particularly promising composite types: CDs-MOFs, MNPs-MOFs, COFs-MOFs, and biomacromolecules-MOFs, which have already demonstrated significant potential in drug identification and screening.

3 Types of MOF composites

By integrating MOFs with functional nanomaterials, composite structures overcome many limitations of single-component MOFs, such as restricted signal output, low stability, poor recyclability, and weak electrical conductivity, while introducing significantly enhanced functional properties. These improvements, including high fluorescence, strong magnetic response, and good biocompatibility arising from synergistic effects, establish MOF composites as promising materials for pharmaceutical analysis. This section focuses on the composition, synthesis strategies, and structural control of four representative types of MOF composites, providing a foundation for understanding their applicability in drug identification and screening.

3.1 CDs-MOFs

In the field of drug identification, particularly for real-time, in situ fluorescence detection of trace drug molecules in complex biological matrices, the greatest challenges are background fluorescence interference, poor photostability of probes, and insufficient specificity. CDs represent a class of novel fluorescent nanomaterials characterized by excellent fluorescence tunability, good biocompatibility, water solubility, diverse raw material sources, and ease of functionalization [34,35]. By integrated with MOFs, which serve as solid supports and pre-concentration matrices, CDs gain protection against fluorescence quenching while targets are selectively enriched through MOF pores. This combination synergistically enhances fluorescence output, leading to significantly improved selectivity and reliability in detection. This section discusses how rational material design and synthesis strategies exploit this synergy, providing a foundation for developing next-generation fluorescent sensing platforms [36].

3.1.1 Synthetic strategies

The choice of synthesis pathway for CDs-MOFs directly determines the spatial distribution of the CDs, their fluorescence performance, and the stability of the composite, all of which collectively influence the final analytical performance of the sensor. 3.1.1.1 One-step synthesis approach

This method involves the simultaneous formation of CDs and MOFs through a single synthetic step by mixing their respective precursors [38]. While offering simplicity and rapid processing, this approach presents several limitations. Primarily, the disparate synthesis conditions required for MOFs and CDs, particularly regarding temperature requirements, pose significant challenges. MOFs synthesis typically occurs at relatively low temperatures (<150 °C), whereas CDs formation generally necessitates higher carbonization temperatures (>150 °C), making simultaneous synthesis in a unified reaction system problematic. Additionally, even when temperature compatibility is achieved, potential interference between CDs formation and MOFs crystallization must be considered, along with possible fluorescence quenching by MOFs precursors. These factors collectively limit the precise control over the growth processes of both components, often resulting in suboptimal composite structures and properties. The one-step method, while simple, is susceptible to mutual precursor interference between MOFs and CDs, leading to heterogeneous structures and properties that are detrimental to highly reproducible drug quantification.

3.1.1.2 Two-step synthesis approach

This predominant synthesis strategy involves the separate preparation of CDs and MOFs, followed by their integration through physical mixing or in situ encapsulation techniques [39]. Physical mixing employs impregnation or PSM to combine pre-formed components, preserving crystallinity and fluorescence but constrained by size compatibility between CDs and MOFs. Alternatively, the in situ encapsulation method introduces pre-synthesized CDs into the MOFs precursor solution, followed by controlled reaction under optimized MOFs synthesis conditions to fabricate CDs-MOFs. This strategy provides three key advantages: (1) A streamlined and efficient fabrication process with demonstrated versatility across diverse CDs-MOFs [6]; (2) Significantly improved interfacial compatibility and interaction between CDs and MOFs, resulting from their simultaneous growth and integration; (3) The ability to precisely control the distribution and spatial arrangement of CDs within the MOF matrix by carefully regulating synthesis parameters enables the optimization of composite performance. As a result, the two-step method, especially in situ encapsulation, has become the predominant strategy. Its principal advantage lies in its capacity to maximize the fluorescence quantum yield of CDs while ensuring their uniform encapsulation within the MOF pores. This structural configuration plays a pivotal role in drug sensing applications. Firstly, it effectively confines CDs within the MOF framework, preventing their leakage into complex biological media such as serum and urine, thereby ensuring remarkable signal stability. Secondly, the MOF pores function as nanoreactors that preconcentrate target drug molecules and facilitate their efficient interaction with the encapsulated CDs, leading to a significantly amplified fluorescence response. Finally, the size-exclusive porosity of MOFs selectively excludes larger interfering species, including proteins and other biomacromolecules, thereby minimizing non-specific adsorption and greatly enhancing detection selectivity in complex matrices.

3.1.2 Structural and property control

Precise structural and functional modulation of CDs-MOFs can be achieved by optimizing the selection of CDs and MOFs constituents, tuning synthesis parameters, adjusting component ratios, and controlling hybridization strategies, based on the roles of CDs and MOFs during CDs-MOFs formation [6,40]. The participation of CDs in the MOFs self-assembly process may induce structural deviations compared to parent MOFs frameworks. For example, Liu et al. [41] demonstrated that MOFs crystal growth undergoes significant modification upon CDs incorporation. Composites fabricated using CDs synthesized at varying tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB) concentrations exhibited distinct morphological. With increasing TEAB concentration, the resulting CDs-MOFs progressively shifted through rod-like structures and urchin-like forms to cubic configurations, ultimately adopting irregular architectures. This transition was attributed to TEAB-mediated alterations in interactions between CDs and MOFs precursors, highlighting the critical influence of synthesis parameters on composite architecture.

Moreover, the porous architecture of MOFs provides a spatially confined environment for the growth of CDs. In CDs-MOFs, MOFs matrices where the spatial constraints enable the formation of uniformly sized CDs whose dimensions match the pore size of MOFs [42]. The size confinement promotes intimate contact between CDs and MOFs, facilitating efficient electron/energy transfer. Consequently, this enhances the composite's fluorescence properties while improving stability, selectivity, and sensitivity for sensing applications. Electronic/energy transfer between CDs and MOFs can induce fluorescence intensity variations, while their integration creates functional group interactions that alter emission wavelengths. Differential spatial positioning of CDs within MOFs modifies electron transition modes between surface groups and MOF matrices, leading to red/blue-shifted emission. These mechanisms enable precise structural and optical property modulation for developing functionally tailored CDs-MOFs.

3.1.3 Advantages and challenges in drug identification and screening

The integration of CDs with MOFs offers a promising material platform to overcome critical bottlenecks in drug sensing, combining exceptional signal amplification, superior selectivity, and enhanced stability. The ultrahigh porosity of MOFs enables efficient preconcentration of target drug molecules around the CDs, dramatically enhancing local concentration and amplifying fluorescence signals by orders of magnitude, essential for achieving ultra-low LOD. Furthermore, the well-defined micropores of MOFs provide molecular sieving that excludes larger biomacromolecules such as proteins, while tailored functionalization of pore surfaces (e.g., with –NH2 or –COOH groups) facilitates specific interactions through hydrogen bonding or π–π stacking, establishing a dual screening mechanism that significantly improves recognition accuracy in complex biological media [6]. In addition, the MOF matrix serves as a protective microenvironment that mitigates photobleaching and chemical degradation of CDs, prolonging functional lifespan and enabling practical recovery and reuse via centrifugation or filtration, thereby reducing operational costs [36].

Despite these advantages, challenges remain including insufficient mechanistic understanding of interfacial energy/electron transfer and drug–composite interactions, interference from nonspecific adsorption and biofouling in real biological samples such as serum and urine, and limited quantification reliability due to sensitivity to environmental variables like pH and ionic strength. Future efforts should focus on developing built-in reference strategies such as ratiometric sensing, improving antifouling surface properties, and employing machine learning methods to interpret complex signals and guide the design of robust sensing systems.

3.2 MNPs-MOFs

MNPs provide strong magnetic responsiveness, enabling rapid separation and enrichment, while MOFs offer high specific surface area and selective adsorption capacity. The integration of these two components combines separation, enrichment, and even detection functionalities into a single system, significantly simplifying operational procedures, shortening analysis time, and reducing sample loss. This makes them particularly suitable for high-throughput drug screening (HTS) and the extraction of trace drugs from complex matrices.

3.2.1 Synthetic strategies

The synthesis of MNPs-MOFs seeks to optimize the interplay among magnetic content, specific surface area, and structural stability, factors that critically govern their efficiency in drug separation and enrichment. Structurally, these composites can be categorized into core-shell and non-core-shell architectures, with the selection of a particular synthesis pathway being dictated by the required structural precision and intended performance in the target application.

In core-shell architectures, MNPs serve as the core encapsulated by a MOF shell through heterogeneous epitaxial growth. Primary preparation strategies include the bottle-around-the-ship method, modified one-pot method, and ship-in-a-bottle method (Fig. 2B) [37]. The ship-in-a-bottle method first involves surface functionalization of pre-synthesized MNP cores (e.g., using surfactants, capping agents, or introducing a buffer layer) to promote the self-assembly of MOF precursors and subsequent epitaxial growth of the shell around the magnetic core. This strategy allows flexible adjustment of core size/shape and MOF shell thickness/composition under mild reaction conditions, making it the mainstream approach for preparing uniform core-shell structures. Based on the control mechanism of MOF shell growth, it can be further subdivided into conventional encapsulation and layer-by-layer self-assembly. Conventional encapsulation requires establishing an interfacial buffer layer between the MNP core and MOF shell to facilitate MOF growth around the magnetic core. The surfactant-functionalized core-shell structure (Fe3O4@UiO-66@UiO-67/cationic surfactant hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) reported by Li et al. [43] exemplifies this strategy. This design synergistically integrates the tunable pore size and abundant active sites of UiO-66@UiO-67, the magnetic responsiveness of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, and the electrostatic interactions conferred by CTAB surfactant, achieving a remarkable Cr(VI) adsorption capacity of 932.1 mg·g−1 in aqueous systems along with excellent recyclability. In the layer-by-layer self-assembly strategy, alternating deposition of metal secondary building units and organic linkers mediated by electrostatic interactions via functional groups on the MNP surface enables precise control over the size and morphology of the MOF shell. The modified one-pot method involves mixing pre-synthesized MNP cores with MOF shell precursors (metal ions and organic linkers) in a single reaction system for in situ encapsulation without intermediate separation steps. This strategy offers advantages such as simple processing, low cost, high efficiency, and good yield. The bottle-around-the-ship method involves introducing precursors of magnetic magnetic nanoparticles (NPs) into a pre-synthesized MOF matrix, followed by in situ reduction to form magnetic NPs. This approach utilizes the confinement effect of MOF pores to produce monodisperse, small-sized magnetic nanomaterials.

Each of the aforementioned synthesis strategies presents distinct advantages and limitations. The bottle-around-the-ship approach, particularly layer-by-layer self-assembly, allows precise control over shell thickness and architecture, yielding composites with high structural homogeneity suitable for quantitative detection applications demanding exceptional reproducibility; however, the procedure is relatively time-consuming. The modified one-pot method offers operational simplicity and scalability, yet competitive nucleation between the magnetic core and the MOF shell can result in heterogeneous encapsulation and unintended MOF self-nucleation, adversely affecting batch-to-batch consistency and performance. Lastly, the ship-in-a-bottle strategy facilitates the formation of ultrasmall nanoparticles, but is constrained by limited loading capacity and potential structural degradation of the MOF framework during synthesis, thus restricting its utility in drug screening applications that require high loading densities or exceptional stability.

The preparation of non-core-shell MNPs-MOFs is relatively simpler, primarily achieved through direct mixing and in situ growth strategies. Direct mixing involves homogenizing pre-formed MNPs and MOFs via ultrasonication or thermal polymerization, promoting MNP attachment to the MOF surface through electrostatic interactions or chemical bonding. This approach exhibits broad compatibility with various MOF frameworks, but weak interfacial bonding may lead to particle detachment [44]. The in situ growth strategy disperses bulk MOFs in a solution containing MNP precursors, allowing NPs to nucleate and embed directly on the MOF surface. This method preserves MOF structural integrity and simplifies processing, but MNP embedding may block MOF pores, reducing specific surface area [45].

Non-core-shell architectures offer advantages in facile synthesis. However, their compromised interfacial connectivity and susceptibility to pore obstruction restrict their applicability primarily to sample pretreatment and magnetic separation/enrichment processes that prioritize high adsorption capacity over efficient interfacial charge transfer. Consequently, their utility in constructing highly sensitive electrochemical or fluorescent sensing platforms remains limited.

3.2.2 Structural and property control

The optimization of the structure and function of MNPs-MOFs relies on precise component selection, interfacial engineering regulation, and synthetic parameters. The core challenge lies in achieving synergistic control over magnetic core size/morphology and MOF shell thickness/porosity: MOF pore sizes are typically less than 5 nm, imposing significant size constraints on MNPs. This limitation makes it difficult for larger nanoparticles to nucleate within cavities, while excessive loading of smaller nanoparticles can easily disrupt the framework structure. To mitigate the size limitations imposed by MOF pores on MNPs, the modified one-pot method modulates nucleation kinetics through precise regulation of surfactant type, solvent composition, and reaction temperature, thereby enabling tailored control over core-shell dimensions. The self-templating method directly controls MOF shell thickness through the concentration of metal precursors. Seed-mediated crystallization, as exemplified by Yang et al.'s work [46], first functionalizes Fe3O4 nanoparticles with citric acid, then disperses them in FeCl3/H3BTC solution. Fe3+ and H3BTC adsorb onto the functionalized surface to form Fe3O4@MIL-100(Fe) precursors. Subsequent hydrothermal reaction in FeCl3/H3BTC mixture yields the final core–shell composite. This in situ seed growth allows adjustment of shell size while shortening processing time. Embedding MNPs within the MOF framework may occupy pores, reducing the material's specific surface area. A progressively developed strategy employing magnetic macroporous polymer matrices circumvents this issue by constructing magnetic components within macroporous polymer scaffolds for subsequent MOF growth. This approach avoids pore blockage caused by direct MNP embedding while establishing an interconnected macroporous network that enhances mass transfer via tunnel effects. For example, Chen et al. [47] selected macroporous polyacrylamide as a functional scaffold due to its abundant terminal amide groups serving as covalent anchoring sites for MOF nucleation. Briefly, a mixture of Fe3O4, acrylamide, N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide, and polyvinyl alcohol was used to prepare magnetic macroporous polyacrylamide scaffolds via emulsion templating. Subsequently, in situ growth of MOFs on the polymer surface and within its pore network was achieved through solvothermal treatment. Furthermore, to simplify synthesis and minimize solvent consumption, dry gel conversion strategies (e.g., work by Tan et al. [48]) and solvent-free mechanochemical synthesis (e.g., work by Bellusci et al. [49]) have been developed, with the latter holding great potential for industrial deployment due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and environmental friendliness.

Yolk-shell architectures provide distinct advantages over core-shell systems, including movable magnetic cores and tunable cavity spaces, which contribute to increased active surface area, improved mass transfer efficiency, and the capacity to encapsulate diverse functional molecules within their hollow domains. However, achieving well-defined heterogeneous growth often necessitates complex surfactant-mediated modifications on MNP surfaces. Stepwise assembly strategies allow precise control over shell thickness through iterative coating cycles, thereby minimizing contamination from surfactant residues [50]. To enhance compatibility, pre-functionalization approaches involve coating MNPs with polymers prior to their incorporation into MOF precursor solutions. This functional interlayer promotes directed MOF nucleation on the magnetic supports, improving monodispersity and strengthening interfacial integration.

Owing to the intrinsically low specific surface area and limited surface functionality of bare MNPs, the magnetization intensity and stability of resulting composites are often compromised. To overcome these limitations, organic-inorganic hybrid coatings, such as those incorporating chitosan, can be applied to encapsulate MNPs, improving colloidal stability and dispersion. These coatings isolate the magnetic core from harsh chemical environments and mitigate aggregation caused by inadequate electrostatic repulsion. Furthermore, the introduction of diverse functional groups onto the coating enhances the material's adaptability to various operational conditions. To further enhance the interfacial bonding between MNPs and MOFs, secondary functionalization can be applied to the coating layer, enabling the directed introduction of specific functional moieties for constructing versatile magnetic composite platforms.

3.2.3 Advantages and challenges in drug identification and screening

The fundamental value of MNPs-MOFs lies in their capacity to significantly streamline and automate sample pretreatment in drug analysis, offering notable advantages in operational efficiency, reusability, and functional integration. Their superparamagnetic properties enable rapid magnetic separation, enrichment, and elution of target analytes from complex matrices within minutes, substantially outperforming conventional techniques such as centrifugation and solid-phase extraction, and rendering them highly suitable for HTS and rapid identification. Furthermore, the integration of immobilized enzymes or molecular recognition elements allows these composites to be magnetically recovered and reused over multiple cycles, greatly reducing operational costs. The synergistic coupling of MNPs with MOFs facilitates the design of miniaturized integrated devices capable of simultaneous separation, enrichment, and detection.

Despite these strengths, several challenges impede widespread adoption. Synthesis of MNPs-MOFs, especially core-shell structures, typically involves multi-step processes characterized by high complexity, cost, and limited scalability. A fundamental challenge involves balancing magnetic performance and porosity: thicker MOF shells can attenuate magnetic sensitivity and prolong separation times, whereas thinner shells or high MNP loadings may reduce specific surface area and adsorption capacity. In biological environments, nonspecific protein adsorption and biofilm formation can lead to pore blockage and reduced efficiency, often exacerbated by protein corona effects. Moreover, key mechanistic aspects, such as interfacial drug transport kinetics and potential non-thermal magnetic field effects, remain poorly understood. Future research should focus on developing streamlined, scalable synthesis routes, and employing in situ analytical techniques to elucidate interfacial and dynamic processes, thereby enhancing practical viability, stability, and cost-effectiveness for real-world applications.

3.3 Biomacromolecules-MOFs

Biomacromolecules (such as enzymes, antibodies, and nucleic acid Apts) provide unparalleled high specificity and affinity for drug identification and screening. However, their free forms suffer from inherent limitations including poor stability, susceptibility to inactivation, difficulty in recovery, and high cost, which severely hinder practical applications. MOFs, with their tunable pore structures, abundant surface functional groups, and excellent biocompatibility, offer an ideal platform for the immobilization of biomacromolecules. Biomacromolecule-MOF composites not only significantly enhance the stability of biomacromolecules under harsh operational conditions, facilitating magnetic separation or centrifugal recovery and reuse, but more importantly, their synergistic effects lay the foundation for constructing a new generation of highly specific and stable drug screening sensors and efficient separation platforms [51].

3.3.1 Synthetic strategies

As research on MOFs advanced, the synthesis of structurally diverse MOFs has enabled innovative approaches to immobilize biomacromolecules [52]. As summarized in Fig. 2C [28], preparation methods have evolved from simple physical adsorption to diverse encapsulation strategies.

The surface adsorption method represents a straightforward approach for immobilizing biomacromolecules on MOFs, relying solely on physical interactions between the biomolecules and the MOFs surface. This method imposes no specific requirements on MOFs properties and typically preserves the structure and activity of biomacromolecules. However, it suffers from limitations such as low biomolecule loading capacity and insufficient immobilization stability. In contrast, the pore encapsulation method involves the adsorption of biomacromolecules on the MOFs surface followed by their diffusion into the MOFs pores. This approach offers superior protection for biomacromolecules and effectively against aggregation [53]. For example, Lykourinou et al. [54] immobilized microperoxidase-11 (MP-11) in mesoporous Tb-TATB MOFs, achieving higher loading and catalytic activity versus mesoporous silica, with minimal enzyme leakage. This method is severely limited by the pore size of the MOFs and is typically only suitable for small enzymes or biomolecules, not for larger ones like antibodies.

To address these limitations, the covalent binding method has been developed, which establishes stronger interactions between biomacromolecules and MOFs through covalent linkages. Shih et al. [55] prepared MOFs-immobilized trypsin materials by covalently bonding trypsin to functionalized MOFs such as MIL-88B-NH2(Cr), which contains both carboxyl and amino groups. These materials exhibited superior catalytic activity compared to free enzymes. While covalent bonding overcomes pore size limitations and improves reusability by preventing biomolecule leaching, it presents its own challenges. Surface-immobilized biomacromolecules may exhibit lower stability compared to those encapsulated within MOFs, and the chemical reactions involved in covalent bonding can potentially lead to biomolecule inactivation. These considerations highlight the need for careful optimization of immobilization strategies based on specific application requirements.

The three methods described above all involve incorporating biomacromolecules into pre-formed MOFs. Factors such as the pore size, morphology, and aqueous stability (particularly relevant for MOFs synthesized using organic solvents) inherently of MOFs limit the type and size of biomolecules that can be immobilized and the biological applicability of the resulting composites. Methods for in situ encapsulating biomacromolecules within MOFs under mild conditions circumvent these limitations [56]. For instance, Lyu et al. [57] demonstrated cytochrome c-zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) composite prepared via in situ encapsulation exhibited 10-fold higher enzymatic activity compared to the free enzyme, attributed to protective confinement and uniform distribution. In this approach, the MOFs forms a protective shell around the biomacromolecule, significantly enhancing its stability in harsh external environments. Furthermore, compared to conventional pore encapsulation, encapsulating the biomacromolecule during MOF formation typically ensures more uniform distribution of the biomolecule within the MOFs matrix. Additionally, the in situ encapsulation method is not constrained by pore size limitations and effectively mitigates the issue of guest molecule leaching. The structural diversity and presence of metal-binding sites in biomacromolecules also enable their use as linkers for constructing metal-bio-MOFs. Moreover, certain self-assembling biomacromolecules can directly form the basis of bio-MOFs. Sontz et al. [58] pioneered ternary crystalline structures assembled from metal ions, linkers, and proteins, achieving high loading and biocompatibility at the cost of complex synthesis requirements. However, this method imposes stringent requirements on the MOFs precursors and the specific type of biomacromolecule, making the synthesis of well-defined bio-MOFs a significant challenge.

Each strategy presents distinct advantages and limitations, and the selection must be tailored to the intended application. In situ encapsulation has emerged as the preferred approach for constructing high-performance biosensing and screening platforms, owing to its capability to preserve biomacromolecular activity unrestricted by pore size limitations, particularly advantageous for enzyme immobilization. Covalent binding offers superior performance in applications demanding extreme stability and minimal leaching, such as enzyme microreactors designed for repeated use, though it requires meticulous optimization to retain bioactivity. Surface adsorption, while straightforward, is typically restricted to preliminary studies or contexts with low stability requirements. Bio-MOFs represent a state-of-the-art development with considerable promise, though their widespread practical implementation remains prospective. Future research should prioritize the development of milder and more universal functionalization and synthesis strategies to optimize the balance between bioactivity, structural stability, and loading capacity.

3.3.2 Structural and property control

The performance of biomacromolecule-MOF composites heavily depends on the immobilization strategy and its impact on the structure and activity of the biomacromolecules. Current strategies for biomacromolecule immobilization in MOFs primarily rely on two types of interactions: strong covalent bonds and weak physical interactions (including hydrogen bonding, π-π interactions, and van der Waals forces). The carboxyl, amino, and hydroxyl groups of MOFs can form covalent bonds with complementary functional groups (amino, hydroxyl, thiol, phenolic, and imidazole) on biomacromolecules. By optimizing MOFs synthesis methods and conditions, the density and distribution of these active groups can be precisely controlled, thereby enhancing covalent immobilization efficiency. Nevertheless, strong covalent interactions may alter biomacromolecule conformation and activity.

Weak physical interactions serve as the primary driving force for biomacromolecule immobilization on MOFs surfaces or within their channels and cages. These interactions are significantly influenced by the size and shape of MOFs cages. Specifically, for pore encapsulation methods, the biomacromolecule size must be smaller than the MOFs pore size or pore window aperture. The predominance of microporous MOFs (pore size < 2 nm) in current research has limited the range of biomacromolecules suitable for encapsulation. The development of mesoporous and hierarchical porous MOFs represents a significant breakthrough, enabling the immobilization of larger biomolecules. MOFs typically exhibit two main pore architectures: cage-like and channel-type structures. Cage-like MOFs feature pores larger than their entry points, making them ideal for biomacromolecule encapsulation. For example, Feng et al. [59] designed a series of ultra-large mesoporous cage-like MOFs based on trivalent metals, and successfully encapsulated three enzymes using the cage-like MOFs as single-molecule traps for enzyme encapsulation. The immobilized enzymes demonstrated reduced aggregation and denaturation, with lower Michaelis constant (Km) and comparable catalytic efficiency to their free counterparts. Similarly, channel-type MOFs, characterized by uniform pore sizes throughout their interconnected channels, offer another promising platform. Li et al. [60] developed MOFs with hierarchical channels, where larger channels accommodated enzyme immobilization while smaller channels facilitated substrate and coenzyme diffusion. This design maximized the free space for enzyme and coenzyme interaction post-immobilization.

The in situ synthesis process often relies on strong interactions (e.g., coordination, electrostatic) between MOF precursors and biomacromolecules [61]. Among these, the surface charge of the biomacromolecule (isoelectric point, zeta potential) has been proven to be the most critical factor controlling MOF nucleation, growth, encapsulation efficiency, and ultimately bioactivity [62]. For example, negatively charged enzymes more readily attract metal ions, inducing rapid biomimetic mineralization and achieving efficient encapsulation with high activity retention [63]. Conversely, positively charged enzymes may undergo a slower encapsulation process and face an increased risk of denaturation due to prolonged exposure to ligand monomers [64]. Additionally, the inherent surface hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity of the MOFs themselves significantly influences the activity and stability of the encapsulated or surface-immobilized enzymes. Liang et al. [65] demonstrated that the surface hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity of MOFs influences the activity and stability of enzyme. Hydrophilic MOFs such as MAF-7 and ZIF-90 maintain enzyme activity and stability in harsh conditions, including organic solvents, proteases, denaturants, and high temperatures. In contrast, hydrophobic MOFs like ZIF-8 tend to inactivate encapsulated enzymes.

3.3.3 Advantages and challenges in drug identification and screening

Biomacromolecule-MOFs are engineered to preserve and enhance the native functionality of biorecognition elements such as enzymes, antibodies, and Apts. These hybrid systems offer exceptional specificity and sensitivity by capitalizing on the inherent molecular recognition capabilities of biomacromolecules, enabling highly specific capture and detection of target drug molecules directly within complex matrices, making them particularly suitable for screening bioactive compounds from natural product extracts. Furthermore, the MOF matrix provides effective physical protection for encapsulated biomacromolecules, conferring resistance to harsh pH, temperature, and organic solvents and allowing magnetic recovery and reuse over dozens of cycles, which substantially reduces operational costs. Their versatile functional integration capability enables coupling with multiple detection modalities such as fluorescence, electrochemical, and colorimetric sensing, facilitating the development of miniaturized platforms that combine recognition, separation, and signal transduction for high-throughput automated screening.

Nevertheless, several challenges impede broader application, including the risk of conformational changes or active-site occlusion during immobilization that may compromise bioactivity, mass transfer limitations due to restricted diffusion in microporous or thick-shell MOF structures, interference from proteases and other biomolecules in real samples such as serum that can cause nonspecific adsorption and pore blocking, as well as the high cost and complex fabrication processes associated with these functional materials. Future efforts should prioritize developing mild and universal immobilization strategies, designing MOFs with hierarchical porosity to facilitate mass transfer, and implementing surface engineering approaches such as antifouling coatings to enhance stability and reliability in complex biological environments.

3.4 COFs-MOFs

COFs are a class of crystalline porous polymers formed from light elements connected via strong covalent bonds, renowned for their exceptional structural regularity, high specific surface area, outstanding chemical stability, and rich designability [66]. The strategic integration of COFs with MOFs aims to synergistically combine the stability and tunable functionality of COFs with the metallic active sites and catalytic diversity of MOFs, thereby creating advanced composite materials whose performance far exceeds that of individual components. In drug identification and screening, COFs-MOFs offer a novel material platform for highly sensitive and selective detection of trace drugs in complex biological matrices through synergistically enhanced adsorption, sieving, catalysis, and signal transduction capabilities.

3.4.1 Synthetic strategies

The synthesis of COFs-MOFs can be categorized into three mainstream strategies based on the sequence of component formation (Fig. 2D), with the choice depending on the stability of the core material and the interfacial and structural requirements of the target application. The first approach, termed as “COFs-on-MOFs”, is most widely used. It involves introducing pre-synthesized MOFs into the COFs reaction mixture. Typically, MOFs are functionalized with amino groups that undergo Schiff base reactions with aldehyde monomers to form C=N imine covalent bonds. This strategy effectively preserves the MOF structure while the COF shell provides additional functionality and stability. For example, Zheng et al. [67] prepared a core-shell UiO@TapbTp composite by growing a TAPB-Tp COF on amino-functionalized UiO-66, which exhibited excellent adsorption and energy transfer properties. The second method, “MOFs-on-COFs”, reverses the synthesis sequence, utilizing pre-synthesized COFs as nuclei or substrates for MOFs growth. It is particularly suitable when MOF precursors cannot withstand the acidic conditions required for COF synthesis, effectively avoiding the risk of MOF core decomposition. For example, Liu et al. [68] first synthesized TPN-COFs and then grew Co-MOFs in situ on their surface to construct Co-MOFs@TPN-COFs composites, which combine the high specific surface area of COFs with the electrochemical activity of MOFs. The third method, “MOFs + COFs”, involves independently synthesizing MOF and COF nanocrystals followed by their integration via self-assembly in solution or further covalent cross-linking. Although this approach offers less structural precision compared to the previous two approaches, it is more flexible and straightforward, providing a rapid route for constructing multifunctional composites.

The “COFs-on-MOFs” strategy enables the construction of well-defined core–shell structures with relatively tight interfacial bonding, facilitating electron transfer and mass transport, and is particularly advantageous for building high-performance sensing interfaces, despite involving multiple synthesis steps. The “MOFs-on-COFs” strategy leverages the chemical stability of COFs but faces challenges in controlling the uniformity of MOF nucleation and growth on COF surfaces, potentially leading to insufficient interfacial contact. The “MOFs + COFs” physical mixing method is the simplest but suffers from weak interfacial interactions, making the interface a performance weak point prone to separation in complex environments. It is more suitable for adsorption and enrichment scenarios with less demanding interface requirements rather than highly sensitive sensing. Therefore, for drug sensing applications requiring precise interfaces and efficient signal transduction, “COFs-on-MOFs” represents a more promising strategy.

3.4.2 Structural and property control

The practical performance of COFs-MOFs heavily relies on precise control over their microstructure (e.g., core-shell, yolk-shell) and interfacial properties. The construction of core-shell structures often require stepwise modification strategies. For example, Lu and co-workers [69] employed a polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)/branched polyethyleneimine (BPEI) dual-functional modification strategy, where PVP promoted dispersion and BPEI drove uniform COF nucleation, achieving precise control over shell thickness, which is crucial for regulating mass transfer pathways and sensing performance. To obtain superior architectures, researchers have designed yolk-shell structures through acid etching, phase transition, and contraction treatments, creating cavity spaces that significantly enhance active surface area and mass transfer efficiency [70]. The pore environment is key to drug recognition selectivity. Maspoch and co-workers [71] utilized a spray-drying combined phase transition strategy to generate abundant micropores and mesopores in situ at the interface of UiO-66-NH2@COF-TAPB-BTCA composites, whose porosity significantly exceeded the sum of the parent MOF and COF, providing an ideal platform for efficient drug molecule enrichment and sieving.

Regarding functional expansion and performance enhancement, advances in synthesis methods have enabled the introduction of diverse functionalities. For example, Zhang et al. [72] constructed a UiO-66-NH2@COF electrochemical aptasensor that leveraged the synergy between MOF-provided active sites and COF-optimized electron transfer kinetics, achieving ultrasensitive detection of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and chloramphenicol (CAP) with LOD at the fg/mL level. Furthermore, to broaden the applicability of COF-MOF hybrid materials in electrochemical biosensing platforms, Liu et al. [68] engineered Co-MOF@TPN-COF composites. By leveraging the synergistic interaction between the nitrogen-enriched framework of triazine rings and the electroactive sites of the Co-MOF, an ultra-low LOD of 0.217 fg/mL was achieved. Lin et al. [16] engineered a Fe3O4@NH2-MIL-125@TpPa-SO3H magnetic composite demonstrating exceptional adsorption capacity (>60 mg/g) for quinolone antibiotics. Its core-shell design not only enables rapid magnetic separation but also substantially enhances selectivity via multi-interaction mechanisms (including electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and π-π stacking), outperforming conventional adsorbents.

3.4.3 Advantages and challenges in drug identification and screening

The integration of COFs and MOFs transcends simple physical mixing, offering synergistic enhancements through rational structural design and multifunctional performance unattainable by individual components. These composites exhibit superior structural stability, wherein the inherent chemical and mechanical robustness of COFs substantially reinforces the material's resistance to degradation in demanding aqueous and biological environments, thereby mitigating MOF collapse and extending functional durability. Furthermore, the well-defined and tunable porosity of COFs acts synergistically with MOF channels to enhance molecular selectivity and enrichment capacity through dual sieving and specific binding, enabling efficient drug capture while excluding interferents. The complementary nature of COF and MOF building blocks also allows precise modulation of photoelectric and catalytic properties, such as electronic band structure, charge transport, and active sites, facilitating the design of highly sensitive sensors across fluorescence, colorimetric, and electrochemical modalities. Moreover, the composite retains the adsorption and catalytic advantages of MOFs while incorporating additional functionalities from COFs, significantly broadening applicability in diverse drug screening contexts.

Despite these advantages, COFs-MOFs remain at an early developmental stage and face considerable challenges, including complex and costly synthesis due to multi-step processes, expensive ligands, and stringent conditions that impede scalable production. Mechanistic understanding is also limited, particularly regarding heterointerface interactions, electronic structures, and molecular transport pathways, constraining rational design. Additionally, biological validation remains insufficient, with most studies conducted under idealized conditions rather than in realistic environments such as serum or tissue fluids, leaving critical gaps in the assessment of stability, anti-biofouling capacity, and practical selectivity. Moving forward, efforts should focus on developing efficient and scalable synthesis strategies, coupled with advanced in situ characterization and computational modeling, to elucidate interfacial mechanisms and accelerate translation toward practical biomedical applications.

3.5 Other nanomaterial-MOFs

Beyond conventional integrations involving CDs, MNPs, biomacromolecules, and COFs, the incorporation of various emerging nanomaterials with MOFs has furnished a diverse and robust toolkit to address persistent challenges in drug identification and screening. By harnessing the complementary properties of individual components, these composite systems are designed to overcome inherent limitations in sensitivity, specificity, stability, and functional integration encountered in conventional detection platforms. This strategic convergence of materials opens new avenues for the precise and reliable analysis of trace-level drugs within complex biological environments.

3.5.1 Representative composite materials

This section outlines several representative “other nanomaterial-MOF” composites, emphasizing their unique design concepts, enhancement mechanisms, and application value in pharmaceutical analysis. Graphene and its derivatives (e.g., graphene oxide (GO)), known for their excellent electrical conductivity and large specific surface area, are ideal for improving the intrinsic conductivity of MOFs. By combining the robust structure of zirconium-based MOFs (Zr-MOFs) with the high conductivity of reduced GO (rGO), researchers developed a novel rGO/Zr-MOF-based electrochemical sensor that maximizes electron transfer kinetics and enhances the adsorption capacity for gemcitabine (GEM), achieving an ultra-low detection limit of 0.008 μM [73]. MXenes (e.g., Ti3C2Tx), which possess metal-like conductivity and hydrophilic surfaces, significantly enhance electrochemical sensing performance when composited with MOFs [74]. The core advantage of these materials lies in their substantially improved electrochemical response speed and sensitivity. A major challenge, however, is achieving uniform and stable integration between the carbon materials and MOFs, preventing restacking of graphene sheets or oxidation of MXene, and precisely controlling the interface properties to optimize electron transfer efficiency.

MIPs provide “artificial antibody”-like recognition cavities for target compounds. Combining the selective recognition capability of MIPs with the exceptional porosity of MOFs can significantly improve detection sensitivity and selectivity. For instance, Liu et al. [75] synthesized a composite of magnetic MOFs and MIPs (M-UIO-66@MIPs) that synergistically integrates the functional pores of MOFs, specific recognition sites of MIPs, and the superparamagnetism of iron oxide nanoparticles, enabling efficient and selective identification and removal of five target antibiotics from aqueous solution. This strategy perfectly marries the high selectivity of MIPs with the high surface area and designability of MOFs, achieving a synergistic “1 + 1 > 2” effect. The challenge lies in matching the polymerization process of MIPs with the stability of MOFs, avoiding pore blockage, and ensuring the accessibility and uniformity of the imprinted cavities.

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), with their outstanding conductivity and surface plasmon resonance effects, can be used to construct ultra-low impedance electrode interfaces through synergistic coordination with functionalized MOFs. This amplifies the signal in electrochemical aptasensing for sensitive detection of antibiotic residues [76]. AuNPs can greatly enhance the electrocatalytic activity and optical signal intensity of the composite. Key challenges include controlling the size and distribution of AuNPs and preventing their detachment from the MOF surface to ensure signal reproducibility and stability. Their high cost also somewhat limits large-scale application.

Additionally, composites of hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) and MOFs can form yolk-shell or heterojunction structures, leveraging flexible H-bond networks and rigid MOF pores to synergistically enhance molecular recognition and biocompatibility [77]. Quantum dots (QDs) provide highly bright and stable fluorescence signals, and their combination with MOFs enables the construction of high-performance fluorescence sensors [78]. CNTs, with their excellent one-dimensional electron conduction channels, can be used to build conductive MOF composites [79].

3.5.2 Advantages and challenges in drug identification and screening

The integration of diverse nanomaterials, such as HOFs, graphene, MIPs, AuNPs, MXenes, and QDs, with MOFs offers a modular and customizable design strategy that addresses specific limitations in drug analysis through tailored functional enhancements. By integrating complementary functional nanomaterials, these composites facilitate targeted enhancement of specific properties. For example, graphene or AuNPs enhance electrical conductivity. MIPs provide highly specific artificial recognition sites mimicking antibodies, and HOFs improve biocompatibility. Moreover, these composites exhibit emergent synergistic mechanisms, such as interfacial electronic effects, plasmonic resonance energy transfer, and multi-recognition cooperation, that transcend mere functional addition, thereby enabling ultra-sensitive and selective detection. This versatile strategy significantly broadens the applicability of MOF-based materials across diverse analytical scenarios, including high-sensitivity electrochemical sensing, selective target enrichment from complex biological samples, and multimodal integrated detection.

Nevertheless, several critical challenges remain. These include inadequate control over interfacial uniformity and stability, wherein poorly defined or metastable interfaces may cause performance decay or material dissociation under physiological conditions. Synthesis methodologies often lack universality and scalability due to reliance on harsh conditions and multi-step processes, impeding reproducible mass production. Furthermore, mechanistic understanding of micro-level interactions, such as electronic coupling, energy transfer pathways, and the origin of synergism, remains insufficient, limiting rational design and further innovation.

4 Application of MOF composites in drug identification and screening

Building upon the multifunctional capabilities of MOF composites outlined in Section 3, this section provides a systematic evaluation and comparison of their performance in practical pharmaceutical analysis applications. As underscored previously, MOFs possess high specific surface area, tunable porosity, and easily functionalizable interfaces, making them ideal substrates for developing advanced analytical platforms. This segment focuses specifically on how these intrinsic material properties are harnessed to overcome analytical challenges. The discussion is structured into two main categories: drug identification and drug screening.

4.1 Drug identification

This section aims to elaborate on the application of MOF composites in constructing high-performance sensing platforms for the highly sensitive and selective detection and quantification of specific target drugs. The following will sequentially discuss four major sensing modalities based on fluorescence, electrochemistry, colorimetry, and SERS, analyzing and comparing the performance advantages and limitations of different MOF composites within each mode.

4.2 Fluorescence

Fluorescence sensing technology consistently faces key challenges in drug detection: poor photostability of traditional fluorescent probes, susceptibility to interference from complex biological matrices, and limited specific recognition capability for trace target drugs. MOF composites, with their precisely designable pore structures, ultrahigh specific surface areas, and abundant modification sites, provide a new-generation platform to systematically address these issues. Their core advantages lie in the fact that MOFs can not only effectively protect fluorophores (e.g., CDs, metal nanoclusters) from photobleaching and quenching but also pre-concentrate target drug molecules through size exclusion and affinity adsorption, significantly increasing local concentration and thereby amplifying the fluorescence signal. More importantly, leveraging energy transfer or dual-emission designs, MOF composites can construct ratiometric fluorescence sensing systems that effectively eliminate environmental fluctuations through self-calibration mechanisms, greatly enhancing detection accuracy and reliability [80].

Among various fluorescent MOF composites, CDs-MOFs have been the most extensively studied. The combination of CDs and MOFs further enhances fluorescence sensing performance. CDs offer high photostability, low toxicity, and ease of functionalization, while MOFs provide high specific surface area, ordered pores, and binding sites, offering an ideal dispersion and immobilization carrier for CDs. The two components synergistically enhance the fluorescence response and sensing stability. Jalili et al. [81] utilized the confinement effect of ZIF-8 to co-encapsulate green CDs and AuNCs, constructing a ratiometric probe. Cephalexin (CFX) specifically quenched the emission of AuNCs at 630 nm, while the signal of gCDs at 520 nm served as a stable internal reference (Fig. 3A) [81]. Based on the ratiometric signal F520/F630, highly sensitive detection of CFX in milk was achieved (LOD: 0.04 ng/mL, 0.12 nM), effectively overcoming the problem of single signals being vulnerable to environmental interference (Fig. 3B) [81]. To further improve recognition efficiency for specific drugs, researchers have also designed sensing mechanisms utilizing specific interactions between the metal sites or organic ligands of MOFs and the target molecules. For instance, a study leveraged the coordination between Cu2+ in a Cu-MOF and carboxyl groups on the CD surface, and the stronger coordination with the pesticide thiophanate-methyl (TM), to construct a “dissociation-luminescence” probe [82]. TM caused the disintegration of the MOF structure and the release of CDs, leading to changes in the fluorescence ratio at 430 nm and 600 nm (LOD: 3.67 nM), achieving visual detection of TM in food. Strategies such as heteroatom doping and chiral design are widely applied in CDs-MOFs systems to further enhance selectivity and performance. In 2024, the combination of nitrogen-sulfur co-doped CDs with Tb-MOF achieved specific detection of fluoroquinolones (e.g., LOD for norfloxacin: 36 nM) through enhanced energy transfer, and was successfully integrated with a paper-based platform to advance field detection [87]. The same year, Wang et al. [84] co-encapsulated chiral CDs and magnetic Fe3O4 within hierarchical porous ZIF-8, constructing a chiral magnetic composite that achieved differential recognition of naproxen enantiomers (LOD for S-naproxen: 50 nM; LOD for R-naproxen: 0.30 μM), demonstrating the potential of MOF composites in complex chiral drug analysis (Fig. 3C) [84].

The combination of MIPs with MOFs further strengthens recognition specificity. Zhang et al. [88] constructed an MIP sensor with ZIF-8 loaded with nitrogen-doped CDs. Through spatial matching and chemical interactions within the imprinted cavities for TC, highly selective detection of TC in milk and eggs was achieved (LOD: 0.045 μg/mL, 101.25 nM).

The introduction of COFs brings more ordered π-structures and high stability to MOF composites, further expanding the performance boundaries of fluorescence sensing. In 2023, Quan et al. The ternary composite system CDs@Cu-MOF/TMTA-COF developed by Hui et al. Based on these dual signal changes, ratiometric detection of Gly in environmental samples was achieved (LOD: 70 nM), combined with deep learning for intelligent analysis of fluorescence images, marking a step towards automation and intelligence for such sensors (Figs. 3H and I) [86].

Fluorescence sensing platforms utilizing MOF composites have enabled ultrasensitive detection of various drugs, particularly antibiotic and pesticide small molecules. Their performance originates from the synergistic enrichment and protective effects of MOFs, combined with integrated fluorescence modulation mechanisms. While these systems have demonstrated practical utility in analyzing simple matrices, such as in food safety and environmental monitoring, their application in highly complex biological fluids (e.g., serum and tissue fluid) remains challenging due to limitations in selectivity, anti-fouling capacity, and long-term stability. Future efforts should focus on developing MOF carriers with enhanced biocompatibility and stability (e.g., Zr- or Fe-based MOFs), elucidating the interactions between composites and biological interfaces, and fostering integration with microfluidics, portable imagers, and AI algorithms to enable in vivo drug monitoring and clinical precision diagnostics.

4.3 Electrochemical

Electrochemical sensing technology, while offering advantages such as operational simplicity and low cost in drug detection, has long faced challenges including interference from complex sample matrices, weak response signals from trace targets, electrode passivation, and insufficient reliability of single-signal output. MOF composites, with their high specific surface area, tunable pore structures, and abundant active sites, provide a novel pathway to systematically address these issues. Their core value lies in the ability of MOFs to efficiently enrich target drug molecules, significantly increasing local concentration and thereby amplifying the electrochemical response; furthermore, by compounding with other functional materials, they can effectively overcome the limitation of poor intrinsic conductivity, synergistically enhancing electron transfer rates and catalytic activity, and comprehensively improving detection sensitivity, selectivity, and stability [89].

To address the vulnerability of single-signal output to interference in complex samples, constructing dual-mode or multi-mode sensing platforms has become an important trend. Li et al. [90] developed an electrochemical-fluorescence dual-mode sensor for detecting kanamycin (KANA) in milk by constructing a composite interface of CDs@Cu/Al-MOF and Ti3C2. This design utilized the fluorescence stability of CDs@MOF and the conductivity of Ti3C2. Through the competitive binding of KANA and cDNA to the Apt, it synchronously triggered a decrease in electrochemical signal and an enhancement in fluorescence signal, achieving ultra-sensitive detection in both modes (electrochemical LOD: 0.52 fM; fluorescence LOD: 0.44 fM), effectively improving the reliability and accuracy of the results.

Enzyme-MOFs demonstrate significant advantages in enhancing the stability of biosensing interfaces. By synthesizing a series of isostructural three-dimensional chiral Ni-MOFs with tunable pore sizes, an acetylcholinesterase (AChE) electrochemical biosensor was successfully constructed for detecting the chiral drug inhibitor galantamine hydrobromide (GH). The study showed that when the MOF's pore size and chiral environment better matched the target, the host-guest interaction between the substrate and the sensing interface could be significantly enhanced. Based on this strategy, the optimized sensor platform (AChE/L-Ni-BPY/DpAu) exhibited extremely high sensitivity for GH, with a detection limit as low as 0.31 pM and a linear detection range of 1 × 10−6 to 1 μM. Further microcalorimetry experiments clarified the critical influence of pore size and chiral microenvironment on sensor sensitivity. Quantitative analysis of the apparent energy of host-guest interactions provided a solid experimental and thermodynamic basis for understanding the structure-activity relationships in MOF-based electrochemical biosensors (Figs. 4A and B) [91]. Xiao et al. The combination of COFs with MOFs further enriches electrode interface design strategies. Zhang et al. [72] prepared a core-shell MOF@COF composite via covalent connection, fully utilizing the metal affinity of UiO-66-NH2 and the π-π stacking effect of the COF (TAPB-DMTP-COF) to achieve high-density Apt immobilization, constructing an ultra-sensitive electrochemical aptasensor for CAP (LOD: 6.45 fg/mL, 0.22 fM) (Figs. 4C and D) [72]. Li et al. [93] designed a ternary Au@UiO-66-NO2@COF composite. Through the synergistic effects of conductivity enhancement from AuNPs, directional immobilization by the MOF, and enrichment by the COF, they achieved ultra-sensitive detection of trace oxytetracycline (OTC) in milk (LOD: 1.13 fM), showcasing the advantage of multi-component synergy (Fig. 4E) [93].

The composite of metal nanoparticles with MOFs is an effective strategy for improving interfacial electron transfer. Chansi et al. [95] significantly enhanced conductivity by modifying the MOF-5 electrode surface with AuNPs and constructed an electrochemical immunosensor for pesticide residue detection using specific recognition by polyclonal antibodies, achieving rapid quantification of targets in plant samples. The ratiometric sensor developed by Yang et al. [96], based on sulfur nanoparticles (SNPs)@MOF and boron nanosheets (BNSs)-ferrocene (Fc), used an internal Fc reference signal to achieve highly reliable detection of doxorubicin (ADR) (LOD: 2 nM) and real-time monitoring of drug release, effectively eliminating interference from environmental fluctuations.

The combination of MIPs with MOFs provides a new strategy for constructing highly selective sensing interfaces. The introduction of highly conductive materials like graphene can significantly enhance the conductivity of MOF composites and prevent graphene self-aggregation. Building molecularly imprinted sensors based on such composites is expected to improve the sensitivity of electrochemical sensors. Fu et al. [94] prepared a MOFs@graphene composite that fully utilized the high conductivity of graphene and the high specific surface area of MOFs, achieving highly sensitive detection of ketamine through dual signal amplification. They further constructed a molecularly imprinted membrane (MIM) on a MOFs@G-modified screen-printed electrode via ultraviolet (UV)-induced polymerization of methacrylic acid and ethylene glycol dimethacrylate, significantly improving the sensor's sensitivity, selectivity, and stability. The detection linear range reached 1.0 × 10−4–40 μM, with LOD at 40 pM, recoveries of 98.0%–116.0%, and RSD < 3.1% (Fig. 4F) [94]. Sensitivity is a property constantly pursued by researchers developing electrochemical sensors, and dual functional monomers can greatly enhance the selectivity of MIPs. Based on this, in 2023, Duan et al. [97] used dopamine and o-phenylenediamine (o-PD) as dual functional monomers to form a MIP film via electropolymerization on the surface of an aminated iron-based MOF and graphene composite modified electrode, constructing a molecularly imprinted electrochemical sensor (MIECS) for trace taurine (Tau) detection. This sensor utilized the porous structure of the MOF to increase the effective surface area and recognition sites, while the dual-monomer strategy further improved selectivity. It achieved a linear response in the range of 1.0 × 10−8–1.0 × 10−2 μM, an LOD as low as 3.2 fM, and was successfully applied to the quantitative analysis of Tau in human serum samples with recoveries of 97.3%–113%.

By incorporating functional components such as conductive nanomaterials, biological recognition elements, COFs, and MIPs, MOF-based electrochemical sensors have successfully overcome the limitation of insufficient intrinsic conductivity and achieved a leap in detection performance. Currently, such sensors can achieve nanomolar or even femtomolar detection of various drug molecules and demonstrate good applicability in real sample analysis. However, for future practical applications, the following challenges remain: (1) Further improving the anti-fouling ability and long-term stability of composites in complex biological matrices; (2) Developing greener, more economical, and scalable composite electrode preparation processes; (3) Promoting deep integration with microfluidics, wearable devices, and artificial intelligence algorithms to achieve real-time, continuous dynamic monitoring.

4.3.1 Colorimetric

Although traditional colorimetric sensing technology offers advantages such as simple operation, low cost, and visual readout, it faces significant challenges in drug detection applications: relatively low sensitivity, susceptibility to background interference from colored components in complex sample matrices, and difficulty in achieving high specificity for structurally similar analogs. MOF composites provide innovative solutions to overcome these bottlenecks through their tunable enzyme-mimetic activity, high specific surface area, and ease of functionalization. Their core advantages lie in the fact that MOFs can not only serve as highly efficient nanozyme catalysts to significantly amplify colorimetric signals but also act as excellent carriers to enrich targets via pore confinement effects, increasing local reaction concentrations. More importantly, through rational composite design, multimodal or ratiometric sensing platforms can be constructed, utilizing built-in references to effectively eliminate environmental interference and greatly enhance detection accuracy and reliability [98].

To improve the accuracy of detection results in complex samples, developing multi-mode output sensing strategies has become an important research direction. To address the challenge of single-signal vulnerability to interference in on-site Gly detection, Luo et al. [99] constructed a multifunctional nanosensor by in situ anchoring nitrogen-doped CDs onto zirconium-based Fc MOF nanosheets. This material exhibited excellent peroxidase (POD)-like activity, which could be inhibited by GLP via blocking effect, leading to simultaneous triple changes in absorbance, fluorescence, and temperature signals. This enabled multi-mode cross-validated detection of GLP (colorimetric LOD: 0.0131 μg/mL, 77.48 nM), significantly improving the reliability of on-site detection (Figs. 5A–C) [99]. Similarly, in ratiometric sensing, a study in 2025 reported a ratiometric fluorescent nanoprobe based on CDs@LZIF-8-Eu3+ [100]. By one-pot modification of fluorescent CDs and Eu3+ onto LZIF-8, and leveraging the IFE of TCs on the CDs and the antenna effect on Eu3+, a visible blue-to-red color transition was achieved within 10 s, with detection limits as low as 2.94–4.46 ng/mL (7 nM). This platform was successfully used to distinguish multiple TC antibiotics in food and environmental samples. Liu et al. [101] constructed a dual-mode sensor by combining the POD-like catalytic properties of FeNi-MOF with the fluorescence of orange carbon dots (OCDs). Enzyme-MOF composites cleverly combine the high specificity of biological enzymes with the stability of MOFs. Encapsulating AChE in ZIF-8 not only significantly improved the enzyme's environmental stability but also promoted substrate mass transfer, making the AChE@ZIF-8-based sensor ten times more sensitive to organophosphorus pesticides (OPs) than the free enzyme [104]. Chai et al. [102] further constructed a confined catalytic system by integrating nanozymes and biological enzymes: they co-encapsulated two-dimensional V-TCPP(Fe) MOF nanozyme with ultrahigh enzyme-like activity and AChE within ZIF-8 (Fig. 5D) [102]. Leveraging nanoscale proximity effects, the colorimetric and chemiluminescence signals were amplified by 31 and 36 times, respectively, enabling highly sensitive dual-mode visual detection of OPs. This system was successfully integrated into a gel sensor combined with a smartphone for on-site analysis, representing a significant step toward practical application of such sensors (Figs. 5E and F) [102].

To further address the issue of specific recognition in drug detection, the combination of molecular imprinting (MIP) technology with MOFs has shown great potential. One study prepared a MIL-101(Fe)-NH2@MIP composite by in situ polymerization of dopamine on the surface of MIL-101(Fe)-NH2, skillfully combining the enzyme-like catalytic activity of MOFs with the specific recognition ability of MIPs (Fig. 5G) [103]. This enabled dual-mode detection of CAP (colorimetric LOD: 93.38 nM) and the development of a companion smartphone-based visual detection device, providing an effective tool for rapid on-site detection of CAP in fresh milk samples (Figs. 5H and I) [103]. Similarly, another research team constructed a colorimetric-fluorescence sensor based on FeCu-MOF@MIP. Through the hindering effect of TC molecularly imprinted channels on the TMB color reaction and the inner filter effect (IFE) on fluorescence, they achieved highly selective (colorimetric LOD: 21.61 nM; fluorescence LOD: 12.64 nM) and portable detection of TC, demonstrating the superiority of this strategy for accurate analysis in complex matrices [105].

Furthermore, MOFs also demonstrate unique potential in multi-component drug detection. In 2023, researchers developed a colorimetric sensor based on a prussian blue (PB)/Fe-MOF/Ce nanocomposite core-shell structure (PB/Fe-MOF/Ce NP) [106]. With multiple capture sites and synergistic catalytic activity, this material was successfully used for the direct detection of OPs in fetal bovine serum (LOD: 4.8 μg/L, 25 nM), showcasing the application prospects of MOF composites in the direct analysis of complex biological samples.

Colorimetric sensing technology based on MOF composites has successfully pushed the detection limits of various drugs (especially antibiotics, pesticides, etc.) to the nanogram or even picogram level. Its core advantages stem from the synergistic catalytic enhancement, specific recognition, and integration of multiple signal output modes provided by MOFs. However, when facing more complex clinical samples (e.g., whole blood, urine), their resistance to matrix interference and long-term stability requires further enhancement. Future research should focus on developing more specific recognition elements (e.g., nucleic acid Apt, biomimetic antibodies (BAs), optimizing the integration process of composites with portable devices (e.g., paper-based chips, smartphone readout devices), and promoting their innovative applications in vivo monitoring and precision medicine.

4.3.2 SERS

SERS is a powerful fingerprint spectroscopic technique capable of rapid, ultra-sensitive detection at nearly the single-molecule level, providing direct structural information of analytes without sample destruction. However, its practical application in drug identification still faces three core challenges: the complex and poorly reproducible preparation of high-performance enhancement substrates; severe interference from matrix components in complex biological samples; and a lack of specific recognition capability for trace target molecules. MOF composites, with their highly tunable pore structures, enormous specific surface areas, and rich surface chemistry, offer a new-generation platform to systematically address these issues. Their unique value lies in the fact that MOFs can not only efficiently enrich target drug molecules through size exclusion and affinity interactions, significantly increasing their local concentration at the laser spot, but also serve as excellent carriers or templates for the precise assembly of noble metal nanostructures, enabling the controllable construction of electromagnetic hot spots. More importantly, through functionalization, MOFs can be endowed with specific recognition capabilities, thereby achieving highly selective and sensitive SERS detection of target drugs in complex matrices [107].

Combining MOFs with COFs or magnetic components can further optimize the performance of SERS substrates. For instance, a recent study constructed a magnetic hierarchical porous material (Mag MOF@COF-Apt) via a surface synthesis strategy for the selective recognition of 17β-estradiol (E2) (Figs. 6A and B) [108]. This material first assembled an Fe-MOF on a magnetic core, then grew a TABB-DMTP-COF shell in situ to form a heterojunction, and finally immobilized amine-modified E2 Apts onto the COF layer via glutaraldehyde cross-linking. This design not only enabled rapid separation through magnetism but also suppressed matrix interference via the size exclusion effect of the COF, while the shell-isolation effect enhanced SERS activity. Ultimately, highly sensitive detection of E2 was achieved with a detection limit of 110.14 nM. To address the challenge of separating and detecting trace drugs in biological samples, combining MOFs with magnetic components has become an efficient strategy. Feng et al. [109] developed a method combining magnetic solid-phase extraction with SERS based on Fe3O4@SiO2@MIL-101(Fe). This material cleverly integrated the high enrichment capacity of MIL-101(Fe) with the rapid separation capability of the magnetic core, successfully applying it to therapeutic drug monitoring of carbamazepine (CBZ) and clozapine (CLO) in human serum, achieving detection limits of 0.072 μg/mL (0.305 μM), and 0.12 μg/mL (0.367 μM), respectively, and spiked recoveries of 94.0%–105.0%. Combining MOFs with metals (e.g., Au, Ag) is a classical strategy for obtaining SERS substrates with high enhancement factors. Chen et al. [112] constructed a MIL-101(Cr)@Ag composite by anchoring AgNPs onto the surface of MIL-101(Cr) via in situ synthesis, leveraging both the enrichment capability of MOFs and the enhancement effect of AgNPs to achieve ultrasensitive detection of chlorpromazine hydrochloride and aminophylline in human serum (LODs reached 8.91 × 10−2 μg/mL (250 nM), and 3.4 × 10−2 μg/mL (81 nM), respectively). Another study constructed a core-shell Au@MIL-100(Fe) structure, utilizing the MIL-100(Fe) shell to simultaneously stabilize the AuNPs and enrich the target flunixin meglumine (Flu), achieving a detection limit of 0.15 mg/kg (303 nM) in pork and pig liver samples [113]. These studies show that MOF-noble metal composites can significantly improve the stability and practicality of SERS detection, but the cost of noble metals and their potential biotoxicity remain factors to consider. Mesoporous materials often exhibit high conductivity and chemical stability, and their combination with chiral MOFs (HMOFs) offers a new pathway for enantioselective drug recognition. A study combined mesoporous gold films with HMOFs to construct hierarchically porous hybrid films, achieving ultrasensitive recognition of pseudoephedrine enantiomers in undiluted blood plasma with a detection limit as low as 10−12 M. This platform fully utilized the physical enhancement from mesoporous gold and the chemical enhancement and chiral recognition capability of HMOFs, demonstrating excellent anti-interference performance in complex biological matrices (Figs. 6C and D) [110].

To improve detection specificity, various functionalization strategies have been developed for SERS substrates. Zhao et al. [114] electrochemically synthesized bimetallic CoNi–ZIF nanostructures and integrated them with AgNPs to fabricate a three-dimensional hybrid substrate. This platform achieved an exceptionally low detection limit of 0.01 nM for TC, while demonstrating high reproducibility with recovery rates ranging from 94.45% to 114.25% in complex matrices such as environmental water and milk samples. Zhi et al. [111] utilized MXene Ti3C2Tx nanosheets loaded with Ti-based MOF and an imidacloprid (IMI)-specific peptide chain to construct a bifunctional nanoprobe (TN@Ti-MOFs/PT), developing a SERS/resonance Rayleigh scattering (RRS) dual-mode detection platform (Fig. 6E) [111]. TN@Ti-MOFs/PT not only possessed the catalytic function of a nanozyme for a novel nanogold indicator reaction but also had the specific molecular recognition capability for IMI. This dual-mode biosensing platform enabled the detection of trace IMI with a linear range of 0.01–0.25 ng/mL (39–980 pM); the detection limits measured by SERS and RRS were 0.0011 ng·/mL (4.3 pM) and 0.0039 ng·/mL (15.25 pM), respectively (Figs. 6F and G) [111].

The introduction of MIP and BA technologies has further enhanced the specificity of SERS sensors. Xiu et al. [115] assembled HKUST-1(Cu) onto a paper-based substrate via electrodeposition and combined it with BAs and dendritic silver structures to construct a flexible SERS sensing strip, achieving highly sensitive (LOD: 1.39 × 10−4 mg/kg, 0.96 nM) and rapid on-site detection of ethephon in food. The MIP-enhanced SERS aptasensor developed by Zhang et al. [116], leveraging the multi-level enhancement effect of MOFs@Au/SiO2@AuAg nanocones, achieved extremely high detection sensitivity for CAP (LOD: 0.76 pM), showcasing the unique advantages of combining MIP with SERS for complex sample detection. SERS sensing technology based on MOF composites has successfully pushed the detection limits of various drugs to the picomolar or even femtomolar level. Its core advantages stem from the synergistic enrichment effect of MOFs, the ordered assembly of metal nanostructures, and the integration of specific recognition elements. However, for complex application scenarios such as clinical therapeutic drug monitoring, the following challenges remian: (1) Improving the reproducibility and quantitative accuracy of SERS signals; (2) Reducing the preparation cost and improving batch consistency of composites; (3) Enhancing the anti-fouling capability of composites in complex biological matrices. Future research should focus on developing multi-mode integrated sensing platforms, incorporating machine learning algorithms for spectral analysis, and promoting deep integration with microfluidic technology, ultimately enabling their widespread application in point-of-care testing (POCT) and personalized medicine.

4.4 Drug screening

This section focuses on the application of MOF composites in drug screening, the efficient discovery and enrichment of lead compounds with specific bioactivities (e.g., enzyme inhibitory activity) from complex mixtures such as natural product extracts. This differs from the recognition of individual compounds discussed in the previous subsection.

4.4.1 LF

LF is a technique based on the specific affinity between ligands and target molecules. By immobilizing target proteins or enzymes onto solid supports, it enables efficient capture, screening, and enrichment of active ligands from complex mixtures (e.g., natural extracts). MOF composites, with their designable hierarchical pore structures and rich surface chemistry, provide an ideal platform for efficient and stable immobilization of targets, significantly enhancing the efficiency of LF technology.

To address the challenges of low screening efficiency for active ingredients in natural product extracts and the easy deactivation of immobilized enzymes, Chen et al. [128] utilized the high porosity and surface amino groups of UiO-66-NH2 to achieve high loading (98.31 mg/g) and high activity recovery (104.4%) of porcine pancreatic lipase (PPL). Applied to screening extracts of Prunella vulgaris, it successfully captured 13 lipase ligands, with 12 compounds identified via high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time of flight-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS) and confirmed as potential inhibitors through activity validation and molecular docking. Magnetic MOF composite-based LF strategies are highly favored for their operational convenience. Xu et al. [129] constructed a Fe3O4-COOH@UiO-66-NH2@PPL magnetic composite, achieving high PPL loading (247.8 mg/g) and activity recovery (101.5%). Using an external magnetic field, rapid separation of the immobilized enzyme was achieved, completely avoiding centrifugation and significantly improving processing throughput. Eight inhibitors were successfully identified from Scutellaria baicalensis extracts (Figs. 7A−C) [129]. Common enzyme immobilization methods such as encapsulation and pore adsorption often lead to unfavorable conformational changes and reduced activity during fixation. To enhance the activity and stability of immobilized enzymes, surface modifications and novel immobilization strategies have been widely adopted. For example, in 2024, Hu et al. [132] introduced sodium cholate as a stabilizing agent for lipase immobilized within ZIF-8, markedly enhancing enzymatic activity (182% relative activity retained) and operational stability, while achieving an immobilization efficiency of 92.89%. The resulting composite demonstrated high efficacy in screening hydrophobic constituents, leading to the identification of nine potential lipase inhibitors. That same year, Chen et al. developed an in situ screening strategy based on dual recognition of Ni/his-tag and SIRT1/ligand, enabling direct capture of His-tagged SIRT1 from cell lysates without protein purification or immobilization steps (Fig. 7D) [130]. The adsorption capacity for His-tagged SIRT1 reached 154.04 mg/g, with a relative enzyme activity recovery of 93.16%. Using this dual-recognition screening method, 16 natural SIRT1 inhibitors were successfully identified from two herbal extracts, offering a new approach for efficient discovery of natural active molecules (Figs. 7E and F) [130]. In 2025, Wang et al. [133] developed a novel strategy for synthesizing enzyme-MOF composites by co-encapsulating CD-modified enzymes and Fe3O4 nanoparticles within ZIF-8. The CDs on the enzyme surface competed with imidazole for coordination with Zn2+, promoting the formation of a mesoporous structure and mitigating mass transfer limitations. This surface modification also optimized interfacial interactions, accelerating composite assembly. The resulting material, THR@CDs/Fe3O4@ZIF-8, exhibited superior affinity, enzymatic activity, and stability compared to its unmodified counterpart. Under harsh conditions, the composite retained 81.76% of its initial activity and maintained 66.0% after ten reuse cycles, demonstrating strong potential for applications in enzyme inhibitor screening.

The relatively small size of MOFs makes them difficult to separate easily from enzymatic reaction media, somewhat limiting their application in immobilized target enzyme screening technologies. MOF composite membranes and paper-based platforms have also expanded the forms of LF applications. In 2024, a study induced ZIF-8 nucleation and growth on a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane via a polydopamine intermediate layer, enabling in situ encapsulation of α-glucosidase (α-Glu) [134]. The strong adhesive capability of PDA, forming robust non-covalent and covalent bonds, facilitated the nucleation and growth of a well-intergrown ZIF-8 membrane. Polydopamine on the membrane served as an anchor, covalently linking Zn2+ ions, followed by chelated Zn2+ and the organic ligand 2-methylimidazole self-assembling on the membrane surface to form a three-dimensional porous ZIF-8 nanoparticle framework. Simultaneously, α-Glu was encapsulated in situ within the ZIF-8 structure for enzyme immobilization. The immobilized enzyme demonstrated good reusability, retaining 79.3% of its initial activity after 10 cycles, and successfully screened 13 inhibitors from traditional Chinese medicines. MOF composite membranes offer stable spatial structures and good operational stability, making them more suitable for continuous-flow operations, though their preparation process is relatively complex. The same year, Chen et al. [131] constructed a visual test strip platform integrated with α-Glu/glucose oxidase (GOx)/horseradish peroxidase (HRP)@Fe-MIL-88B-NH2 nanoreactors (Fig. 7G) [131]. Through a cascade reaction involving α-Glu hydrolysis of maltose to produce glucose, GOx oxidation generating H2O2, and Fe-MIL-88B-NH2 catalyzing TMB color development, highly efficient α-Glu inhibitors were visually screened from oak leaf extracts, leading to the discovery of a class of acylated flavonoid glycosides with anti-diabetic potential (Figs. 7H and I) [131].

From the above studies, it is evident that different immobilization strategies each have their own advantages and disadvantages: magnetic composites facilitate separation and are suitable for high-throughput operations; in situ screening strategies simplify the process; while membrane reactors demonstrate potential for continuous operation. Future development directions include designing core-shell magnetic MOFs with both high magnetic responsiveness and stability, creating integrated MOF membrane reactors compatible with microfluidic technology, and expanding their screening applications to more complex physiological environments such as membrane proteins and cell lysates.

4.4.2 IMERs

IMERs, which physically or chemically immobilize enzymes onto carrier materials, combine enzymatic catalysis with operational stability and have become important tools in drug screening [135]. Compared to traditional free enzyme systems, IMERs offer advantages such as reusability, enhanced environmental tolerance, and ease of integration with separation and detection technologies. The core challenge in IMER technology lies in simultaneously achieving high enzyme activity retention, long-term stability, and seamless integration with downstream detection methods. MOFs, with their highly ordered pores, large specific surface area, and tunable chemical environments, have emerged as ideal carriers for constructing high-performance IMERs [136].

IMERs can be classified into offline and online operation modes, each with distinct application scenarios. Offline IMERs are simple to operate, with enzymatic reactions and separation/detection performed in separate steps. In 2019, Zhong et al. [137] innovatively proposed a colorimetric sensing strategy based on a bifunctional hybrid enzyme-MOF reactor for the sensitive screening of α-Glu inhibitors (Fig. 8A) [137]. The team self-assembled α-Glu, GOx, Cu2+, and 4,4′-bipyridine into a flower-like spherical hybrid enzyme–MOF reactor, achieving sensitive screening of α-glucosidase inhibitors with an approximately 4.58-fold increase in enzyme activity compared to the free enzyme system (Fig. 8B) [137]. In 2021, Wan et al. [138] prepared a core–shell magnetic composite, Fe3O4@CS@ZIF-8, via layer-by-layer assembly and encapsulated α-glucosidase in situ within the ZIF-8 lattice for offline enzyme reaction–capillary electrophoresis screening. The immobilized enzyme exhibited enhanced temperature and pH stability, retaining 74% of its initial activity after 10 reuse cycles, and successfully screened α-glucosidase inhibitors from 14 traditional Chinese medicines. Manganese dioxide (MnO2), known for its high specific surface area, strong adsorption capacity, good permeability, short diffusion paths, high stability, excellent activity, low toxicity, and natural abundance, can further enhance the properties of MOF composites when combined with them, providing a superior carrier for enzyme immobilization. In 2023, Liu et al. [139] immobilized AChE on an MnO2–ZIF-67 composite via electrostatic adsorption. The immobilized enzyme retained 78.2% of its relative activity after five reuse cycles, and its Michaelis constant (Km) was 3.46 mM, significantly lower than that of free AChE (10.57 mM), indicating stronger substrate affinity. Leveraging this performance advantage, the composite successfully screened 11 compound inhibitors from Inula macrophylla. That same year, Wang et al. [140] prepared ultra-thin two-dimensional Cu-CAT nanosheets, which exposed more edge active sites, significantly enhancing enzyme-like activity and substrate affinity. The Km was 5.73 mM, and the maximum reaction rate (vmax) reached 3.072 × 10−9 M/s. Online IMERs, on the other hand, integrate enzymatic reaction, termination, and separation into a single continuous workflow, offering higher throughput, accuracy, and automation. In 2022, Fu and co-workers [141] pioneered the use of MOFs as both the capillary electrochromatography (CEC) stationary phase and enzyme immobilization carrier, constructing an integrated online enzyme analysis system based on UiO-66-NH2 IMERs-CEC (Fig. 8E) [141]. This microreactor maintained 80.5% activity after 10 days of continuous operation, far exceeding that of the free enzyme (42.9%), and was successfully applied to detect OPs pesticides in various leguminous vegetables (Fig. 8F) [141]. In 2024, Li et al. [142] used a cysteine-triggered in situ growth method to successfully immobilize lipase within a ZIF-8 capillary. The measured lipase Km was 2.75 mM, lower than the kinetic constant determined in offline reactions, indicating higher affinity of the immobilized enzyme. This IMER screened lipase inhibitors within 3 min, identifying 7 active compounds from 10 natural products, demonstrating the high efficiency of the online mode.

MOF-based IMERs not only streamline operational procedures but also significantly improve separation efficiency and detection reliability, greatly accelerating the process of enzyme inhibitor screening. The offline mode offers flexibility, while the online mode provides higher automation and throughput. In the future, through the development of multi-channel and multi-mode detection strategies, IMERs are expected to achieve multi-dimensional activity information output, further enhance anti-interference capabilities, and construct intelligent drug screening platforms that are rapid, low-cost, highly specific, and highly sensitive.

The success of a drug screening platform hinges on its efficiency, throughput, and reliability. In summary, the selection of an appropriate MOF-based platform for drug screening applications is guided by specific operational requirements and performance objectives. MNPs-MOFs emerge as the optimal platform for the rapid extraction and identification of bioactive molecules from complex crude extracts, leveraging their exceptional magnetic separation and enrichment capabilities. Online IMERs are distinctly suited to scenarios demanding ultra-high throughput and full process automation, offering superior reproducibility and continuous operation. Enzyme-MOFs membranes represent a specialized solution for conducting demanding reactions under continuous-flow conditions or within integrated catalytic and separation systems.

5 Conclusions and perspectives

This review has systematically summarized recent research advances in multifunctional MOF composites for drug identification and screening. Through the synergistic integration of CDs, MNPs, biomacromolecules, COFs, and other materials with MOFs, these composites not only amplify the inherent advantages of MOFs, such as high porosity, structural tunability, and surface modifiability, but also generate synergistic “1 + 1 > 2” effects, resulting in new properties unattainable by individual components (e.g., unique optical responsiveness, magnetic responsiveness, electrochemical activity, and enhanced bioaffinity). Leveraging multiple mechanisms (size/shape sieving, host-guest interactions, signal transduction amplification), these composites enable highly sensitive detection, highly selective recognition, and rapid response towards target drugs, establishing them as a leading frontier in pharmaceutical analysis.

Despite substantial progress, the practical translation of multifunctional MOF composites faces core challenges:(1) Insufficient structural stability limits practical applications. The inherent chemical/hydrothermal instability of MOFs, particularly their susceptibility to degradation under harsh detection environments (e.g., physiological aqueous systems, strong acidic/alkaline media, electrochemical cycling processes), limits their application in scenarios requiring reusability or long-term operation. Future efforts should focus on developing novel high-stability MOF building blocks (e.g., high-connectivity nodes, hydrophobic MOFs, bi/multimetallic MOFs), enhancing framework stability through metal-node synergy or ligand engineering, and optimizing composite strategies such as core-shell structuring and encapsulation techniques to improve overall robustness in complex environments while maintaining excellent recognition performance and biocompatibility.

(2) The structure-property relationships remain unclear. A profound understanding is lacking regarding the precise spatial distribution of components within the composites, their interfacial structures, and how these elements synergistically influence the final performance metrics, such as recognition site density, mass transfer efficiency, and signal generation/transduction efficiency. This often leads to empirical approaches in material screening and optimization. There is an urgent need to strengthen the role of computational simulations (e.g., molecular dynamics, high-throughput virtual screening) and artificial intelligence (e.g., machine learning for predicting structure–performance relationships), combined with advanced in situ and multiscale characterization techniques, to dynamically analyze the adsorption, diffusion, recognition, and binding processes of drug molecules within composites and promote rational design and performance-oriented optimization.

(3) Interfacial interactions between components are weak. Current composite fabrication often relies on weak physical interactions (e.g., van der Waals forces, π-π stacking, particularly in emerging composites like COFs-MOFs), hindering the formation of strong chemical bonding (e.g., covalent bonds, coordination bonds) and long-range ordered crystallization between heterogeneous materials. These fragile interfaces readily lead to structural disintegration or performance degradation during application. To address this, precise interface engineering strategies, such as directed covalent grafting (e.g., click chemistry), coordination anchoring, or surface-initiated polymerization, should be developed. These approaches enable the construction of robust, chemically linked interfaces between MOFs and functional components. This enhances the overall structural stability of the composite, improves interfacial charge/mass transport efficiency, and boosts long-term performance.

(4) Behavior assessment under complex biological environments is insufficient. Current research predominantly focuses on detection performance under standardized in vitro conditions, critically lacking systematic evaluation of material behavior, particularly interfacial phenomena such as protein corona formation, non-specific adsorption, biodegradation, cytotoxicity, biodistribution, and metabolism, within realistic, complex biological milieus (e.g., serum, extracellular matrix, living tissues). This significant gap hinders the translation of these composites towards in vivo diagnostics or therapeutic monitoring applications. Addressing this limitation necessitates a systematic approach to in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility and performance evaluation. This includes developing biomimetic interface modification strategies (such as phospholipid bilayer coating, PEGylation, or cell membrane-mimetic coatings); utilizing advanced biological models like organoids and organ-on-a-chip systems to simulate human microenvironments for physiologically relevant evaluation; and integrating in vivo imaging techniques (e.g., fluorescence imaging, positron emission tomography) with radioisotope tracing. These combined approaches enable real-time, dynamic analysis of composite biodistribution, retention, clearance kinetics, and interactions with target sites within living organisms.

(5) The distinguishing ability in scenarios involving multiple drugs (polypharmacy) is limited. Current systems are predominantly optimized for single-drug analysis, often exhibiting insufficient selectivity for distinguishing structurally similar drugs (e.g., different β-lactam antibiotics). When multiple drugs are present concurrently, overlapping and interference within their generated optical or electrochemical signal channels frequently occur, hindering their ability to meet the demands of clinical therapeutic monitoring for polypharmacy. To overcome this limitation, several strategies can be pursued: (i) Designing composite structures featuring spatially separated recognition sites, such as multi-channel MOFs arrays or multifunctional zone-modified MOF composites; (ii) Developing multiplexed signal decoding strategies that integrate sensor array technology with machine learning algorithms (e.g., pattern recognition, deep learning) to efficiently resolve and differentiate multidimensional response signals; (iii) Integrating microfluidic concentration gradient generators with multiplexed detection modules to enable high-throughput, parallelized multi-drug analysis.

(6) Scale-up manufacturing and cost-effectiveness present significant challenges. The transition of laboratory achievements to industrial scale faces significant bottlenecks, including complex synthesis process control (multi-step reactions, harsh conditions), batch-to-batch consistency issues, high costs, and environmental sustainability concerns (solvent consumption, waste generation). Critically, core performance metrics, such as high loading efficiency, stability, and specificity, must be maintained throughout this scale-up process. To address these challenges, the development of green and continuous production processes (e.g., continuous flow reactors, mechanochemical synthesis) is essential. Integrating precision control technologies like microfluidics enables high-accuracy regulation of synthesis parameters, ensuring material uniformity and batch consistency. Concurrently, costs can be reduced through process optimization and novel material development. Crucially, key application performance metrics must be concurrently optimized and validated during the scale-up manufacturing process itself.

Research on multifunctional MOF composites for drug recognition and screening is burgeoning with immense potential. Future endeavors must not only deepen the focus on overcoming the core challenges outlined above (material stability, structure-activity relationships, interface engineering, biological behavior, multi-drug detection, and scalable manufacturing) but also prioritize the deep integration of cross-disciplinary technologies and the expansion of practical application scenarios:(1) Deep integration of emerging detection and information technologies could be pursued. High-performance MOF composites should be tightly coupled with portable fluorescence/colorimetric detectors, smartphone-based sensing platforms, paper-based microfluidic devices, and portable electrochemical workstations. This integration will enable the construction of intelligent recognition systems characterized by high sensitivity, specificity, portability, user-friendliness, and automation.

(2) The advancement of practical application scenarios towards implementation could be prioritized. Efforts should focus on enhancing the practicality and reliability of materials and devices in real-world settings, particularly in POCT, bedside monitoring, HTS, and personalized medicine.

In conclusion, multifunctional MOF composites represent a vibrant research frontier. Through sustained innovation in materials (enhancing stability and precision interface control), revolutionary design strategies (computation- and AI-driven rational construction), advanced manufacturing processes (green, continuous, low-cost scale-up production), and deep technological convergence (integration with intelligent detection platforms), this field is poised to overcome existing bottlenecks. Such progress will accelerate the transition of these materials from laboratory research to practical applications addressing clinical and societal needs, ultimately supporting advancements in drug discovery and precision medicine through upgraded recognition and screening technologies.
