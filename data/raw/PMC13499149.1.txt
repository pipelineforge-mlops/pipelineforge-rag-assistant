Introduction and aims

Interpreting condylar osseous changes on CBCT is challenging for general practitioners. This study evaluated the ‘zero-shot’ diagnostic performance and utility of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as AI assistants for detecting condylar abnormalities.

Methods

We analysed 72 CBCT images from the EHPN study for internal validation and constructed a balanced sample of 70 images from the MMDental dataset for external validation, with dual-radiologist consensus as the ground truth. Three frontier VLMs (Gemini-3, GPT-5.2, and Qwen3-VL) were used to evaluate representative sagittal slices without prior fine-tuning. Performance was measured by diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and balanced accuracy, while the Quality Assessment of Medical AI-generated Information (QAMAI) framework assessed the quality of AI-generated structured reports. This study was reported following STARD-AI and CLAIM guidelines as primary checklists, with TRIPOD-LLM as a supplementary framework.

Results

Gemini-3 achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy (90.3%; 95% CI: 81.0%-95.5%), outperforming GPT-5.2 (75.0%) and Qwen3-VL (55.6%). External validation on a constructed balanced sample from the MMDental dataset confirmed these findings, with Gemini-3 achieving 90.00% accuracy. Regarding report generation, Gemini-3 surpassed the other models across five QAMAI dimensions, providing more accurate, clear, and clinically useful justifications aligned with diagnostic standards.

Conclusion

VLMs, particularly Gemini-3, exhibit zero-shot capabilities in identifying condylar changes and generating high-quality diagnostic reports. While these models maintain a conservative diagnostic tendency, they demonstrate potential as preliminary, training-free screening aids in oral maxillofacial radiology. Further multicentre validation is warranted to establish clinical utility.

Clinical Relevance

Cloud-based VLMs could offer accessible screening assistance by allowing general practitioners to query condylar findings via simple web interfaces without specialized hardware. These tools may contribute to structured reporting workflows, though their diagnostic consistency in routine practice remains to be established, and they should complement rather than replace expert clinical judgment.

Introduction

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has emerged as a cornerstone of maxillofacial imaging, providing high-resolution, three-dimensional diagnostic data essential for modern dentistry.1,2 In the context of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), CBCT excels in detecting condylar osseous changes, such as erosion, flattening, and osteophyte formation,3, 4, 5 thereby enabling clinicians to mitigate progressive risks and tailor individualized treatment plans.

Despite the increasing prevalence of CBCT scanners in dental clinics across the United States, interpreting the nuances of the condylar osseous status remains challenging for those without specialized radiologic training. This diagnostic gap can lead to clinical pitfalls, including misdiagnosis or a lack of diagnostic confidence. Given that TMJ osteoarthritis and its related pathologies are relatively common and require timely intervention,6 accessible tools that empower general practitioners to accurately identify these signs and ensure high-quality patient care are needed.

To address this problem, some machine learning (ML) algorithms, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been developed to automate the detection of condylar abnormalities.7,8 Although these models have demonstrated acceptable accuracy, they are often hindered by significant limitations, such as labour-intensive labelling by human experts, substantial computational power, namely, graphical processing units (GPUs), and complex software installations.9,10 These factors pose financial and technical barriers, particularly for private clinics and practitioners in resource-limited regions.

The recent evolution of vision-language models (VLMs), such as Gemini-3, represents a potentially important development. Unlike traditional ML models that rely on task-specific training, VLMs exhibit ‘zero-shot’ or ‘few-shot’ learning capabilities, allowing them to interpret pathological features without prior exposure to a labelled dataset.11,12 Furthermore, as cloud-based platforms, these models eliminate the need for expensive local hardware, potentially lowering access barriers for advanced diagnostic assistance via simple web interfaces.13

Beyond simple classifications, VLMs possess the unique ability to generate nuanced textual interpretations. Although conventional artificial intelligence (AI) often operates as a ‘black box’, providing only a binary diagnosis, VLMs can produce descriptive reports that assist practitioners in decision-making and facilitate patient communication by translating complex radiographic findings into understandable reports.14

Recent studies have highlighted the expanding role of artificial intelligence in oral and maxillofacial radiology. The landscape of dentomaxillofacial imaging is shifting from traditional convolutional neural networks toward sophisticated, multimodal, and predictive AI frameworks. Building upon established research in CBCT-based diagnostics and clinical decision support,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 recent advancements in predictive mandibular modelling and multimodal large language models have underscored the transformative potential of foundation models. These milestones establish a useful framework for investigating the application of zero-shot Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as candidate automated assistants for the assessment of condylar osseous changes in CBCT imaging.

This study aimed not to replace practitioners but to evaluate the potential of VLMs as candidate decision-support tools under controlled conditions. We focused on two core objectives: assessing whether VLMs can assist in the diagnostic detection of condylar osseous changes on CBCT and evaluating their capacity to produce high-quality diagnostic reports.

Methods

The use of open datasets is a safe and ethical method for testing VLMs. We utilized a specialized TMJ osteoarthritis (OA) dataset from the University of Michigan’s Ensemble via Hierarchical Predictions through Nested cross-validation (EHPN) study, which was originally approved by the University of Michigan IRB (Approval No. HUM00113199) with informed consent obtained from all participants. This publicly available dataset includes high-resolution cone-beam computed tomography (hr-CBCT) images, providing superior visualization of condylar morphology.20 The EHPN dataset was acquired using a 3D Accuitomo 170 scanner (J. Morita, Kyoto, Japan) with the following parameters: 90 kVp, 5 mA, 30.8-second scan time, and a field of view of 40 × 40 mm, yielding an isotropic voxel size of 0.08 mm. This high resolution enables detailed visualization of subtle cortical changes critical for early osteoarthritis detection.

A total of 72 patients were identified in the ‘Baseline.zip’ file downloaded from Deep Blue Data at the University of Michigan in November 2025 and included in this study sample. Following a rigorous quality control process, no patients were excluded, yielding a final study population of 72. This study sample features a spectrum of common radiographic signs of condylar osseous changes, including flattening, sclerosis, erosion, subchondral cysts, and osteophytes.

In clinical practice, practitioners typically identify the most representative pathological slices from a 3D CBCT volume to establish a diagnostic impression. To simulate this real-world scenario, two senior oral and maxillofacial radiologists (each with >10 years of clinical imaging interpretation experience) performed independent blinded reviews of the full 3D volume (using 3D Slicer v5.10.0). The interobserver agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.89), indicating excellent initial agreement. Discordant cases were subsequently resolved through consensus discussion to establish the final ground truth. Rather than replacing the clinician, the VLMs were evaluated as diagnostic assistants, tasked with interpreting the selected slices and generating clinical reports, a workflow designed to test their utility in streamlining routine diagnostic tasks and assisting general practitioners.

All CBCT images in this study were screened at Beijing Stomatological Hospital in 2025 based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure quality and consistency of this study.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) CBCT scans encompassing the complete temporomandibular joint (TMJ) region; (2) availability of sagittal reformatted images allowing for standardized slice selection; and (3) clear visibility of the condylar head and articular eminence.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Presence of significant motion or metal streak artifacts (eg, from orthodontic appliances) that obscured the condylar bony cortex; (2) incomplete field of view (FOV) where the entire condyle was not captured; and (3) a documented history of condylar fractures or previous TMJ surgery.

A single representative sagittal slice was extracted from each of the 72 cases (32 pathological and 40 controls). In cases of unilateral pathology, the affected side was selected; for bilateral involvement, the side with the most prominent osseous change was selected. This ‘one patient, one slice’ protocol was strictly enforced not only to simulate the clinical scenario but also to ensure statistical independence and eliminate potential clustering bias. The sagittal plane was selected as the primary view for evaluation, as it represents the classic perspective for TMJ assessment, providing critical information on anteroposterior bone contour and joint space – key features for identifying osteoarthritis (OA). This single-slice analysis aligns with the routine viewing habits of general practitioners using panoramic radiographs, facilitating rapid clinical screening. We acknowledge that this approach may introduce selection bias and cannot fully replace the comprehensiveness of 3D volumetric analysis. The current study is positioned to validate the zero-shot efficacy of VLMs as ‘rapid auxiliary screening tools’ rather than systematic radiographic diagnosis. Future studies will explore multiview fusion and 3D input models. To provide a standardized baseline for the VLMs’ zero-shot performance, all images were exported and clipped to 330 × 330 images, showing the entire structure of the condyle. Diagnostic criteria for condylar osseous changes

The definition of ‘condylar osseous changes’ in this study strictly adhered to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD).21 The primary inclusion criteria comprised: cortical bone erosion (irregular cortical surface with focal loss of cortical continuity), condylar flattening (loss of normal convexity), osteophyte formation (marginal bony outgrowths), and subchondral sclerosis (increased bone density beneath the articular surface). These criteria represent the most commonly observed radiographic manifestations of degenerative joint disease affecting the TMJ.

Model selection and experimental design

We evaluated three state-of-the-art VLMs: Gemini-3, GPT-5.2, and Qwen3-VL (Qwen3-VL-235b-a22b-thinking), selected based on their frontier capabilities and wide public availability at the time of study.

All models were accessed through the OpenRouter platform (https://openrouter.ai) using Python-based custom pipelines during January 3 to 15, 2026. For reproducibility, we report the following details for each model. Gemini-3 (model ID: google/gemini-3-pro-preview), GPT-5.2 (model ID: openai/gpt-5.2), and Qwen3-VL (model ID: qwen/qwen3-vl-235b-a22b-thinking) were each accessed via their respective OpenRouter API endpoints. Inference settings were held constant across all models: temperature was set to 0 to ensure deterministic outputs, and no other sampling parameters (eg, top-p, top-k) were adjusted from their API defaults. Each image was processed once per model; repeated runs were not performed because the deterministic temperature setting (temperature = 0) eliminates stochastic variation, making output stability testing redundant under these conditions. The prompt used for all evaluations (provided in full in Supplementary Material 1) was finalized prior to any model testing and remained unchanged throughout the study; no prompt refinement occurred after viewing any model results. We note that exact implementation details – such as internal preprocessing pipelines and proprietary vision encoder architectures – are unavailable due to the closed-source nature of these commercial models, which represents a limitation inherent to evaluating frontier VLMs through third-party API platforms. All images were manually verified to contain no personal or identifying information before uploading, stored on local hardware, and no data were missing in this study. The complete prompts are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Task 1: diagnostic classification and interpretation

This task evaluated the ability of the model to assist dentists in detecting osseous changes. For internal validation, each model was presented with 72 radiographic slices from the EHPN dataset individually. A structured prompting strategy was used to simulate the clinical reasoning of a senior radiologist (Supplementary Material 1).

To evaluate cross-dataset consistency of our diagnostic findings, we incorporated the MMDental dataset,22 which was originally approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Lishui University (Approval No. 2022YR014), as a constructed balanced external validation set (n = 70: 35 abnormal, 35 normal). For the external validation (MMDental dataset), images were acquired using HiRes 3D-Plus/Boen scanners with age- and sex-adjusted protocols (90-100 kVp, 3-9 mA).

We acknowledge that the external validation set represents a constructed balanced sample rather than a consecutive clinical cohort. From the available MMDental CBCT scans, we first screened cases for adequate TMJ/condylar field of view, excluding scans where the condylar region was incompletely captured or image quality was insufficient for reliable assessment. Given that this dataset lacked pre-existing TMJ pathology annotations, we implemented a rigorous annotation protocol: two senior oral and maxillofacial radiologists (the same evaluators as the internal validation) performed independent blinded reviews following DC/TMD criteria. Interobserver agreement was assessed (κ = 0.85), and discordant cases were resolved through consensus discussion. Random sampling was employed to ensure balanced sample distribution; once a predetermined sample size (n = 35) was reached for either category, additional scans of that category were excluded from selection. This approach ensured an objective validation set composition. A total of 106 scans were reviewed for eligibility, of which 70 cases meeting the selection criteria were included. The excluded 36 scans consisted of cases with truncated condylar anatomy (n = 8), significant image artifacts (n = 3), and normal (control) cases exceeding the target quota for balanced sampling (n = 25), consistent with a greater number of normal cases in the source dataset.

Task 2: standardized report generation

The models generated radiographic reports based on the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) guidelines for the 32 patients with confirmed osseous changes from the internal validation set (EHPN).21 Critically, this report-generation evaluation was conducted exclusively on these positive/abnormal cases; the quality of reports for normal cases was not assessed. Therefore, the QAMAI scores reported herein reflect the linguistic and structural quality and potential clinical usefulness of AI-generated reports for known abnormal cases only and should not be interpreted as evidence of report quality for normal condylar findings. This scope of assessment represents an important study limitation. We assigned models to adopt the role as senior experts in oral and maxillofacial radiology. Two experienced radiologists independently assessed the quality of the generated reports using the Quality Assessment of Medical AI-Generated Information (QAMAI) framework (1-5 scale).23

The QAMAI framework was originally developed and validated for assessing AI-generated medical information quality across multiple dimensions. While it was not specifically designed for dental radiology reporting, its five core dimensions (Accuracy, Clarity, Relevance, Completeness, and Usefulness) are broadly applicable to evaluating structured medical reports. It is important to note that these QAMAI dimensions evaluate three related but distinct outcomes that should not be conflated: (1) diagnostic correctness – the factual accuracy of the report content, which overlaps with but is not equivalent to the binary diagnostic classification assessed in Task 1; (2) linguistic and structural quality of the generated text – captured by the Clarity and Completeness dimensions, reflecting how well the report is organized and articulated; and (3) potential clinical usefulness – captured by the Relevance and Usefulness dimensions, reflecting whether the report would be actionable in a clinical setting. These three outcomes are related but separate, and no single QAMAI dimension should be treated as equivalent to another or to the diagnostic accuracy metrics from Task 1.

To minimize bias, the evaluators were asked to rate using a specific Python Gradio rating interface to keep them blinded to the model identities.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the three VLMs in Task 1, we calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and overall accuracy, using radiologist consensus as the gold standard. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for these proportions were estimated using the Wilson score method.24

To compare the diagnostic accuracy of the three models, the McNemar test was used for pairwise comparisons.25 This method is specifically designed for paired-sample designs where the same test set is evaluated by different models, analysing discordant pairs (ie, cases where Model A was correct while Model B was incorrect, denoted as n₀₁ and n₁₀) to determine the statistical significance of performance differences.

Given that the VLMs were instructed to provide binary diagnostic conclusions (‘yes/no’ for the presence of condylar osseous changes) rather than continuous probability scores, a conventional threshold-based ROC analysis was not feasible. Instead, we calculated balanced accuracy, defined as the average of sensitivity and specificity: Balanced accuracy = (Sensitivity + Specificity) / 2. This metric represents the model’s overall discriminative ability at its single operating point. The 95% confidence intervals for balanced accuracy were estimated using a nonparametric bootstrap percentile method (2000 resamples; random seed = 42). For pairwise model comparisons, we applied Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate, setting the adjusted significance threshold at P < .0167 (0.05/3).

For Task 2, the internal consistency between the two independent evaluators was first assessed using weighted Cohen’s kappa to ensure the reliability of the ordinal QAMAI scores. To compare the performance differences among the three models (Gemini, GPT, and Qwen), the Friedman test was employed across five QAMAI dimensions: Accuracy, Clarity, Relevance, Completeness, and Usefulness. This nonparametric approach was selected to account for the ordinal nature of the data and the repeated-measures design.

For dimensions demonstrating significant differences (P < .05), posthoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction to control for Type I error, setting the adjusted significance threshold (α) at 0.0167 (0.05/3). Furthermore, effect sizes (r) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of the differences, with |r| > 0.5 interpreted as a large effect. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (v3.11), and a P-value < .05 (precorrected) was considered statistically significant.

To ensure methodological rigor and prevent data leakage, the following measures were implemented: (1) Prompt development was finalized before any model evaluation commenced; no cases from either the internal or external validation datasets informed prompt tuning. (2) The external validation set (MMDental) was entirely isolated from all prompt and model-development decisions; it was accessed and annotated only after the experimental protocol was locked. (3) Patient-level independence was ensured through the ‘one patient, one slice’ protocol, preventing any clustering effects. (4) All three VLMs were evaluated using identical prompts and procedures to ensure fair comparison. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Furthermore, the study design and manuscript preparation followed STARD-AI (Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy – Artificial Intelligence) as the primary reporting checklist for AI-based diagnostic accuracy studies,26 complemented by CLAIM (Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging) for medical imaging AI-specific considerations.27 TRIPOD-LLM guidelines were retained as a supplementary framework given the large language model components of this study.28 The completed STARD-AI, CLAIM, and TRIPOD-LLM checklists are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study evaluated whether frontier VLMs can assist in identifying condylar osseous changes from expert-selected CBCT sagittal slices under controlled conditions. Our findings demonstrate that VLMs, particularly Gemini-3, achieved promising zero-shot diagnostic performance without task-specific fine-tuning. However, it is essential to interpret these results within the context of the simplified study design, which evaluates single expert-selected slices rather than true volumetric CBCT interpretation.

The superior performance of Gemini-3 over GPT-5.2 and Qwen3-VL in terms of both diagnostic accuracy and reporting quality is noteworthy. Although all three were top-tier VLMs, Gemini-3’s higher sensitivity (84.4%) indicated a greater ability to detect subtle osseous changes. This may reflect differences in multimodal image processing capabilities across frontier VLMs, as suggested by comparative evaluations in radiology,29 enabling a finer analysis of radiographic contrast, a critical factor in bone pathology, where the distinction between healthy cortical bone and early sclerosis is subtle. In task 2, Gemini-3’s reports achieved the highest QAMAI scores, particularly in ‘Clarity’ and ‘Accuracy’. The structured prompt-based textual justifications generated by the model reflect its capacity to follow DC/TMD diagnostic reasoning patterns, though such prompted outputs should not be equated with formal explainability or interpretability methods (eg, attention maps or saliency-based techniques) that reveal internal model decision processes. It is important to reiterate that this report-generation assessment was limited to the 32 abnormal cases from the internal dataset, so the QAMAI scores reflect report quality for known pathological findings only and do not address report generation for normal anatomy.

Our findings align with recent advances in AI-assisted dental radiography. Kim et al10 demonstrated the feasibility of deep learning for automated cortical thickness measurement of the mandibular condyle on CBCT images. Similarly, studies employing convolutional neural networks for TMJ osteoarthritis detection have reported comparable accuracy metrics.30,31 The emergence of large language models with visual capabilities represents the next possible step, offering the unique advantage of providing interpretable diagnostic reasoning alongside classification outputs.32 The consistency between internal and external validation results supports the stability of the observed performance patterns across these two datasets, despite the constructed balanced composition of the external sample. All three models demonstrated stable performance on the external dataset compared to the internal validation. Gemini-3 maintained comparable accuracy from 90.3% (internal) to 90.0% (external), with balanced accuracy remaining stable at 0.90. Similarly, GPT-5.2 showed consistent performance with 75.0% (internal) to 74.3% (external) accuracy, and Qwen3-VL from 55.6% to 52.9%. These consistent results across two independent datasets, despite the constructed balanced composition of the external sample, suggest that the observed performance differences among models are not attributable to overfitting on the primary dataset.

From a clinical perspective, the high specificity observed across models (particularly Gemini-3) suggests potential utility as a preliminary screening tool. For general practitioners who may encounter TMJ abnormalities but lack specialized training in oral and maxillofacial radiology, VLMs may serve as a ‘second opinion’ to flag cases warranting specialist referral.30 However, the zero-shot performance reported here was achieved under highly controlled conditions with expert-selected representative slices, which may not reflect the challenges of routine clinical practice. The accessibility of cloud-based VLMs via simple web interfaces could lower technical barriers for resource-limited settings,33 though prospective reader-assistance studies are needed to establish real-world utility.

One of the notable advantages of VLMs over traditional ML is their ability to generate descriptive justifications.34 In our study, the models were instructed to perform a systematic anatomical assessment (anterior surface, superior surface, and medullary space). This structured output extends AI’s function beyond binary classification. By providing a ‘why’ behind the ‘yes/no’ diagnosis, VLMs may enhance clinician trust and facilitate patient communication, as complex radiographic findings are translated into understandable language according to standardized guidelines.

Limitations

Spectrum bias and dataset composition. The use of balanced datasets with approximately equal proportions of abnormal and normal cases introduces spectrum bias. In clinical practice, the prevalence of condylar osseous changes may differ in general dental settings. The artificially elevated disease prevalence affects positive and negative predictive values, and future validation studies should incorporate consecutive clinical cohorts reflecting the true disease spectrum.

Single-slice analysis versus volumetric interpretation. The evaluation of single expert-selected sagittal slices rather than complete volumetric CBCT interpretation represents a fundamental departure from clinical workflow. In routine practice, radiologists examine multiple planes throughout the entire volume to identify pathological features. Our study design required expert preselection of diagnostically relevant images, a task demanding radiological expertise that general practitioners may lack. Consequently, the reported performance likely represents an upper bound estimate constrained by current VLM architecture limitations that process only single two-dimensional images.

Report-generation scope. The report-generation evaluation using the QAMAI framework was conducted exclusively on the 32 positive/abnormal cases from the internal dataset. Therefore, the QAMAI scores reflect the linguistic and structural quality of AI-generated reports for known pathological findings only.

Closed-source model transparency. The three VLMs evaluated in this study are proprietary, closed-source models accessed via a third-party API platform. Exact details regarding model training data, architecture specifications, and alignment procedures are unavailable, which may lead to unstable long-term accuracy. This limitation is inherent to evaluating frontier commercial AI systems and should be considered when interpreting the reproducibility of these findings.

Demographic considerations (SAGER)

In accordance with the SAGER guidelines, attempts were made to evaluate the demographic characteristics of the study population. However, because the source data were obtained from open-access third-party repositories with incomplete demographic indexing, granular metadata (such as standardized sex and age labels) could not be reliably verified for the entire cohort. Consequently, sex-disaggregated subgroup analyses were omitted – a limitation common to the retrospective secondary use of public datasets. This technical evaluation thus focuses strictly on the models’ core algorithmic performance.

Summary

This proof-of-concept study provides preliminary evidence that zero-shot VLMs may have potential as auxiliary screening aids for identifying condylar osseous changes under controlled conditions. Gemini-3 achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy among tested models. However, the simplified study design – evaluating single expert-selected slices rather than full volumetric interpretation – represents a substantial difference from clinical practice.

These results should be interpreted as an initial benchmark rather than evidence supporting immediate clinical implementation. Future prospective, multicentre studies with consecutive clinical cohorts and real-world reader-assistance testing are essential to establish whether VLM-assisted diagnosis can provide meaningful clinical benefit in routine dental practice.
