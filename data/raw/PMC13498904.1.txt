Abstract

INTRODUCTION

Neuroinflammatory processes may play a central role in promoting mixed pathologies and cognitive impairment in the oldest‐old. We hypothesize that elevated neuroinflammation, indicated by reactive astrocytosis, acts on cerebrovascular disease and separately, on amyloid beta (Aβ) pathology, to promote downstream medial temporal lobe atrophy and memory impairment.

METHODS

We examined whether plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) concentration is associated with vascular territory‐defined white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), global Aβ burden on positron emission tomography (PET), and episodic memory in a diverse cohort (N = 439) of the oldest‐old: The LifeAfter90 Study.

RESULTS

Higher plasma GFAP was associated with increased posterior cerebral artery (PCA)‐defined WMH volume, but not with global amyloid or episodic memory. Both PCA WMH and global amyloid were associated with lower regional medial temporal lobe cortical thickness, which, in turn, was associated with episodic memory.

DISCUSSION

These findings offer insights into new therapeutic and prevention strategies in the oldest‐old population.

Highlights

Higher plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was associated with greater posterior cerebral artery (PCA)‐defined white matter hyperintensities (WMHs).

Plasma GFAP was not associated with global amyloid or episodic memory.

PCA WMH and amyloid were associated with medial temporal lobe cortical thickness.

Medial temporal lobe cortical thickness was related to memory in the oldest‐old.

1 BACKGROUND

The oldest‐old, or individuals aged 90+ years, are the fastest growing age segment, 1 with a prevalence of cognitive impairment of > 50%. 2 , 3 Yet, little is known regarding Alzheimer's disease (AD)‐related biomarkers contributing to this increased risk. Previous work, mostly based on post mortem studies, demonstrated that the presence of multiple pathologies is very common in the oldest‐old and is closely related to dementia. 4 , 5 In contrast, very few studies have studied neuroinflammation as one of the core contributing pathologies in this group. 6 , 7 There is an emerging interest in plasma neuroinflammatory biomarkers that may signal early AD 8 and a faster risk of cognitive decline in aging. 9 Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker of reactive astrocytic activity and an early predictor of AD, 10 is associated with small vessel cerebrovascular burden, 11 as identified by white matter hyperintensities (WMHs). More recent work suggests that GFAP is associated with WMHs, 12 , 13 especially in those who are amyloid positive. 14 Other work suggests that plasma GFAP is associated with amyloid, but not with WMHs, in cognitively unimpaired younger‐older adults. 15 Evidence also points to the association of GFAP with the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) as measured by positron emission tomography (PET) in younger‐older adults. 16 , 17 Moreover, GFAP is associated with memory impairment and the onset of AD 18 , 19 in the general aging population. However, these previous studies on GFAP and its association with other pathologies and memory were not performed in the oldest‐old, or adults aged 90+. Importantly, the oldest‐old represent a distinct group, characterized by a higher burden of mixed pathologies, altered relationships between neuropathology and cognition, and strong survival and resilience effects. 20 These features limit the generalizability of findings from younger older adults.

In a cognitively unimpaired aging population, recent work highlighted a potential pathway through which GFAP can affect memory: plasma GFAP was associated with only Aβ burden, which was associated with higher phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)217. 15 p‐tau217 was associated with lower hippocampal volume, which in turn was positively associated with memory function. We build on this work by including regionally defined WMHs by vascular territories and testing similar hypothetical models in the oldest‐old population

Understanding the associations of GFAP on regional WMHs would have implications for characterizing downstream effects of regional WMHs on memory as well. The link between posterior WMHs and memory and cognitive decline is well established in aging. 21 , 22 There is also emerging evidence that posterior WMHs are linked to integrity and neurodegeneration of the medial temporal lobe (MTL), 23 an area critical for memory function. The posterior cerebral artery (PCA) supplies to both posterior brain regions and areas of the MTL, 24 including parahippocampal cortex and the subiculum of the hippocampus. 25 A potential mechanism is that lower perfusion of the PCA leads to greater posterior WMHs linked to subsequent MTL neurodegeneration and memory impairment.

To assess this theoretical framework, we propose the concept of neurovascular susceptibility, encapsulating three interlinked processes driving memory impairment within the neurovascular unit: neuroinflammation, vascular pathology, and neurodegeneration. In this view, through a funnel‐like cascade, neuroinflammation occurring globally via astrocytic activity acts on small and deep posterior perfusing vessels that are particularly vulnerable in aging and disease, 26 leading to regional vascular lesions. This posterior vascular pathology, especially when distributed along PCA regions as one of the vascular territories, drives structural changes in the MTL, which contributes to memory impairment.

Here, we tested whether plasma GFAP is associated with vascular territory‐defined WMHs, global Aβ burden, and episodic memory in an ethnoracially diverse cohort of individuals aged 90+ years. We hypothesized, based on the hypothesized neurovascular susceptibility, that GFAP is associated with PCA‐defined WMHs, and that PCA WMHs are associated with lower MTL structural integrity and worse memory performance. Importantly, we looked for evidence of these pathways among the oldest‐old, in whom neuroinflammatory, vascular, and neurodegenerative processes are not well characterized.

2 METHODS

2.1 Participants

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We have identified existing studies regarding the association of plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), amyloid beta (Aβ), and memory function in older adults. There is limited work thus far done in the oldest‐old on these associations.

Interpretation: We found that higher plasma GFAP was associated with specifically increased posterior cerebral artery (PCA)‐defined WMHs, but not with global Aβ or episodic memory. Moreover, both posterior cerebral artery WMHs and global Aβ were associated with lower regional medial temporal lobe thickness, including parahippocampal cortical thickness and lower entorhinal cortical thickness, respectively. These regional thickness measures were associated with episodic memory in the oldest‐old.

Future directions: Future work can examine these relationships longitudinally to probe the timing and directionality of these associations among plasma GFAP, regional WMHs, amyloid, medial temporal lobe structure, and memory.

We included participants from LifeAfter90, a large and ongoing longitudinal cohort study that aims to understand life‐course factors of cognitive decline and brain health in ethnoracially diverse individuals aged ≥ 90 years. Eligible participants were Spanish and/or English speaking, long‐term members of Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), and resided in the San Francisco Bay and Sacramento areas of California at the start of the study in 2018. Exclusion criteria included inability to provide informed consent or a diagnosis of dementia or other neurodegenerative disease, hospice care, or dialysis in their electronic medical record at the time of recruitment. LifeAfter90 participants were asked if they were interested in participating in neuroimaging, and a random sample of ≈ 25% of those who were interested was selected for structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and amyloid PET. Study protocols were approved by the KPNC and the University of California, Davis Institutional Review Boards. All participants provided informed consent. Participants were included in the current analyses if they had imaging and/or plasma GFAP data. Of the 1364 LifeAfter90 participants enrolled as of March 2026, N = 439 participants were included in our analytic sample, of whom n = 298 had WMH data, n = 301 had regional volume/thickness, n = 237 had amyloid PET data, and n = 267 had plasma GFAP data. We provide the subsample characteristics that completed each modality (MRI, PET, plasma GFAP) in Table S1 in supporting information. As there were overlapping and non‐overlapping modalities of biomarker data available, we have also provided the sample size of each tested model in our Tables S2–S5 in supporting information and Figure 1.

2.2 MRI

MRI was acquired using standardized protocols on 3T Siemens scanners (TrioTrim and Prisma Fit models). Two sequences were acquired: T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo (3DT1): repetition time (TR) 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1100 ms, 192 slices, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm; fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR): TR = 8800 ms, TE = 500 ms, TI = 2360 ms, 96 slices, FOV = 256 mm, acquisition matrix = 256×256, slice thickness = 2 mm.

2.3 MRI analyses

2.3.1 WMH segmentation

WMHs were segmented using a Bayesian approach estimated from FLAIR images 27 , 28 (see Figure 2A) and co‐registered into the Montreal Neurological Institute space using linear and non‐linear transformation. WMH volumes were obtained from FLAIR and T1‐weighted images using a validated automated segmentation pipeline. 27 , 29 Briefly, T1‐weighted images were co‐registered to FLAIR, non‐brain tissue was removed using the T1‐derived brain mask, FLAIR intensities were normalized, and Bayesian tissue segmentation was applied to identify WMH voxels. This WMH quantification approach has demonstrated excellent inter‐rater reliability, test–retest repeatability, and inter‐scanner reproducibility in the MarkVCID neuroimaging validation study, with intraclass correlation coefficients > 0.94 across reliability/reproducibility assessments. 29

FIGURE 2 Visualizing WMH and a regional segmentation approach. A, Axial slice of a FLAIR image with noticeable WMH. B, Vascular territory atlas overlaid on a smoothed FLAIR MNI template, depicting the ACA, MCA, and PCA territories. ACA, anterior cerebral artery; FLAIR, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery; MCA, middle cerebral artery; MNI, Montreal Neurological Institute; PCA, posterior cerebral artery; WMH, white matter hyperintensity

Using the vascular territory atlas, 30 we derived left and right anterior cerebral artery (ACA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), and PCA WMH volumes. Left and right ACA, MCA, and PCA WMHs were summed to derive (total) ACA, MCA, and PCA WMH volumes, respectively, as visualized in Figure 2B. Global WMH volume was calculated as the sum of ACA, MCA, and PCA, cerebellum, and pons/medulla WMH volumes.

2.3.2 MTL subregional segmentation

Hippocampal segmentation used a harmonized atlas‐based 31 , 32 , 33 diffeomorphic approach 34 with atlas fusion using multi‐atlas label fusion 35 and intensity‐based label refinement. Total intracranial volume (ICV) segmentation was performed using a convolutional neural network method, as described previously. 36 Mean cortical thickness computation uses a registration method based on Das et al. 37 , 38 Using FreeSurfer, parcellations of regional brain volumes and cortical thickness were also derived from the Desikan–Killiany atlas. 39 , 40 Left and right entorhinal thickness and parahippocampal thickness were averaged across hemispheres. Hippocampal volumes were summed across hemispheres and residualized for total ICV.

2.4 Plasma GFAP and quantification

Blood samples were obtained and processed within 2 hours of collection. Plasma aliquots (250 µL) were stored at −80°C following previously reported standard procedures. 41 Plasma GFAP concentrations were measured using the single molecule array (Simoa) Neurology 4‐Plex E (N4PE+) Advantage PLUS assay (product code 104465) with a 10‐fold dilution on the Quanterix HD‐X analyzer (Quanterix). All samples were randomized prior to analysis and processed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

2.5 Amyloid PET acquisition and processing

PET scans in LifeAfter90 were conducted at the University of California Davis Medical Center using the amyloid‐binding fluorine‐18 radiotracer florbetapir (Avid Radiopharmaceuticals) and were processed using Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative protocols that are found online (see https://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/pet‐analysis‐method/pet‐analysis/). Images were smoothed and linearly registered to the structural T1 MRI native space. Mean standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated to quantify regional tracer uptake across voxels relative to the whole cerebellar gray matter reference region. 42 We analyzed global SUVR as a continuous variable.

2.6 Visual quality control

All image processing pipelines underwent visual quality control and inspection of images. 43 , 44

2.7 Neuropsychological testing

Verbal episodic memory was assessed by the Spanish and English Neuropsychological Assessment Scales (SENAS) 45 , 46 during study visits occurring approximately every 6 months. The SENAS is a cognitive battery previously validated for comparisons of cognitive change across racial/ethnic and linguistically diverse groups. Verbal episodic memory scores were derived using a multi‐trial word‐list learning test. These scores were z standardized using the mean and standard deviation from the full baseline sample. We included verbal episodic memory scores that were selected by preference post‐MRI and closest to the imaging visit of each participant (median time interval between MRI acquisition and closest cognitive evaluation = 0.24 years). For analyses including plasma GFAP and verbal episodic memory, we instead included verbal episodic memory scores closest to the plasma measurement (median time interval between plasma measurement and closest cognitive evaluation = 0.44 years).

2.8 Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.0) through RStudio. We conducted a series of separate linear regression analyses to test sets of associations, as conceptualized by Figure 3A, and depicted the main findings in Figure 3B. We first tested whether plasma GFAP is associated with global and vascular territory‐defined WMHs, global amyloid SUVR, and verbal episodic memory (reported in Table S2). We assessed whether regional WMHs are associated with global amyloid SUVR (reported in Table S3). Next, we examined associations of regional WMHs and global amyloid SUVR with MTL structure (reported in Table S4). Last, we tested whether regional WMHs, global amyloid SUVR, and MTL structure are associated with verbal episodic memory (reported in Table S5). Main results are also depicted in Figure 1. Due to differences in the measurement scale of GFAP concentration (pg/mL) compared to imaging (cm3 or SUVR) and cognitive measures, unstandardized beta coefficients may appear small in magnitude. These coefficients reflect the change in outcome per 1 pg/mL increase in GFAP. One outlier with a plasma GFAP value of > 1000 pg/mL was removed. For WMHs, one participant had a global WMH value of > 100 cm3; subsequently, this participant's global, ACA, MCA, and PCA WMH values were also removed.

FIGURE 3 Conceptual models visualizing tested associations and observed associations. A, Conceptual model visualizing associations among plasma, imaging, and cognitive measures. B, Findings from testing separate associations, with colors of paths indicating directionality and significance (gray dotted line = non‐significant association; green solid = positive association; red solid = negative association)

In analyses of regional WMHs and MTL structure and memory, we explored associations only with the regional WMHs that were significantly associated with plasma GFAP. Similarly, we explored associations of MTL structure with memory only for structures that were associated with the aforementioned regional WMH or global amyloid SUVR. As a post hoc analysis, we tested if regional WMHs that were associated with plasma GFAP were associated with verbal episodic memory when controlling for global amyloid SUVR. In a sensitivity analysis, we assessed whether associations remain after adjusting for baseline cognitive impairment (baseline status of mild cognitive impairment [MCI] or dementia) as an additional covariate.

For all models, we adjusted for age (at the time of the outcome measure) and sex/gender. We included a covariate for time intervals between cognitive and imaging and/or plasma visits, as well as between imaging and plasma, in models including a combination of cognitive, imaging, or plasma measures. We additionally adjusted for total WMHs in models that included regional WMHs to find regionally specific effects that were not explained by overall WMH severity, an approach described by Biesbroek et al. 47 For models with verbal episodic memory as the outcome, we further adjusted for years of education.

3.2 Association of plasma GFAP with global and vascular territory–defined WMHs, Aβ, and verbal episodic memory

We tested the associations of plasma GFAP with regional WMHs and global amyloid SUVR and visualized these associations in Figure 1A. Plasma GFAP was not associated with global WMH burden (B = −0.014, 95% confidence interval [CI = −0.041, 0.013], P = 0.301). Higher plasma GFAP was only associated with greater PCA WMHs (B = 0.001, 95% CI = [0.000, 0.002], P = 0.026). Plasma GFAP was not associated with ACA WMHs or MCA WMHs (ACA: B = −0.001, 95% CI = [−0.004, 0.002], P = 0.456; MCA: B = 0, 95% CI = [−0.003, 0.003], P = 0.940). Plasma GFAP was also not associated with global amyloid SUVR (B = 0, 95% CI = [−0.000, 0.001], P = 0.184). Further, plasma GFAP was not associated with episodic memory (B = 0, 95% CI = [−0.001, 0.000], P = 0.441).

3.3 Association of vascular territory–defined WMHs with Aβ

We assessed whether there were spatially specific associations with regional WMHs and global amyloid SUVR, and visualized these associations in Figure 1B. We found that regional WMH volumes, including ACA, MCA, or PCA WMHs, were not associated with global amyloid SUVR (ACA: B = −0.004, 95% CI = [−0.023, 0.016], P = 0.704; MCA: B = 0.009, 95% CI = [−0.011, 0.029], P = 0.373; PCA: B = −0.011, 95% CI = [−0.040, 0.018], P = 0.447).

3.4 Association of vascular‐defined WMHs and Aβ with MTL structural integrity

We tested whether PCA‐defined WMH and global amyloid SUVR are associated with regional MTL structural integrity, and visualized these associations in Figure 1C. We found that greater PCA WMHs were associated with lower parahippocampal thickness (B = −0.038, 95% CI = [−0.065, −0.012], P = 0.004), but not with hippocampal volume or entorhinal thickness (hippocampal volume: B = −0.039, 95% CI = [−0.112, 0.033], P = 0.288; entorhinal thickness: B = −0.03, 95% CI = [−0.088, 0.028], P = 0.311). Meanwhile, we found that higher amyloid SUVR was associated with lower entorhinal thickness (B = −0.265, 95% CI = [−0.530, −0.001], P = 0.049), but not with hippocampal volume or parahippocampal thickness (hippocampal volume: B = 0.064, 95% CI = [−0.28, 0.408], P = 0.713; parahippocampal thickness: B = −0.071, 95% CI = [−0.196, 0.054], P = 0.266).

3.5 Association of vascular territory–defined WMHs, Aβ, and MTL structures with verbal episodic memory

We examined the associations of PCA‐defined WMHs, global amyloid SUVR, and MTL structures with episodic memory, and visualized these associations in Figure 1D. Greater PCA WMHs were associated with lower verbal episodic memory (B = −0.177, 95% CI = [−0.308, −0.046], P = 0.008). Increased global amyloid SUVR was associated with worse episodic memory performance (B = −0.875, 95% CI = [−1.505, −0.246], P = 0.007). Both parahippocampal thickness and entorhinal thickness were positively associated with episodic memory (parahippocampal thickness: B = 0.851, 95% CI = [0.294, 1.407], P = 0.003; entorhinal thickness: B = 0.529, 95% CI = [0.277, 0.78], P < 0.001).

3.6 Independent associations of vascular territory–defined WMHs and Aβ with verbal episodic memory

As a post hoc analysis, we last tested whether PCA‐defined WMHs and global amyloid SUVR are independently associated with episodic memory when included in the same model. Greater PCA‐defined WMHs were marginally associated with lower episodic memory independently of global amyloid SUVR (B = −0.122, 95% CI = [−0.259, 0.015], P = 0.08). Higher global amyloid SUVR was also independently associated with worse verbal episodic memory (B = −1.326, 95% CI = [−1.959, −0.694], P < 0.001).

3.7 Covariate adjustment of cognitive impairment

In a sensitivity analysis, we tested whether associations remained after additionally adjusting for baseline cognitive impairment. We found that this covariate adjustment did not affect statistical significance or direction for most associations, except for the association between PCA WMHs and verbal episodic memory performance. While the association between greater PCA WMHs and lower episodic memory was in the previously observed direction, the association was now trending (B = −0.12, 95% CI = [−0.253, 0.012], P = 0.077).

4 DISCUSSION

In this cross‐sectional study of the oldest‐old, we tested whether plasma GFAP is differentially associated with vascular territory–defined WMHs and global amyloid SUVR. We found that plasma GFAP was associated only with PCA WMHs, and not with global amyloid in this cohort. Though we did not find a direct association of plasma GFAP with episodic memory, we found associations linking processes downstream of GFAP with episodic memory. There were no associations between regional WMHs and global amyloid. We observed that PCA WMHs were associated with lower parahippocampal thickness. Meanwhile, increased amyloid burden was also associated with lower entorhinal thickness. Greater PCA WMHs and amyloid burden were associated with worse episodic memory. Furthermore, both lower parahippocampal thickness and entorhinal thickness were associated with worse memory performance. We observed a pattern of associations linking neuroinflammation, cerebrovascular disease, and neurodegeneration with episodic memory in the oldest‐old.

The literature on neuroinflammation and regional small vessel disease is sparse, especially among the oldest‐old. While previous studies did not test whether GFAP promotes WMHs in regionally specific areas, there is some recent evidence suggesting that global neuroinflammation can induce small vessel disease in a regionally specific manner. For example, a recent study demonstrated that cerebrospinal fluid chitinase‐3‐like protein 1 (YKL‐40), another reactive astrocytic marker, was associated with both parietal and temporal WMH longitudinally. 48 One study measuring in vivo microglial activation as measured by [11C]PK11195 PET imaging found that increased global neuroinflammation was associated with deep rather than periventricular WMHs. 49 Alternative to the view that neuroinflammation occurs globally, another study demonstrated that increased [11C]PK11195 binding was found locally in the occipital lobe in patients with AD dementia, 50 indicating that perhaps the association of GFAP with PCA WMHs is stronger compared to other regions due to regional neuroinflammation.

While each of our associations was hypothesized to be unidirectional in our conceptual model, there are potential bidirectional paths. For example, there is evidence of a bidirectional association between neuroinflammation and AD pathology, 51 in which GFAP can induce amyloidosis, which itself can cyclically release more proinflammatory factors. 52 , 53 Similarly, while more evidence demonstrates that neuroinflammation is upstream of vascular disease, there is also work suggesting that vascular pathology can promote reactive astrocytic activity. 54 The relationship between WMHs and Aβ has been unclear in previous work, and whether these two pathophysiological processes occur in parallel, or if one drives the other, remains unknown. 55 , 56 We tested whether WMHs are associated with global Aβ, to rule out the possibility that these processes are driving one another, and indirectly promoting neurodegeneration and memory impairment due to higher levels of Aβ. Furthermore, our post hoc analysis showed that vascular processes such as PCA‐defined WMHs may have contributions to memory independent of Aβ levels. This suggests multiple independent pathways converging to memory impairment in the oldest‐old.

These findings support our hypothesized model (see Figure 3) of neurovascular susceptibility being relevant in the oldest‐old. In this unified model, all three processes act in a spatially specific manner to contribute to memory impairment: neuroinflammation, vascular disease, and neurodegeneration. In this view, neuroinflammation is thought to promote vascular pathology in regions particularly vulnerable to aging and disease. In PCA‐defined vascular areas, WMHs may drive neurodegeneration in spatially overlapping MTL areas, part of the PCA territory. 24 , 57 Atrophy in these MTL regions leads to subsequent memory impairment in the oldest‐old. Importantly, the association of PCA WMHs with memory impairment was marginally independent of Aβ levels. In an alternative view, neuroinflammation is elevated locally, which may drive its association with PCA‐defined WMHs. However, evidence for this alternative view is limited by our use of plasma GFAP, which captures widespread astrocytic activity in the central nervous system. Individuals with the highest volumes of PCA WMHs may exert disproportionate influence on some of these associations, particularly given that severe WMH burden is less common among relatively healthy oldest‐old adults in our sample. Preserving the biological distribution of WMH values, rather than transforming the raw WMH volumes, allowed us to find these biologically plausible relationships.

We found evidence of potentially separable and independent pathways through which both regional WMHs and Aβ lead to episodic memory impairment in the oldest‐old. Our finding of plasma GFAP being only associated with PCA‐defined WMH but not with Aβ was unexpected, as previous studies have linked GFAP with Aβ burden. 16 , 17 However, this previous work was conducted among younger older adults. We highlight these findings in the oldest‐old that are different from those in the younger‐old, to emphasize that such associations should be taken in the context of the oldest‐old population. Future work should examine these questions longitudinally; cross‐sectional analyses may have limited us from finding these associations between GFAP and Aβ accumulation.

This study has potential implications on development of personalized therapeutic interventions for the oldest‐old, who are often underrepresented in clinical trials. 58 Currently approved treatments for AD, including anti‐amyloid antibodies, carry higher risks for this population. 59 As the oldest‐old are more prone to mixed‐pathology, 6 , 7 a multi‐targeted treatment, rather than a single pathway approach, may also be more relevant. The current study illustrated that there are multiple points in the pathway between neuroinflammation and memory impairment that are potentially intervenable. While our study was limited to individuals without a dementia diagnosis in their electronic medical record at the time of enrollment, this did not preclude undiagnosed individuals or individuals who later progressed to develop dementia from being included in our study. Adjusting for baseline cognitive impairment (MCI/dementia status) attenuated the association between PCA WMH and episodic memory to trend level, which may reflect that baseline cognitive impairment lies on the pathway between PCA WMHs and subsequent memory impairment.

The current study had some limitations. Given that these oldest‐old individuals may display cognitive resilience despite high levels of AD pathology, 20 it is important not to overgeneralize to all aging individuals, including younger older adults. Cross‐sectional analyses were performed due to the availability of biomarker measures at a single time point, whereas cognitive measures were collected longitudinally with substantial variability in their timing relative to biomarker acquisition. This heterogeneity would limit the interpretability of biomarker and cognitive change models, precluding longitudinal analyses across biomarkers in the current study. Our cross‐sectional design limits us from concluding on the temporality or causality of the associations among markers or with cognition. While multiple comparisons corrections were not applied to the current analyses, the limited number of studies and data on the oldest‐old population motivated us to prioritize a hypothesis‐driven exploratory study that may inform future confirmatory studies. Future work can investigate these associations longitudinally and conduct mediation analyses given sufficient power. Amyloid PET–based regional staging approaches would allow for a better understanding of any associations of GFAP with different stages of amyloid spread. Additionally, future studies incorporating tau PET can assess the hypothesis that PCA‐defined WMHs drive elevated local tau PET levels, leading to regional MTL neurodegeneration. However, this is the first study of the contribution of GFAP on vascular territory–defined WMH, and its associations with Aβ and episodic memory in ethnoracially diverse oldest‐old.

Our work in the oldest‐old demonstrated that while plasma GFAP is not directly associated with episodic memory in the oldest‐old, it is associated with PCA‐defined WMHs, which carries important downstream consequences. We found that PCA‐defined WMHs are associated with lower parahippocampal thickness, which is positively associated with episodic memory. This effect of PCA‐defined WMHs on memory was marginally independent of Aβ levels. While plasma GFAP was not associated with Aβ levels, Aβ was associated with both entorhinal cortex thickness and memory impairment. These separable and independent pathways highlight multiple points of intervention to lower and delay AD progression.
