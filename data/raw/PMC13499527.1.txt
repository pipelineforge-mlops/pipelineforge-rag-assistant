Abstract

Background

Over the past decade, neuropsychopharmacology has shifted from stagnation to momentum, with first-in-class mechanisms and biomarker-enabled trials spanning psychiatry and neurology.

Methods

We narratively synthesized advances from 2013 to 2026 across central nervous system (CNS) discovery and development, including pivotal trials, regulatory actions, digital/real-world evidence, genetics, artificial intelligence (AI), and implementation/global-access themes that are endorsed by international societies.

Results

Therapeutic gains include rapid-acting drugs for treatment-resistant depression (intranasal esketamine); psychedelic-assisted therapy for posttraumatic stress disorder and depression; neuroactive steroid γ-aminobutyric acid-A receptor positive allosteric modulators (brexanolone, zuranolone) for postpartum depression; non-dopaminergic muscarinic agonists (xanomeline–trospium) for schizophrenia; orexin receptor antagonists for insomnia; and anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab) for early Alzheimer’s disease. Persistent barriers include high mid-/late-stage attrition that is driven by placebo effects, subjective endpoints, and preclinical-to-clinical gaps; regulatory and economic headwinds; and limited generalizability from tightly run trials. Emerging enablers include adaptive/platform designs, digital health technologies, patient-reported outcomes, and clinical outcome assessments, real-world evidence (RWE), AI/machine learning (ML), genetics for target de-risking and biomarker-guided stratification, and publicly accessible large CNS relevant biological datasets.

Conclusions

To convert momentum into durable progress, we recommend: (1) deeper academia–industry/stakeholder collaboration and sustained funding for high-risk/high-reward science from industry, governments, and non-for profit foundations; (2) modernized regulation (flexible evidentiary paths, novel endpoints, and clear guidance on adaptive/platform trials); (3) data-driven development integrating RWE, AI/ML, and precision medicine; (4) the adoption of Neuroscience-based Nomenclature; and (5) a global-access mandate with essential-medicine inclusion, equitable pricing/licensing, capacity building, tele-enabled mental health, and geographically diverse research. Aligning scientific innovation with implementation and equity can accelerate translation and ensure new treatments benefit patients worldwide.

Summations

1) The past decade has demonstrated that mechanism-driven innovation in central nervous system disorders is feasible, with first-in-class therapies emerging across depression, schizophrenia, insomnia, and Alzheimer’s disease, reflecting the successful translation of foundational neuroscience into clinical practice.

2) Persistent barriers including high placebo response, translational gaps, regulatory conservatism, and limited generalizability continue to constrain success rates, necessitating adaptive trial designs, biomarker integration, and data-driven development strategies.

3) Sustainable progress in neuropsychopharmacology will depend not only on scientific innovation but also on regulatory modernization, precision-medicine approaches, collaborative funding models, and equitable global access to emerging therapies.

Considerations

1) While several first-in-class therapies have emerged, their effect sizes are often modest, long-term durability remains under evaluation, and real-world effectiveness may differ from controlled trial outcomes.

2 Although adaptive designs, biomarkers, and data-driven strategies hold promise, their regulatory acceptance, scalability, and reproducibility across heterogeneous global settings remain uncertain.

3) Calls for regulatory modernization and global equity must be balanced against safety oversight, resource constraints, and the economic realities of drug development, which may limit rapid or universal implementation.

Introduction

Over the past decade, neuropsychopharmacology has emerged from a prolonged period of limited progress into a phase of accelerated therapeutic innovation. Across the central nervous system (CNS) spectrum, spanning psychiatry and neurology, new modalities have reached patients: rapid-acting antidepressants, including ketamine and its S-enantiomer esketamine; psychedelic-assisted therapies; non-dopaminergic approaches to psychosis; orexin receptor antagonists for insomnia; and putative disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Collectively, these advances are reshaping the CNS treatment landscape. Yet the field’s trajectory remains uneven. Notable late-stage failures such as those involving trace amine-associated receptor 1 (TAAR1) agonists and glycine transporter-1 (GlyT1) inhibitors underscore persistent scientific, methodological, and regulatory hurdles in CNS drug development.

In this position paper developed with the endorsement of leading societies in this field, including the International College of Neuropsychopharmacology (Collegium Internationale Neuro-Psychopharmacologicum [CINP]), the International Brain Research Organization, the Asian College of Neuropsychopharmacology (AsCNP), and the African College of Neuropsychopharmacology, we highlight the recent successes and setbacks in the field, discuss the converging paths of psychiatry and neurology, and emphasize how fundamental neuroscience discoveries have translated into therapies. We also examine ongoing hurdles, such as placebo effects, translational gaps, regulatory conservatism, real-world applicability, and high clinical trial failure rates, and advocate for strategies to sustain momentum, including stronger academia–industry collaboration, innovative trial designs, harnessing large-scale data and digital health measurements, use of genetic and biomarker information for precision medicine, regulatory reform, the adoption of Neuroscience-based Nomenclature (NbN), and greater investment by government, industry, and non-for-profit foundations in high-risk, high-reward research. Our aim is to provide an up-to-date, balanced perspective on the last decade’s progress, including both successful and unsuccessful efforts, and to delineate priorities that can guide the next phase of neuropsychopharmacology. Based on the literature synthesized here and recent experience in the field of psychiatry and neurology, we prioritize 5 major barriers to CNS drug innovation: (1) biological and clinical heterogeneity, (2) weak signal detection and placebo inflation, (3) translational gaps between preclinical findings and later-stage efficacy, (4) regulatory and evidentiary bottlenecks, and (5) implementation and access barriers. We correspondingly highlight 5 actionable priorities for the next decade: smarter trial designs and outcome measures, earlier target de-risking through biomarkers and human genetics, integration of real-world and digital data, modernized but rigorous regulatory pathways, and explicit planning for implementation and equitable global access. The following sections elaborate these priorities and their practical implications for CNS therapeutic development, with Table 1 providing a concise overview of recurring barriers and practical remedies. As a narrative review and position paper, this article does not apply a formal evidence-grading framework.

Therapeutic breakthroughs bridging psychiatry and neurology

Rapid-acting drugs for depression and psychedelic therapies

One of the most significant psychiatric breakthroughs over the past decade has been the advent of rapid-acting antidepressant treatments. In 2019, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved intranasal esketamine (the S-enantiomer of ketamine) for treatment-resistant depression (TRD), marking the first truly new pharmacological mechanism of action for TRD in decades. Esketamine, delivered via nasal spray, demonstrated rapid antidepressant effects in patients who had not responded to conventional therapies. For example, in one pivotal trial the day 28 response rate (≥50% reduction in the Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale total score) was 69.3% with esketamine plus an oral antidepressant versus 52.0% with placebo plus an oral antidepressant.1 This was a game-changing development, as traditional drugs for depression often take weeks to show their full efficacy and fail to help a significant subset of patients. Notably, ketamine’s antidepressant effect was initially discovered through academic research in the early 2000s,2,3 decades after the drug’s use as an anesthetic. Its success illustrates how previous basic neuroscience insights into glutamate neurotransmission in depression4 were translated into a novel therapy for depression, essentially drug repurposing from anesthesia to psychiatry driven by mechanistic understanding.

In parallel, the once-taboo field of psychedelic therapy has moved into mainstream clinical research. Novel trials of 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA or midomafetamine) for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and psilocybin (the active compound in “magic mushrooms”) for depression have yielded promising results.5,6 For example, 2 Phase III trials found that MDMA-assisted therapy was significantly more effective than placebo plus the same psychotherapy in reducing PTSD symptom severity and functional impairment.5,7 These findings have prompted regulators to facilitate further development. Indeed, the FDA granted Breakthrough Therapy designations to MDMA for PTSD in 2017 and psilocybin for TRD in 2018 and later major depressive disorder (MDD) in 2019, respectively, to expedite research. However, in 2024 the FDA did not approve midomafetamine for PTSD and issued a Complete Response Letter, indicating that the submitted data were insufficient for approval and that additional clinical evidence would be needed.8 Psilocybin was tested in numerous Phase II trials for depression9 and TRD6 and is moving into a Phase III trial.10 Lysergic acid diethylamide was tested in Phase II for generalized anxiety11,12 and is moving also to Phase III.13 Neurobiological studies have shown that psychedelics exert their effects through multiple mechanisms. Serotonin 5-hydroxytryptamine-2A (5-HT2A) receptor-mediated neuroplasticity is a key driver of antidepressant action,14 while polypharmacology across serotonin, dopamine, and adrenergic G protein-coupled receptors reveals additional pathways that contribute to both therapeutic outcomes and subjective experiences.15 Psychedelic-assisted therapies remain subject to stringent regulation, yet their trajectory illustrates how translational research spanning the rediscovery of mid-20th-century compounds and, further back, the revival of substances that are rooted in traditional healing practices that have been in use for over 2 millennia can yield new therapeutic paradigms in mental health in the 21st century.

Postpartum depression: neuroactive-steroid GABAA receptor modulators in practice

Among advances in reproductive mental health, neuroactive-steroid γ-aminobutyric acid-A (GABAA) receptor positive allosteric modulators (brexanolone and zuranolone) emerged from peripartum neurosteroid physiology. Allopregnanolone concentrations rise during pregnancy and decline after delivery,16 and this biology informed the development of exogenous analogues to restore inhibitory tone in postpartum depression (PPD). Brexanolone, an intravenous formulation of allopregnanolone, produced rapid, clinically meaningful improvements in randomized trials and, in March 2019,17 became the first FDA-approved therapy specifically for PPD.

To broaden access, the orally bioavailable analogue zuranolone was developed as a once-daily, 14-day course. Phase III evidence demonstrated antidepressant effects within days with benefits maintained beyond the dosing window.18 On August 4, 2023, the FDA approved zuranolone as the first oral treatment for PPD. Altogether, brexanolone and zuranolone exemplify a bench-to-bedside pathway in which clinical observation of hormonally triggered mood disturbances and mechanistic validation converged to yield a novel rapidly acting, mechanism-anchored treatments inaugurating a new therapeutic class for PPD.

Schizophrenia: new mechanisms beyond the dopamine hypothesis

Schizophrenia treatment has long relied on antipsychotic drugs that attach to dopamine D₂ receptors and attenuate the dopaminergic neurotransmission, a fundamental mechanism that has remained unchanged since the 1950s. The past decade, however, brought forward first-in-class therapies that do not depend on dopamine blockade. A prime example is KarXT (xanomeline–trospium), an oral combination that stimulates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (M1/M4 subtypes) in the brain while mitigating peripheral cholinergic side effects. KarXT builds on years of basic and clinical research suggesting that muscarinic receptor agonism can reduce psychotic symptoms.19,20 In Phase II and III EMERGENT trials that were published in 2021-2024, KarXT significantly reduced Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale scores versus placebo, with low rates of extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinemia that are typical of D₂-blocking drugs.21,22 Building on these data, the U.S. FDA approved xanomeline–trospium in September 2024 as Cobenfy for adults with schizophrenia, establishing a new antipsychotic class based on muscarinic receptor agonism. This breakthrough, spearheaded by academia–industry collaboration (ie, a small biotechnology company advancing a molecule that was first studied via an academic–pharma partnership), heralds a shift toward non-dopaminergic treatment pathways in schizophrenia.

Not all novel mechanisms have succeeded, however. TAAR1 agonists offered another approach to treating schizophrenia without directly targeting dopamine receptors. Ulotaront (SEP-363856), the first TAAR1 agonist to reach late-stage development, showed encouraging results in early studies.23 However, in 2023 2 Phase III trials in acutely psychotic schizophrenia patients failed to meet their primary endpoints. The developers attributed this setback in part to an unexpectedly high placebo response that may have masked the drug’s effects. This disappointment echoes another recent failure, namely inhibitors of the GlyT1. GlyT1 inhibitors such as iclepertin aim to enhance N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor function by increasing synaptic glycine, targeting cognitive impairment that is associated with schizophrenia rather than positive symptoms per se.24 Despite promising Phase II signals of improvements in cognition, a comprehensive Phase III program (the CONNEX trials) for iclepertin failed to show significant benefit over placebo for cognition or functioning at 6 months; the sponsor discontinued the long-term extension trial (CONNEX-X) following these results. These twin setbacks underscore the high risks in translating complex CNS mechanisms into clinical success and the challenges of psychiatry trial design and endpoints, particularly high placebo responses and measurement issues in cognition trials. Nonetheless, the push beyond dopamine remains scientifically justified, and KarXT’s success shows that some bets on novel neurobiology do pay off. For example, evenamide, a selective voltage-gated sodium channel blocker that was developed as an add-on therapy for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, has shown promising efficacy and safety.25

Orexin receptor antagonists for insomnia: from orphan receptor to clinical use

Sleep medicine has been transformed by the development of orexin receptor antagonists for chronic insomnia, a breakthrough rooted in basic neuroscience and pharmacology. The orexin (hypocretin) neuropeptide system, which promotes wakefulness, was discovered in 1998 by 2 research groups as ligands for previously orphan G-protein–coupled receptors (OX1R/OX2R).26,27 Soon after these discoveries, seminal animal studies showed that loss of orexin signaling produces narcolepsy phenotypes in prepro-orexin knockout mice and hypocretin receptor-2 mutations in narcoleptic dogs and, shortly thereafter, human narcolepsy was linked to a marked loss of orexin neurons in the hypothalamus.28,29 Orexin A and orexin B (also known as hypocretin-1 and hypocretin-2, respectively) bind both OX1R and OX2R, with orexin B showing relatively greater selectivity for OX2R. Loss of orexin signaling, particularly along OX2R-related pathways, is strongly linked to narcolepsy, which helps explain why orexin receptor pharmacology has become such an important therapeutic avenue in sleep–wake disorders.30 These findings suggested that dampening orexin signaling could facilitate sleep, catalyzing industry efforts to develop orexin receptor antagonists. The first of these, suvorexant, was approved by the U.S. FDA in 2014, establishing proof-of-concept that orexin receptor antagonists can treat insomnia; pivotal trials showed improvements in sleep onset and maintenance with an acceptable safety profile.31 Following suvorexant, 2 additional orexin receptor antagonists expanded options: lemborexant (approved in 2019) with Phase III data (SUNRISE-1/-2) demonstrating efficacy on both objective and patient-reported measures, and daridorexant (approved in 2022) with Phase III trials showing improvements in night-time sleep and daytime functioning.28,32,33 From discovery (1998) to first approval (2014) took 16 years. By 2022, 3 orexin receptor antagonists had reached the clinic, which can be regarded as an illustrative case of translational neuroscience that integrated basic biology (identifying a wake-promoting system) with medicinal chemistry and clinical development, ultimately bringing a new mechanism from bench to bedside. The orexin story also exemplifies cross-disciplinary collaboration. Sleep researchers, neurologists, and psychiatrists collectively advanced a therapy that bridges neurological circuitry and psychiatric symptomatology.

Alzheimer’s disease: disease-modifying therapies

Nowhere have the stakes for breakthrough therapies been higher than in AD, a field that has been marred by decades of clinical trial failures. In this context, the recent success of lecanemab was a watershed moment. Lecanemab is a monoclonal antibody that targets aggregated amyloid-β, especially soluble protofibrils, in the brain. In the large Phase III Clarity AD trial in patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD, lecanemab significantly slowed cognitive and functional decline by 27% over 18 months versus placebo on the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (mean change 1.21 vs 1.66).34 This effect was modeled to translate into several additional months of preserved function for the average patient over the trial period.35 These results, first published online in late 2022, provided the clearest evidence to date that reducing amyloid burden can modify the course of early AD. Regulators responded swiftly; the FDA granted accelerated approval on January 6, 2023, followed by traditional approval on July 6, 2023, making lecanemab one of the first disease-modifying therapies for AD with confirmatory clinical benefit. While lecanemab is not a cure and carries risks such as amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, careful patient selection and monitoring are recommended. Other therapies are close behind. Donanemab, another antibody that clears deposited amyloid, subsequently received FDA approval on July 2, 2024 for early symptomatic AD after a positive Phase III result. At the same time, multiple anti-tau protein approaches and other mechanism-based therapies remain in active clinical development, reflecting a broadened focus beyond amyloid alone.36 Progress in AD has been enabled by advances in both therapeutics and biomarkers, notably amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, first demonstrated with Pittsburgh Compound-B,37 which allowed researchers to measure target engagement and plaque removal in trials such as Clarity AD. What was once an intractable neurodegenerative disease now has a toehold of therapeutic modification, built on painstaking molecular pathology and biomarker science.

Foundations of discovery: translating neuroscience into therapies

Each breakthrough highlighted above rests on foundational discoveries in neuroscience and pharmacology. Basic research has been the wellspring of innovation, often bearing fruit only after years of work. The glutamatergic (including NMDA) hypothesis of depression, developed from preclinical studies of glutamatergic modulation and synaptic plasticity, paved the way for ketamine’s psychiatric use. Early randomized trials showed rapid antidepressant effects, and mechanistic work linked these effects to mammalian/mechanistic target of rapamycin-dependent synaptogenesis and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors.38,39 Decades-old observations that classic psychedelics are 5-HT₂A receptor agonists, reinforced by modern human studies that showed that 5-HT₂A receptor antagonism blocks effects of lysergic acid diethylamide and psilocybin, combined with neuroimaging reports of large-scale network changes, often described as a “network reset,” helped inform the revival of psychedelic therapy research for depression and PTSD.40–42 Reduced muscarinic receptor signaling in schizophrenia, including evidence implicating M4-related pathways, was reported by Scarr and Dean43 and helped motivate renewed interest in cholinergic approaches. Xanomeline, a muscarinic receptor agonist that was originally investigated in AD in the context of cholinergic theories of cognition, was later repurposed for schizophrenia. The subsequent development of xanomeline–trospium illustrates how revisiting older mechanisms with improved pharmacologic strategies can yield clinically meaningful innovation. In sleep science, discovery of orexin/hypocretin peptides as ligands for previously orphan G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and subsequent animal and human evidence that loss of orexin signaling causes narcolepsy, motivated the idea of therapeutically antagonizing the arousal-promoting orexin system to treat insomnia. Alzheimer’s disease drug development has likewise rested on decades of basic biology from identifying amyloid-β peptides in plaques to developing transgenic models and biomarker techniques that allow in vivo tracking of disease processes. The gut microbiome also represents a plausible longer-term avenue for CNS therapeutics and prevention through immune signaling, metabolic pathways, and gut-brain communication.44 Another emerging area of interest is the potential role of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in neurological and psychiatric disorders. Their metabolic and CNS effects, including possible anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective actions, make them mechanistically relevant to the CNS innovation agenda,45 although their definitive therapeutic role across specific brain disorders remains to be established. These examples indicate that translational efforts thrive on the bedrock of basic science. Breakthroughs in CNS therapeutics do not emerge ex nihilo; they are built on years of receptor biology, neurochemistry, genomics, and disease-mechanism research, often funded by public agencies and nurtured in academic laboratories long before industry can turn them into drugs. As we celebrate a new generation of treatments, it is crucial to recognize the chain of scientific discovery that underpins each one and to continue strengthening that chain through sustained basic research investment. Notably, this basic research is mostly funded by public expenses through government research agencies and academic institutions, which assume the high-risk, long-term investigations that provide the scientific foundation for pharmaceutical innovation. Nevertheless, the downstream benefits of many publicly funded advances, including intellectual property rights, regulatory exclusivities, and substantial financial returns, may be captured disproportionately by a limited number of private corporations,46 while relatively few mechanisms ensure that the public proportionally benefits from its investment. To mitigate this imbalance, new models of collaboration and accountability are needed. For example, the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) Clinical and Translational Science Awards program and the European Union’s Horizon Europe framework illustrate how policy can sustain long-term public investment in translational and foundational research infrastructure.47,48 Various mechanisms such as tiered pricing, conditional licensing, and the reinvestment of a portion of revenues into public research or local healthcare infrastructure could help ensure that industry success also benefits the communities and public institutions that enabled it. Such approaches would promote a more equitable and sustainable cycle of innovation.

Persistent challenges in CNS drug development

Despite recent breakthroughs, CNS drug development remains fraught with difficulties, and the path from idea to approved therapy is often tortuous. Success rates in CNS drug development remain lower than in many other therapeutic areas, contributing to persistent caution in investment and development strategies.49 Several factors underlie this statistic. Placebo effects are well-documented in many CNS conditions, especially psychiatric disorders. Meta-analyses have found that placebo response rates in psychiatric trials have been rising over time, likely because of a combination of higher baseline score inflation, the enrollment of less severely ill subjects, and inducing higher patient expectancy.50 Managing the placebo effect, for example, through better patient selection, rater training, and innovative trial designs remains an ongoing challenge.51 Sequential parallel comparison designs (SPCDs) may be particularly useful in CNS disorders with a high placebo response. In simplified terms, SPCD is a multi-stage design that can reduce placebo-related noise, such as by re-randomizing placebo nonresponders in a second stage, thereby improving assay sensitivity without abandoning rigorous comparison. The goal is not always only to diminish placebo responses in patients, but to design studies that robustly quantify efficacy above this expected background. Centralized review and monitoring of clinician ratings and harmonization of endpoints across sites and regions can reduce variability and improve signal detection, particularly in large multinational trials. Relatedly, endpoint selection and measurement variability can undermine CNS trial reliability. Unlike fields with objective, physiological biomarkers (eg, blood pressure, tumor size), CNS outcomes often rely on patient self-report or clinician ratings, introducing substantial noise. This makes it harder to detect modest but meaningful drug effects. Digital biomarkers, such as continuous, objective measures captured via wearables or smartphone applications, are being developed to improve signal detection, and regulators have issued guidance on their use in clinical research.52 Trial design should also be more participant-centered. Patient and public involvement can improve recruitment, retention, acceptability of study procedures, and the relevance of patient-facing materials and outcomes, thereby increasing the likelihood that trial findings generalize to real-world care.

Translational gaps also persist at the preclinical and early clinical stages. Many promising therapies that show activity in animal models or Phase I human studies fail to translate into success in Phase II or III trials, a phenomenon often termed the “valley of death” in drug development.53 Complex human CNS diseases are often not predicted well by existing animal models, and small Phase I trials that focus on safety or biomarkers may not reveal the nuances of efficacy needed to design successful larger trials. For example, a novel compound might have encouraging biomarker effects in Phase I healthy volunteers yet still fail to produce clinical benefit in the patient population due to unanticipated factors like disease heterogeneity or suboptimal dosing. Bridging positive Phase I findings into Phase II and especially Phase III success remains difficult. Improvements in trial design and patient selection can help, such as incorporating adaptive dose-finding, enrolling biomarker-defined patient subgroups that are more likely to respond and using adaptive “go/no-go” criteria54 to decide early whether to advance to a costly Phase III trial. Enhancing collaboration between academia and industry at this early stage is also critical; often academic investigators have deep disease expertise that can inform early-phase trial design. Investigational New Drug (IND) enabling studies comprise the package of nonclinical work required to support first-in-human testing, including pharmacology, toxicology, safety pharmacology, and related studies that are needed for initial regulatory submission and human exposure. Early-stage CNS development, including IND-enabling studies and first-in-human trials, is frequently conducted by smaller biotechnology companies supported by venture capital and other external financing. Moreover, more rigorous preclinical de-risking, including stronger target validation, assay design, medicinal chemistry, and preclinical pharmacology, may help reduce later-stage attrition.55

Beyond scientific hurdles, regulatory and economic factors present additional challenges. Drug regulators historically have been very cautious in approving CNS drugs, given the brain’s complexity and past safety issues. Some observers argue that regulators have at times been overly conservative; for instance, requiring multiple large trials for psychiatric indications and being slow to accept novel endpoints or surrogate biomarkers as evidence of efficacy. This cautious approach may be beginning to soften in light of unmet needs. The FDA’s recent willingness to grant accelerated approval for drugs for AD based on a surrogate biomarker (amyloid reduction) and to designate psychedelic therapies as Breakthrough Therapies suggests an openness to regulatory innovation when faced with serious illnesses. Moreover, the FDA has also published draft guidance outlining general considerations for clinical investigations of psychedelic drugs, including issues related to trial design, functional unblinding, and interpretation of efficacy.56 Another issue is that clinical trials often diverge from real-world practice, limiting the applicability of results. Trials tend to enroll highly selected patients by excluding those with comorbidities and concomitant medications and using rigid protocols, whereas clinicians treat a broad, heterogeneous population in flexible ways. This efficacy-effectiveness gap means a drug that has been proven efficacious in a trial may perform differently in routine care; conversely, some potentially useful drugs might fail in Phase III trials due to impractical trial conditions rather than true lack of efficacy. Large healthcare datasets can also help bridge the efficacy–effectiveness gap that often emerges after conventional randomized trials. Electronic health records, claims databases, and registries permit evaluations of longer-term outcomes, treatment heterogeneity, adherence, and safety signals in broader and more comorbid populations than are typically enrolled in randomized controlled trials. When analyzed with robust causal-inference methods and linked, where appropriate, to biomarker or biobank data, such resources can complement trial evidence and help identify which patients benefit most, which patients are at higher risk, and how therapies perform in routine practice.

Finally, the high cost and risk of CNS research and development have, over the past decade, driven some major pharmaceutical companies to scale back or even abandon neuroscience programs.57 The combination of expensive, lengthy trials, a high likelihood of failure, and uncertainties in disease mechanisms led to a period of disinvestment just when science was opening new doors. This retreat by several large companies created a funding gap that was partially filled by smaller biotechnology firms and academic spin-offs, which have spearheaded many recent innovations including the resurgence of psychedelic drug research. With several high-profile successes in hand, there are signs that industry interest in CNS is reviving. Nonetheless, sustaining a robust pipeline will require continued willingness to tackle these challenges through creative approaches, as well as public-sector and philanthropic support to underwrite high-risk, high-reward projects that industry might deem too risky.

In recent years, to solve this huge gap in drug discovery, governmental agencies such as the U.S. NIH, the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR), the European Union’s Horizon Europe program, and Japan Agency for Medical Research and Development (AMED), have started to play a central role in supporting early-stage clinical development. Through mechanisms like the NIH Small Business Innovation Research and Small Business Technology Transfer programs,58 start-ups and biotechnology companies receive direct funding to conduct Phase I and Phase II trials, thereby reducing early financial risk and enabling the translation of academic discoveries into human studies.59 Similarly, the CIHR and the Accelerating Clinical Trials–Accélérer les Essais Cliniques in Canada provide targeted grants to academic investigators and emerging Canadian companies to initiate proof-of-concept and early clinical trials.60 In Europe, Horizon Europe funds academia–industry consortia that undertake first-in-human and early efficacy trials across a broad range of therapeutic areas, fostering collaborations between universities, research hospitals, and biotechnology companies.48 In Japan, AMED supports translational and clinical research through initiatives such as its Core Center Program, which funds hospitals to conduct high-quality early-phase clinical trials and bridge basic science.61 By directly financing early-stage clinical development, these agencies help bridge the so-called “valley of death” in drug development, where promising innovations often stall because of lack of private investment. In recent years, several philanthropic organizations have emerged as key sponsors of clinical trials in rare neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders, particularly in conditions where no approved therapies exist and where pharmaceutical investment remains limited because of low prevalence. For example, the FRAXA Research Foundation (United States and Canada) has provided sustained funding for early-phase clinical trials in Fragile X syndrome, supporting such compounds as metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 antagonists and GABA receptor agonists that were initially overlooked by large pharmaceutical companies.62 Autism Speaks has supported clinical trials to test pharmacological interventions and behavioral treatments for core and associated symptoms of autism spectrum disorder, including early-phase studies of oxytocin, cannabivarin and other molecules. These organizations provide financial support and facilitate patient recruitment, establish trial networks, and engage directly with regulatory agencies to accelerate therapeutic development. Collectively, they have become indispensable in bridging the translational gap for rare brain and mental disorders where unmet clinical needs remain profound.

The way forward: strategies to accelerate innovation

Strengthening collaboration and funding for innovation

To capitalize on the momentum of recent breakthroughs and address ongoing challenges, a concerted effort from all stakeholders is needed. Closer collaboration between academia and industry is essential, especially in early phases of drug development. Academic researchers are often the source of novel insights into brain biology and early-stage therapeutic ideas, while pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies have the resources and expertise to translate these ideas into clinically tested drugs. Initiatives that encourage academia–industry partnerships such as shared preclinical data platforms, public–private research consortia in areas like neurodegeneration or psychiatric illness, and the co-funding of early trials can reduce risks in innovation and accelerate the journey from bench to bedside. Open-data platforms, shared precompetitive resources, and co-funded early translational programs can strengthen academia–industry collaboration while reducing duplication and fragmentation. Such models can improve target validation, facilitate early-phase testing, and ensure that both positive and negative findings contribute to cumulative progress. Examples include the Structural Genomics Consortium, which has advanced an open-science model for precompetitive target biology and chemical-probe development,63 and the Innovative Medicines Initiative in Europe, which has supported large-scale public–private collaborations to overcome bottlenecks in drug development.64 Dedicated funding streams for systematic drug repurposing, particularly for off-patent and cross-indication candidates, should be established to unlock near-term, lower-cost therapeutic opportunities. Many of the past decade’s breakthroughs originated from academic discoveries that were later picked up by companies; formalizing these pathways could yield more “low-hanging fruit” from basic science. In particular, involving academic experts in Phase I/II trials of novel compounds through academic clinical research centers or hybrid networks can improve study design and signal detection, helping promising candidates survive the transition to Phase III. Enhanced early collaboration also means sharing data on failed compounds, so that the wider community can learn from setbacks and avoid redundant efforts.

These programs have begun to bear fruit in the form of new research tools and resources (eg, technologies for circuit mapping and cell-atlas building; large-scale, precompetitive data resources) and potential therapeutic targets. We encourage more funding agencies and foundations worldwide to support such collaborative mega-projects in neuropsychopharmacology. Large-scale grants can create shared infrastructure, such as biobanks, longitudinal patient databases, and preclinical model repositories, that individual investigators or small companies would struggle to build alone. They also enable multidisciplinary teams consisting of neuroscientists, chemists, artificial intelligence (AI) specialists, and clinicians to work together on ambitious goals like curing schizophrenia or preventing dementia. Some of the past decade’s progress was accelerated by initiatives like the NIH Accelerating Medicines Partnership (eg, AMP-AD; AMP-SCZ) and the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), funded by government and charity grants. These examples show what is possible when resources are pooled at scale. Still, mental and neurological health research remains underfunded relative to its burden. Governments spend an average of around 2% of health budgets on mental health, underscoring the need for greater global investment. We call on additional funding agencies and novel models like venture philanthropy and global health funds to join the effort in supporting CNS innovation. With sustained and diversified funding, more bold ideas, including those that may not have immediate commercial return, can be tested and developed. This will ensure a healthy pipeline of new therapies for the future.

Regulatory reform and smarter trial design

Regulatory agencies play a pivotal role in shaping the pace of drug development. We encourage regulatory reform aimed at modernizing how CNS therapies are evaluated and approved. Regulators should continue becoming more flexible in trial requirements and endpoint selection, in light of advances in science and the serious unmet needs in this field. Recent examples provide hope. The FDA first granted accelerated approval to lecanemab based on a surrogate biomarker (amyloid reduction) on January 6, 2023, and then converted it to traditional approval on July 6, 2023, underscoring a willingness to use biomarker-based pathways when appropriate. Likewise, esketamine was approved with a strict Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy that requires in-clinic administration under direct supervision and at least 2 h of monitoring. As of the January 2025 U.S. label, esketamine is indicated for TRD as monotherapy or in conjunction with an oral antidepressant. In a similar manner, Australian authorities began allowing controlled clinical use of MDMA for PTSD and psilocybin for TRD by authorized psychiatrists under strict safeguards starting July 1, 2023. Building on this momentum, we advocate several concrete steps:

Adaptive and innovative trial designs

Regulators should encourage the use of adaptive trial designs in CNS drug development. Adaptive trials, which allow pre-planned modifications to the trial based on interim data, can identify effective doses or patient subgroups more efficiently than fixed designs. Seamless Phase II/III trials, platform trials testing multiple therapies in parallel, and other novel designs could accelerate development while maintaining rigor. For instance, in neurodegenerative diseases, platform trials have been used to concurrently test multiple drug candidates against a shared control group, thereby accelerating the discovery of effective treatments. Such approaches could be expanded in psychiatry as well. Regulators can provide clear guidance on using these designs and on accepting their results for approval, which would incentivize sponsors to adopt them. We also support master protocols that coordinate multiple sub-studies under a unified infrastructure. Where appropriate, prospectively planned interim analyses should be conducted under strict control of type I errors to enable early, data-driven decisions without compromising statistical integrity.

Novel endpoints and digital measures

Traditional clinical endpoints such as rating scales or cognitive tests can be subjective. We support the use and validation of digital biomarkers and real-world endpoints that align with regulatory guidance on clinical outcome assessments and patient-focused drug development. Wearables and smartphones can continuously capture activity, sleep, heart-rate variability, speech, and mobility; these may provide useful signals related to mental states or neurological function. However, caution is warranted, as some initiatives may overinterpret the ability of AI to infer complex clinical states from limited wearable data alone. In schizophrenia, the passive smartphone monitoring of mobility and speech has shown potential for relapse detection.65 Many such measures remain experimental, and their generalizability is under active study. Regulatory engagement is advancing. The FDA issued final guidance in 2023 on digital health technologies for remote data acquisition,52 while the European Medicines Agency (EMA) has qualified a wearable-based gait endpoint66 and launched a clinical study data pilot (formerly the raw-data pilot).67 The broader use of patient-reported outcomes and functional measures, consistent with FDA guidance68 and with evolving EMA regulatory practice, would help ensure that new drugs are judged by outcomes that are meaningful to patients. Biomarker-based endpoints such as PET imaging or electroencephalogram could also serve as supportive evidence when validated for a defined context of use. Many CNS disorders are multi-causal and unfold over time, such that medication alone may be necessary but insufficient. Future development programs should thus be more willing to test multimodal strategies, including pharmacological, behavioral, sleep, and physical-activity interventions. Pragmatic, adaptive, and platform designs may be particularly useful for evaluating such combinations more efficiently than traditional one-drug-versus-placebo frameworks.

Accelerated and conditional approvals

Given the high unmet need in many CNS disorders, regulators should continue to leverage expedited pathways to bring promising drugs to patients sooner. In the United States, the Breakthrough Therapy designation provides intensive FDA guidance and priority review for drugs that show substantial improvement over existing therapies. Accelerated Approval allows early market entry based on surrogate or intermediate endpoints reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit, with confirmatory trials required post-approval. In the European Union, Conditional Marketing Authorization permits approval of medicines with an incomplete clinical dataset when the public health need is urgent, contingent upon the sponsor providing comprehensive data after launch. When early evidence suggests substantial benefit or a first-in-class mechanism, approval based on a surrogate endpoint or a smaller clinical dataset with a requirement for rigorous post-marketing confirmatory studies may be appropriate. This approach has already been applied for the CNS; for example, aducanumab (2021) and lecanemab (2023) received US accelerated approval for AD based on a reduction of amyloid-β plaque burden. In Europe, conditional marketing authorizations have been granted for some therapies in neurodegenerative and rare diseases, subject to annual renewal and specific post-authorization obligations. We caution that safety must remain paramount, but smart, transparent risk–benefit judgments can prevent potentially effective treatments from languishing in development for years. Close dialogue between regulators, researchers, and patient advocacy groups is needed to align on acceptable uncertainty levels in exchange for faster access to life-changing therapies.

Over the past 2 decades, numerous drugs have failed in large international Phase III clinical trials. Contributing factors include high placebo response rates,69 methodological limitations, and cultural and regional variations in trial conduct.70 Differences in patient expectations, symptom reporting, site-related factors, and rater calibration may introduce systematic variability and obscure true drug effects.71 A preferable response is to improve assay sensitivity prospectively through stronger rater training, centralized review, endpoint harmonization, and pre-specified quality-control procedures, while recognizing that these remain evolving rather than universally standardized practices. Prospectively planned heterogeneity-of-treatment-effect analyses may also help identify whether treatment effects vary meaningfully across patient groups or trial contexts. Although exclusion of sites with clear and prospectively defined methodological deficiencies may be considered in some cases, this approach should remain exceptional and be interpreted with caution.

Overall, a more innovation-friendly regulatory environment can reduce cost and time without sacrificing rigor. By modernizing trial-design guidance, endorsing novel endpoint strategies, and exercising flexibility in approval decisions, regulators will help remove historical roadblocks in CNS drug development. The past decade’s milestones from psychedelics moving into late-phase therapy to biomarkers that guide AD approvals signal a shift in regulatory mindsets. We urge that this spirit of reform continue, in step with scientific advances, so effective treatments reach patients as swiftly as possible.

Harnessing big data and digital health

The explosion of big data in biomedicine provides an unprecedented opportunity to advance neuropsychopharmacology. Large-scale data collection through nationwide health records, insurance databases, research cohorts, and direct-to-consumer digital devices can be leveraged to glean insights that traditional trials or laboratory experiments might miss. We see several avenues where big data can catalyze progress:

Real-world evidence

Mining large healthcare databases can reveal real-world treatment outcomes, safety signals, and usage patterns at scale. For example, analyses of electronic health records or insurance claims across millions of patients can identify off-label medication effects, which could lead to potential drug repurposing opportunities, or long-term outcomes that would be infeasible to study in a prospective trial. Real-world evidence studies have already informed regulatory decisions in other fields and are increasingly used in psychiatry and neurology to supplement trial data.72,73 By linking such clinical data with biobank information (eg, genetics or laboratory results), researchers can also explore predictors of treatment response in heterogeneous populations.74 To fully exploit RWE, we encourage initiatives to improve data quality, interoperability, and methods for causal inference from observational data. Adopting common data models and interoperability standards across electronic health records and registries will further harmonize data and enhance reproducibility, enabling robust multi-site RWE analyses.

Genomic and biomarker consortia

Large datasets in genomics, proteomics, and imaging are illuminating the underpinnings of CNS disorders. The past decade saw the rise of international consortia that amassed DNA and biomarker data from hundreds of thousands of individuals, yielding dramatic discoveries. For instance, the PGC identified 287 loci associated with schizophrenia in a genome-wide association study of 76 755 cases and 243 649 controls.75 The SCHEMA exome consortium implicated ultra-rare coding variants in 10 genes that confer substantial schizophrenia risk.76 Likewise, exome sequencing consortia (eg, SCHEMA) pinpointed rare gene mutations that confer high risk for disorders like schizophrenia. Together with complementary preclinical resources, including post-mortem human brain atlases, patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell neuronal/glial models, and cross-species animal datasets, these findings provide a trove of targets and pathways for drug discovery. Integrating these preclinical datasets with large human genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and imaging resources using AI or machine learning can identify disease-relevant molecular signatures and prioritize targetable pathways. Genetics is also revealing overlaps between disorders, which could lead to transdiagnostic treatments rather than our traditional siloed approach. To translate big data into therapeutics, we propose continued support for such consortia and the creation of open-data platforms where scientists can query these rich datasets. A lifespan perspective is especially important in CNS drug development. Too often, interventions are tested only after disorders are clinically manifest, using datasets and trial horizons that are too short and fragmented to capture how brain disorders emerge and evolve over time. Longitudinal cohorts, registries, and biobanks can help identify when, in whom, and along which trajectory an intervention is most likely to alter disease course meaningfully. Moreover, incorporating genetic or biomarker stratification into clinical trials can improve their power. Precision psychiatry and neurology, enabled by large datasets, will help ensure that the right patients receive the right treatment.

Digital health and remote monitoring

The ubiquity of smartphones and wearable sensors means we can collect continuous real-world data on patients’ physiology and behavior. These digital phenotyping approaches can augment both clinical trials and clinical care. For instance, wearable actigraphs and sleep trackers are being used to monitor activity and sleep patterns in depression or PTSD trials, providing objective readouts of symptoms like psychomotor retardation or insomnia that previously relied on subjective reports. In neurological disorders, wearable gait and tremor sensors can quantify Parkinson’s disease severity or multiple sclerosis relapse frequency in between clinic visits. Such data, when aggregated over large populations, help identify subtle treatment benefits or early warning signs of disease exacerbation. Digital platforms also facilitate large-scale remote clinical trials in which patients can be enrolled online and contribute data from home, vastly increasing reach and representativeness. The COVID-19 pandemic spurred a surge in telemedicine and remote assessment, which is likely to persist. Embracing these tools can reduce trial costs and patient burden, improve enrollment and retention, and ultimately yield more data to inform drug development. At the same time, big data-enabled research changes not only scale but also the ethical and governance landscape. Progress will depend on frameworks that protect privacy, ensure transparency, and maintain public trust while still enabling socially valuable research. In jurisdictions that are governed by data-protection regimes such as the General Data Protection Regulation,77 key issues include lawful bases for data use, data minimization, proportionality, secure linkage, the governance of secondary use, and clear accountability for cross-institutional data sharing. In this sense, ethics and data protection should not be viewed only as constraints78; when implemented well, they can strengthen scientific legitimacy, improve research quality, and support durable public trust in large-scale CNS data science. We foresee a future where big data is tightly integrated with hypothesis-driven research. For example, AI algorithms might analyze smartwatch and phone data to predict which patients are responding to a new antidepressant within days of starting it, allowing trials to adapt in real time.79

In summary, the big data revolution, spanning genomic consortia, real-world clinical data, and digital health, is poised to greatly enhance CNS drug development. By integrating these massive datasets, we could discover new targets, stratify patient populations, and monitor outcomes on a scale never before possible. We recommend that stakeholders in neuropsychopharmacology continue to build data-sharing networks and analytical tools to fully harness this potential. Science and medicine are entering an era where quantity of data, coupled with quality, can drive qualitative leaps in understanding and treating brain disorders.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning in drug discovery

Complementing the big data trends, AI and machine learning have rapidly become transformative tools in drug discovery and development. In the realm of CNS disorders with their complex biology and lengthy trials, AI offers ways to accelerate and optimize many steps of the pipeline. A few key contributions include:

Target identification and drug design

Artificial intelligence algorithms can sift through enormous datasets to identify novel drug targets or suggest drug repurposing opportunities. For example, AI-driven analyses of gene expression and protein networks have suggested new molecular pathways for depression and chronic pain that researchers might otherwise overlook.80 Once a target is chosen, generative AI models can then design new molecules with desired properties such as receptor-binding profiles and brain penetrance in silico. Notably, in the past few years several drug candidates that were generated by AI methods have entered clinical trials.81 Estimates of the number of AI-designed candidates in human testing vary by definition, reflecting a still-small, fast-moving field. A recent industry-authored analysis reported Phase I “success” rates of 80%-90% for AI-discovered molecules but only on limited samples and with variable endpoints; Phase II success (approximately 40%) approximated historical norms.82 As of March 13, 2026, no AI-designed drug has been approved. Altogether, these data suggest early promise in hit quality and design efficiency, with pivotal evidence still pending.

Optimizing clinical trials

Artificial intelligence and machine learning can also refine the clinical trial process.83 Predictive algorithms can help identify patients who are most likely to respond to a given treatment or those at high risk for adverse effects, enabling more efficient trial enrollment and a precision-medicine approach. Machine learning models that are fed by baseline patient data such as neuroimaging, molecular data, and symptom profiles, have shown promise in predicting antidepressant response.84 This approach could be used to enrich trials with likely responders and thus demonstrate efficacy with smaller sample sizes. Artificial intelligence can assist in trial monitoring as well. Natural language processing can flag anomalies in clinician notes or patient reports, and adaptive algorithms can adjust dosing or stratification on the fly within adaptive trial frameworks. Moreover, advanced statistics and machine learning can extract signals from noisy data. These approaches are still nascent, but regulatory agencies are exploring ways to qualify AI-driven tools in drug development. Over time, AI may even help solve problems like the placebo effect by identifying which participants are likely placebo responders possibly via digital phenotyping and accounting for that in trial design or analysis.

Drug repurposing

Another valuable application of AI is in systematic drug repurposing. Algorithms can analyze vast chemical and clinical databases to predict new uses for old drugs. For example, AI models have been used to scan patterns in gene expression changes that are caused by various compounds and match them to disease signatures, yielding hypotheses that a drug approved for hypertension might influence pathways that are involved in, for example, AD or autism. Given the high costs of new drug development, repurposing known safe drugs is an attractive strategy to bring treatments to patients faster. Drug repurposing can shorten timelines and reduce cost, but it should not be treated as a shortcut around evidentiary rigor. Repurposing should be supported by disciplined trials, transparent methods, and fair-access planning, particularly when off-label enthusiasm or re-commercialization of older molecules risks amplifying inequity. There have already been notable repurposing successes identified through data-driven approaches. While such resources like Clue.io demonstrate the feasibility of systematic drug repurposing, the further expansion and integration of such computational platforms with experimental validation remain critical to fully maximize the therapeutic value of existing molecules.85 Notably, ketamine’s use in depression is essentially a repurposing of an anesthetic drug to a new domain, and many other medications likely have potential utility in psychiatric or neurological conditions. Artificial intelligence can help uncover these opportunities more systematically.

Of course, alongside its promise, AI introduces challenges around interpretability, data bias, and the need for robust validation. Operationally, sponsors should implement drift monitoring to detect and mitigate data and model drift over time, coupled with human-in-the-loop oversight and pre-specified criteria for model updating or retraining. It is crucial that AI-derived predictions be tested rigorously in the laboratory and clinic because the human brain is an immensely complex system and not every pattern that is spotted by a computer will translate to a viable therapy. There are also regulatory considerations for AI-designed drugs such as intellectual property questions and quality control in synthesis. Nonetheless, the trajectory is clear: AI is becoming an indispensable ally in our quest to understand and treat CNS disorders. We recommend ongoing investment in AI research specific to neuropsychiatric drug development, cross-training of neuroscientists and data scientists, and the establishment of regulatory frameworks that can accommodate AI-driven innovations. If responsibly developed and applied, AI has the potential to greatly shorten the timeline from disease hypothesis to effective treatment, which would be a welcome paradigm shift in neuropsychopharmacology.

Toward precision medicine: genetics and personalized therapies

Brain disorders have historically been treated with a one-size-fits-all approach; however, growing knowledge of genetic and biological heterogeneity is paving the way for precision medicine in psychiatry and neurology. Large genomic studies are revealing genetic variants that influence disease risk and treatment response. For example, common polymorphisms in genes affecting drug metabolism like certain CYP450 enzymes or in drug targets such as various neurotransmitter receptor subtypes can modulate how patients respond to psychotropic drugs. Pharmacogenetic tests are already clinically available to guide medication selection for depression in some cases (eg, choosing or avoiding a certain antidepressant based on a patient’s CYP2C19 genotype to predict metabolic speed). Thus, interindividual variability in CNS pharmacokinetics remains a key determinant of later-stage trial outcomes and should be integrated more explicitly into precision-medicine development. Beyond pharmacokinetics, polygenic risk scores derived from genome-wide data might soon help identify individuals at high risk for illnesses like schizophrenia or AD, who could be prioritized for early intervention trials or preventive measures. We have also learned from genetics that a number of psychiatric diagnoses overlap in their biology, suggesting that traditional diagnostic categories need revision. For instance, genetic studies show a substantial overlap of risk genes between schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, and between depression and anxiety.86 This implies that a drug targeting a particular biological pathway could benefit patients across what we now label as distinct disorders, a concept that reinforces moving toward a neuroscience-based classification rather than strictly symptom-based labels.

To harness genetic information in drug development, several steps can be taken. First, biomarker stratification can be incorporated into clinical trials. For example, testing a new glutamate-based drug preferentially in patients with certain glutamate receptor gene variants, if data suggest those variants confer sensitivity to the treatment. This could increase the drug’s apparent efficacy and lead to an approval for a biomarker-defined subgroup, which can later be expanded if broader efficacy is seen. Second, genetics can be used to de-risk targets. If human genetics strongly implicate a protein in disease, then investing in that target has a higher chance of success. Drug programs that align with genetic evidence, so-called “gene-to-drug” development, should be prioritized. One example is schizophrenia genetics identifying the synaptic protein C4A in complement signaling,87 which led to new hypotheses about excessive synaptic pruning in schizophrenia, illustrating how genetics can open entirely new therapeutic avenues. Furthermore, beyond inherited DNA sequence variation, epigenetic regulation and gene–environment interaction may help clarify when, where, and in whom genetic targets matter.88 Although many such signals remain immature for direct therapeutic translation, they may become increasingly important for trajectory-based and cell-type-informed intervention strategies.89 Third, treatments can be tailored to patient sub-populations. In conditions like epilepsy or autism (which are collections of diverse syndromes), a treatment that is effective for one genetic subtype may not work for another. With cheaper and faster DNA sequencing, it is becoming feasible to identify patients’ specific mutations and enroll them in targeted trials. Regulators have, in some circumstances, approved therapies for ultra-rare genetic conditions based on small studies when the mechanistic rationale is strong and the unmet need is substantial. For example, eladocagene exuparvovec for aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase deficiency was authorized in Europe based on evidence from a small patient population.90 We foresee a future where n-of-1 trials and personalized therapeutics such as gene therapies or antisense oligonucleotides tailored to a patient’s unique mutation become more routine in neurology and psychiatry, especially as gene editing and RNA-based methods mature.

Crucially, achieving precision medicine in CNS disorders will require collaboration across research centers and countries to gather a sufficient number of patients with specific biomarkers or genotypes. Global initiatives like the PGC have already set the stage by uniting researchers from more than 30 countries.91 Continued support for such collaborations, along with data-sharing and biobanking frameworks, will be needed to power the next generation of trials. We also need education for clinicians and investigators to become literate in genomics and personalized approaches, as well as engagement with patients about the benefits and limits of personalized prediction in mental health to manage expectations and ethical considerations. If done thoughtfully, using genetic and biomarker information will not only improve success rates in drug development by targeting therapies to those who are most likely to benefit, but also spare patients from trial-and-error prescriptions and undue side effects. This aligns with the broader trend in medicine of moving from reactive treatment to proactive and tailored care—a shift that is desperately needed in fields like psychiatry, where current treatments are effective for some patients but fail for many others.

Embracing neuroscience-based nomenclature

As we innovate in treatments and understanding of brain disorders, it is also time to modernize the way we name and classify psychotropic medications. The traditional nomenclature of psychiatric drugs using terms like antidepressant, antipsychotic, and mood stabilizer, is rooted in historical convention, which often reflects the first condition a drug was used to treat, rather than the drug’s mechanism of action. This can be misleading and may hinder optimal clinical use of new therapies that defy old categories. For example, “antidepressants” are used not only for depression but also for anxiety disorders. This is also true for “antipsychotics,” some of which have actually been indicated for bipolar disorder and TRD. This discrepancy between their terminologies and indications often confuses patients and their caregivers, which often lead to a misunderstanding of the intrinsic biological effects of prescribed medications. In recognition of these issues, experts from various organizations including the European College of Neuropsychopharmacology, CINP, the American College of Neuropsychopharmacology, AsCNP, and the International Union of Basic and Clinical Pharmacology have spearheaded an initiative to adopt NbN.92 Neuroscience-based Nomenclature proposes classifying drugs by their pharmacological profile and mode of action rather than by clinical indication alone. This approach can provide a clearer understanding of how a drug works, encourage rational pharmacotherapy using drugs with complementary mechanisms, and reduce stigma by avoiding labels that imply a drug is only for a particular disorder. Progress has been made; updated nomenclature has been published for many psychotropic medications,93 but widespread adoption requires buy-in from academia, clinicians, journals, and regulatory bodies. We support the continued refinement and teaching of NbN, as it will better align language with science and facilitate the integration of new therapies that blur the conventional lines between psychiatric and neurological drugs.

Ensuring global access to new therapies

A final but crucial theme in plotting the future of neuropsychopharmacology is global access. Advances over the past decade will mean little if they do not reach the vast majority of people who need them, including those in low- and middle-income countries and other underserved populations. Unfortunately, stark disparities persist. The World Health Organization reports that around 71% of people with psychosis worldwide do not receive mental health services, with coverage of approximately 70% in high-income settings versus 12% in low-income settings. Even in high-income countries only about one-third of people with depression receive formal care, and minimally adequate treatment is estimated to be 23% in high-income versus 3% in low-/lower-middle-income countries.94,95 These statistics underscore that innovation must be paired with dissemination.

Several barriers contribute to the global access gap. The high cost of new medications, such as monoclonal antibodies for AD or novel psychotropic drugs, makes them extremely expensive and often unaffordable for many health systems and patients. For example, despite the success of orexin antagonists for insomnia, cannabidiol for epilepsy, and guanfacine for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), these drugs often remain expensive and, in some countries, are not covered by public health insurance. In addition, precision medicine may improve response rates, but it can also increase cost, service complexity, and inequity if reimbursement and delivery systems are not aligned. Additionally, inadequate healthcare infrastructure and a shortage of specialist workforce limit the delivery of these treatments, particularly in rural areas and low-resource settings. Furthermore, regulatory and policy hurdles, including slow drug approval processes and the absence of psychiatric drugs from essential medicines lists in some countries, also restrict access. Stigma and lack of awareness can likewise prevent uptake of even available treatments.

To address these issues, a multipronged strategy is required. First, international and national policymakers should work to include proven new therapies in essential medicine programs and negotiate pricing or generic manufacturing to make them affordable. For instance, when a breakthrough drug’s patent expires (or via voluntary licensing agreements), generic production should be facilitated to drive costs down, as was done for human immunodeficiency virus medications in the early 2000s. Public–private partnerships could be established to subsidize costly biologics like anti-amyloid immunotherapies for AD in low-income regions, perhaps via global health funding mechanisms. Moreover, managed access agreements, coverage with evidence development, and similar policy mechanisms may help reconcile earlier access with continued evidence generation, especially for high-cost therapies or biomarker-defined subgroups. Second, healthcare system strengthening is needed. Training more mental health professionals, integrating psychiatric treatment into primary care, and deploying tele-enabled mental health can extend the reach of new therapies. Scientific innovation alone is insufficient unless delivery systems, specialist workforce, infrastructure, monitoring requirements, and equitable access are considered from early stages of development. Increasingly, CNS therapeutics require more than a prescription, and implementation planning must be treated as part of development rather than an afterthought. Third, continued advocacy and education are vital so that patients and families worldwide understand that effective treatments exist and demand access to them, while combating the stigma that often surrounds mental illness. The global mental health community should proactively ensure that knowledge about new treatments is disseminated through open-access publications and international conferences that include representation from low-income countries.

There is also an ethical imperative to include diverse populations in research studies from the outset. Many clinical trials for new CNS drugs have been concentrated in North America, Europe, and East Asia. The broader inclusion of trial sites in regions like Africa, South Asia, and Latin America would address fairness and gather data on efficacy and safety in different genetic and cultural backgrounds, ensuring that treatments are truly generalizable. Such inclusion helps preempt scenarios where a drug is approved but there are little data on how it works in under-represented populations, which can make regulators elsewhere hesitant to approve or adopt it. Global collaboration in research, perhaps via networks that are enabled by large grants, as mentioned above, will lay the groundwork for smoother global roll-out of successful therapies.

In conclusion on this point, making neuropsychiatric breakthroughs accessible globally will require as much innovation in policy and implementation as in science. The past decade’s advances give us powerful new tools, but also highlight inequities. For example, while one country may offer psychedelic therapy, another country might not even have basic serotonin reuptake inhibitors widely available. Bridging this gap is a grand challenge for the next decade. Organizations like the WHO have called for a transformation in mental health care, and meeting those calls will involve scaling up resources, securing political commitment, and perhaps rethinking funding allocations. Bridging psychiatry and neurology is not only a scientific agenda but a humanitarian one. Ensuring global access to treatments so that millions of people who suffer from brain disorders, wherever they live, can benefit from the “decade of breakthroughs” and those yet to come. Our position is that no innovation should be deemed a true success until it reaches those who need it most, worldwide.

Conclusion

The past 10 years have revitalized optimism in CNS drug development, showcasing how perseverance in science and a willingness to explore uncharted mechanisms can yield major advances. Patients with illnesses that were once deemed intractable such as refractory depression, PTSD, schizophrenia, and AD have seen genuinely new therapies become available or appear on the near horizon. The convergence of psychiatry and neurology, exemplified by treatments that target overlapping symptom domains and biological pathways, reflects a broader truth. Disorders of the mind and brain are deeply intertwined, and progress will come from unifying knowledge across disciplines rather than maintaining artificial divides. To maintain this forward trajectory, the field must learn from both successes and failures. Every setback such as a failed trial or an abandoned target provides lessons about the complexity of neurobiology and the pitfalls in our methods. Likewise, every success such as a new drug approval or a novel discovery provides a template of how to do things right. By fostering collaboration, embracing smart regulation, investing in data and technology, and continually feeding the engine of discovery, we can accelerate the translation of neuroscience into life-changing therapies. The “decade of breakthroughs” in CNS drug development can serve as a springboard for even more transformative progress in the years to come, offering hope to millions of people worldwide who are affected by brain disorders. Academia, for its part, reaffirms its commitment to bridging the worlds of psychiatry and neurology, supporting innovative research, and advocating for policies that turn scientific advances into better mental health for all. The journey is far from over; indeed, in many ways it is just beginning, but the achievements of the last decade have charted a path forward. It is now up to us, as a global community of scientists, clinicians, industry leaders, and policymakers, to follow that path with determination, wisdom, and compassion.
