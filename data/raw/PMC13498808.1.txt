Background:

Clinical outcomes continue to be a concern after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR). Lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) has reemerged as an adjunct procedure for enhancing knee function; however, its overall clinical effectiveness remains controversial.

Hypothesis:

The addition of LET to primary ACLR would result in superior clinical, functional, and patient-reported outcomes as compared with isolated ACLR.

Study Design:

Systematic review and meta-analysis; Level of evidence, 1.

Methods:

This study was conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) and registered in PROSPERO. Multiple databases were searched from 1995 to October 2025. Ten high-quality randomized controlled trials comparing ACLR with and without LET were included in the final meta-analysis. The outcomes assessed included graft failure, rotational and anterior knee stability, functional scores, patient-reported outcomes, and return-to-sport rates. Random effects models were used, and heterogeneity was evaluated.

Results:

When compared with isolated ACLR, ACLR augmented with LET demonstrated significantly lower graft failure rates (odds ratio [OR], −1.19; 95% CI, −1.73 to −0.65; P < .001) and improved rotational stability, as evidenced by superior pivot-shift outcomes (OR, −0.81; 95% CI, −1.11 to −0.52; P < .001). Significant improvements were also observed in Lysholm scores (OR, 1.98; 95% CI, 0.19-3.77; P = .030) and International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective scores (OR, 1.65; 95% CI, 0.24-3.05; P = .22). No statistically significant differences were identified between the groups in KT-1000 measurements, Lachman test results, IKDC objective scores, Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score subscales, Tegner activity scores, or return-to-sport rates. Heterogeneity was low across most outcomes (I2 = 0%), except for the Tegner scale (I2 = 83.12%).

Conclusion:

The addition of LET to primary ACLR was associated with significantly improved graft survival (log OR, −1.19; 95% CI, −1.73 to −0.65; P < .001); enhanced rotational stability as demonstrated by improved pivot-shift outcomes (log OR, −0.81; 95% CI, −1.11 to −0.52; P < .001); and better patient-reported knee function, including Lysholm scores (mean difference, 1.98; 95% CI, 0.19-3.77; P = .030) and IKDC subjective scores (mean difference, 1.65; 95% CI, 0.24-3.05; P = .22). However, no significant benefits were observed in anterior laxity or return-to-sport rates.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries remain a major source of disability among young and active individuals, and their incidence is rising with increasing participation in pivoting sports. 15 ACL reconstruction (ACLR) is widely regarded as the gold standard treatment to restore knee stability and facilitate return to sport; however, concerns persist regarding residual rotatory laxity, particularly in young patients with knee instability, in whom lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) has been explored as an adjunct procedure. 14 Persistent pivot-shift phenomena after ACLR have been linked to inferior functional outcomes, higher rates of subsequent meniscal and chondral pathology, and increased risk of graft failure.22,39 These concerns have renewed interest in the structures contributing to rotational stability, particularly the anterolateral complex.

The anterolateral complex includes ligamentous, capsular, and fascial structures that limit internal tibial rotation and provide secondary restraint to the ACL.6,18 Before modern arthroscopic reconstruction techniques became the standard, early surgical eras incorporated lateral extra-articular procedures as an adjunct to stabilize the knee.34,42 Although initial enthusiasm has diminished owing to concerns regarding overconstraint and inconsistent long-term results, 25 LET has regained clinical interest. Biomechanical research has demonstrated that LET can reduce internal rotation, decrease anterior tibial translation, and diminish the forces experienced by the intra-articular graft,10,26,28 supporting its potential value in patients at high risk of graft failure or persistent rotatory laxity.

However, clinical evidence remains mixed. Some observational studies have reported better control of rotational instability with LET augmentation, 3 whereas others have raised concerns regarding potential overconstraint or altered loading patterns within the lateral compartment. 19 Existing meta-analyses have summarized these findings but had the limitations of methodological variability, including differences in eligibility criteria, study designs, and outcome selection. Therefore, an updated and focused synthesis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was needed to provide a more consistent and high-level assessment of LET in primary ACLR.13,21,43

Although previous meta-analyses of RCTs have been published,24,29,31 those studies included fewer RCTs and smaller pooled sample sizes, leaving gaps in the current evidence as LET gains wider adoption. Therefore, an updated and focused synthesis of RCTs is needed to clarify whether adding LET to primary ACLR leads to superior outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

The present systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses). 30 The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251070504).

Data Source and Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted from 1995 to October 1, 2025, using the following databases: MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Google Scholar. Our research strategy included the following terms for PubMed/MEDLINE: “anterior cruciate ligament” OR “ACL” AND “anterolateral ligament” OR “extraarticular” OR “extra-articular” OR “extra articular” AND “tenodesis” OR “reconstruction” OR “augmentation.” The full search strategies for all databases are presented in Appendix Table A1.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included per the following PICOS criteria:

Population: patients who underwent primary ACLR with autologous grafts for isolated ACL rupture

Intervention: ACLR with LET

Comparison: isolated ACLR (iACLR) without LET

Outcomes: knee stability, graft failure, functional, or patient-reported outcomes

Study design: RCTs

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) nonrandomized study design; (2) revision ACLR, multiligament knee injuries, or use of allografts; (3) animal studies or cadaveric models; or (4) non-English studies.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved records for eligibility. Potentially eligible studies or those with insufficient information in the abstract were retrieved for full-text review by 2 independent authors to determine final eligibility. Data were extracted independently by 2 authors using a standardized spreadsheet. Any disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion or consulting a third author, if required.

The extracted data included the first author's name, publication year, sample size, sex, mean age, follow-up duration, and surgical approach. The primary outcome was knee stability, assessed by positive rotational laxity (pivot-shift grade ≥1), positive anterior laxity (Lachman grade ≥1), and KT-1000 arthrometer side-to-side difference in anterior tibial translation. Secondary outcomes were patient-reported functional scores—including International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective score, IKDC objective grade (grades C and D were considered poor outcomes), Lysholm, Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), and Tegner score—as well as return to sport at any level, return to initial sporting level, and graft failure.

Assessment of Quality and Risk of Bias

Two reviewers independently assessed the studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool for randomized trials. 36 This tool evaluates potential bias across 5 domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported results. Each study was categorized as having low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias based on these domain-specific judgments. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or consulting a third author, if required.

Statistical Analysis

Data and results from the initial computations (eg, log odds ratios [ORs] and standard errors) in Excel spreadsheets were entered into Jamovi (Version 2.7; https://www.jamovi.org). Meta-analyses were conducted via the MAJOR module (Version 1.2.4), and the effect sizes and mean differences of menu items were determined. For categorical data, log ORs were calculated for each study, employing the Haldane-Anscombe correction to allow for frequencies of 0. For comparisons of means, raw mean differences were used. If only the medians were available, they were substituted for the means. Where ranges were given instead of standard deviations or standard errors, the standard deviation was conservatively estimated from one-fifth of the range. Homogeneity of variances was assumed, using pooled variances for standard error estimates. All analyses assumed a random effects model, and heterogeneity was estimated by restricted maximum likelihood. 40

Results

Literature Search Results

The initial database search yielded 3918 records. After removal of 2000 duplicates, 1918 studies were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 1861 were excluded because they were not relevant to the review question, leaving 57 full-text reports for eligibility assessment. After full-text review, 43 studies were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria, including incompatible study designs, inappropriate or irrelevant outcomes, and interventions that did not match the scope of the review. Eleven studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review. ‖

One study was subsequently excluded from the quantitative synthesis after basic checks of the reported statistical calculations. Therefore, 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis, ¶ as illustrated in Figure 1.

Study and Patient Characteristics

A total of 1419 patients were included across 11 comparative RCTs, with 725 in the ACLR + LET group and 694 in the iACLR group. Studies were published between 2001 and 2025 and conducted in the United States,2,5 France, 4 Egypt,9,27 India, 16 Italy,23,38 Australia, 32 Algeria, 37 and multicenter Canada/Europe cohorts.11,12,17,33 The sample size weighted mean age was 23.9 years in the ACLR + LET group and 23.4 years in the iACLR group (overall weighted mean, 23.6 years), with study-level mean ages ranging from 19.1 to 31.0 years and 18.9 to 32.0 years, respectively. The follow-up duration was heterogeneous, ranging from 3 months11,12,17,33 to 276 months, 23 with studies variably reporting fixed intervals (eg, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months),11,12,17,33 minimum follow-up thresholds (eg, ≥24 months), 9 or long-term mean follow-up4,23 (Table 1).

Meta-analysis

Meta-analyses were performed on 11 studies with adequate data. One study by Gupta et al 16 was removed because basic checks performed on their statistical calculations revealed gross errors. In almost every measure, their effect sizes were clear outliers and contributed to excessive and highly statistically significant heterogeneity. The corresponding author (S.K. Rai) was contacted to request access to the raw data, but no reply was forthcoming. Therefore, this meta-analysis proceeded with 10 studies.

Graft Failure

Seven studies reported graft failure. The mean log OR was −1.19 (mean OR, 0.30; Z = 4.34; P = .00001), indicating a benefit of the combined ACLR + LET over ACLR (see forest plot in Figure 3). There was consistency across the studies, as indicated by the lack of statistical significance for heterogeneity (tau = 0; I2 = 0; H2 = 1.0; Q = 1.018; P = .985). The funnel plot indicated an acceptable spread of standard errors for the effect size (Figure 3) and no obvious asymmetry (Egger regression, −0.472; P = .637). The Rosenthal fail-safe statistic was 33 (P < .0001).

Pivot Shift

Eight studies reported pivot shift. Rotational laxity with a pivot shift was significantly improved by the combined procedure of ACLR + LET as compared with ACLR alone. The mean log OR was −0.814 (mean OR, 0.44; Z = 5.45; P < .00001). There was no statistically significant heterogeneity across the studies (tau = 0; I2 = 0; H2 = 1.0; Q = 5.516; P = .597) (Figure 4). The funnel plot (Figure 4, inset) indicates an acceptable spread of standard errors and no obvious asymmetry (Egger regression, −0.917; P = .359). The Rosenthal fail-safe statistic was 61 (P < .0001).

KT-1000

Four studies provided instrumental anterior laxity test data (KT-1000) in the form of mean differences. The estimate from all studies for the mean difference was −0.217, indicating some benefit from the combined ACLR + LET surgery, although it failed to reach statistical significance (Z = 1.27; P = .205). The effects were consistently in the same direction (Figure 5), and study homogeneity was apparent (tau = 0; I2 = 0; H2 = 1.0; Q = 0.743; P = .863). The Rosenthal fail-safe statistic was 0 (P = .068). The funnel plot did not reveal any asymmetry (see Figure 5 inset) (Egger regression, −0.86; P = .39).

Lachman Test

A meta-analysis across 4 studies using the Lachman test indicated little difference between the 2 surgical procedures. The mean log OR was −0.091 (Z = 0.177; P = .916). There was consistency across the studies (tau = 0.464; I2 = 18.3; H2 = 1.22; Q = 2.58; P = .461), although the direction of one was weakly opposite that of the other 3 (Figure 6). The Rosenthal fail-safe statistic was 0 (P = .350). The funnel plot did not reveal any asymmetry (see Figure 6 inset) (Egger regression, −1.06; P = .290).

Five studies reported that a mean score of 1.98 was statistically higher for the combined ACLR + LET procedure as compared with ACLR alone (Z = 2.17; P = .030). The Rosenthal fail-safe statistic was 4 (P = .018). The funnel plot did not reveal any asymmetry (see Figure 9 inset) (Egger regression, −0.87; P = .384).

Tegner Score

Only 3 studies reported Tegner activity scores. The ACLR + LET group demonstrated a higher Tegner score as compared with the iACLR group (mean difference, 0.617); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Z = 1.95; P = .051). For this measure, there was significant heterogeneity among the studies (tau = 0.49; I2 = 83.1; H2 = 5.9; Q = 13.33; P = .001). The Rosenthal fail-safe statistic was 48 with P < .001. The funnel plot did not reveal any asymmetry, but 2 of the 3 studies fell outside the cone (Figure 10 inset) (Egger regression, −0.41; P = .68).

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to evaluate whether the addition of LET provides superior clinical outcomes as compared with iACLR. Eleven RCTs encompassing 1419 patients were identified, and 10 studies met the inclusion criteria for the final meta-analysis. The studies had a relatively balanced distribution between treatment groups, with 725 patients in the ACLR + LET group and 694 patients in the iACLR group. The overall study population was young, with a sample size weighted mean age of 23.9 years in the ACLR + LET group and 23.4 years in the iACLR group.

Our findings indicate that the addition of LET to primary ACLR is associated with lower graft failure rates and improved postoperative rotational stability, as reflected by improved pivot-shift outcomes and higher Lysholm and IKDC subjective scores. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the KOOS outcomes, return-to-sport rates, Tegner activity scores, IKDC objective scores, Lachman test results, or KT-1000 measurements.

Our results are consistent with those of major systematic reviews24,29,43 that reported lower graft failure rates and improved rotational stability with LET augmentation. These outcomes directly reflect the primary rationale for adding LET, which is to enhance the control of anterolateral rotational instability and reduce graft stress, thereby lowering the risk of graft failure.

In contrast to our findings, Mao et al 24 reported significantly higher postoperative activity levels in patients undergoing ACLR with LET augmentation, as reflected by improved Tegner scores; however, this difference was not observed in the present analysis. Nevertheless, a trend toward higher postoperative activity levels was observed.

Furthermore, Zhang et al 43 identified a significant difference between groups in the KT-1000 measurements, whereas no such difference was detected in our study. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the study methodology and sample size.

Notably, our analysis demonstrated statistically and clinically significant improvements in the Lysholm and IKDC subjective scores. This finding contrasts those of several previously published studies20,41,43 that reported limited benefits of LET with regard to patient-reported outcome measures. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the available literature is largely composed of nonrandomized studies, which may be subject to heterogeneous follow-up durations, selection bias, and recall bias, particularly when assessing subjective outcome measures. In this context, our findings suggest that the impact of LET on patient-perceived knee function may have been previously underappreciated. These results highlight the potentially underrecognized benefits of LET regarding the subjective functional outcomes and represent a novel and clinically relevant contribution to the existing literature.

No statistically significant differences were identified between the groups in the KOOS subscales, return-to-sport rates, IKDC objective scores, or Lachman test results, which is consistent with the findings of prior studies.1,7,18 Clinically, the similar return-to-sport rates indicate that both treatment approaches achieve symptomatic improvement to support the resumption of athletic participation at comparable levels. Also, this finding indicates that the addition of LET may not substantially influence broader patient-perceived recovery domains, such as pain, symptoms, daily function, sport-related function, or quality of life, as measured by KOOS. Similarly, the absence of significant differences in IKDC objective scores and Lachman test results suggests that anterior stability and overall objective knee assessment may be comparable between groups.

The findings of this meta-analysis were strengthened by the inclusion of 10 RCTs, as compared with the inclusion of fewer trials in previous meta-analyses,24,29 thus expanding the available evidence substantially. Moreover, the inclusion of high-quality RCTs resulted in a larger overall sample size. Importantly, no significant heterogeneity was observed across the analyzed outcomes except for the Tegner activity score, reflecting the methodological rigor and consistency of the studies. This overall low heterogeneity, together with rigorous sensitivity analyses, reinforces the reliability and robustness of our conclusions.

Although this review focused mainly on clinical, functional, and patient-reported outcomes, the potential risks of LET should also be considered, as lateral extra-articular procedures may alter knee kinematics, increase lateral compartment loading, and potentially increase the long-term risk of osteoarthritis. 8

However, recent international expert consensus strongly supports the addition of LET in young, active patients undergoing ACLR, highlighting the importance of appropriate patient selection when balancing potential benefits and risks. 35

This study has some limitations. Variability in patient characteristics and surgical techniques among the studies may have influenced the clinical and functional outcomes, and no subgroup analysis was performed according to age, graft type, surgical technique, or patient risk profile, such as high-grade pivot shift or participation in pivoting sports.

In addition, differences in sample sizes among the studies may have affected the relative weighting of individual trials in the pooled analysis. Several studies also had a follow-up <2 years, which may have led to an underestimation of graft rerupture risk and other longer-term complications. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results and may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis of 10 RCTs demonstrated that adding LET to primary ACLR was associated with lower graft failure rates and improved control of rotational instability, as reflected by better pivot-shift outcomes. LET also showed statistically significant improvements in Lysholm and IKDC subjective scores; however, the clinical relevance of these patient-reported outcome improvements remains uncertain. Overall, these findings support the selective risk-based use of LET, particularly in young, high-demand patients at increased risk of graft failure or persistent rotational instability, rather than its routine use in all primary ACLR cases.
