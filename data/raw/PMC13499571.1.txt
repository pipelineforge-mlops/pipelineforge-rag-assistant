Objective

To explore the value of heparin-binding protein (HBP) in the diagnosis of sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) in patients with sepsis.

Methods

This retrospective single-center study enrolled 114 patients with sepsis admitted between January 2023 and January 2025. Patients were divided into SA-AKI group (n=76) and non-SA-AKI group (n=38) based on the presence of AKI. Baseline clinical data were collected for both groups. Venous blood samples were obtained at ICU admission (within 30 minutes of arrival, T0) and at 24 h (T24), 48 h (T48), and 72 h (T72) thereafter for measurement of HBP and other infection-related biomarkers. General characteristics and dynamic changes in HBP levels were compared between the two groups. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnosticvalue of each biomarker for SA-AKI.

Results

SOFA score, length of mechanical ventilation, and 28-day mortality were significantly higher in the SA-AKI group than in the non-SA-AKI group (all P < 0.05). HBP levels showed an increasing trend over time in the SA-AKI group, whereas a decreasing trend was observed in the non-SA-AKI group, with statistically significant differences between the two groups at all time points (P < 0.05). Levels of HBP, procalcitonin (PCT), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were significantly higher in the SA-AKI group compared with the non-SA-AKI group (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were found in white blood cell count or C-reactive protein levels between the two groups. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that HBP, PCT, and IL-6 were effective predictors of SA-AKI, with AUC of 0.869 (95% CI 0.806–0.933, sensitivity 73.7%, specificity 86.8%), 0.777, and 0.738, respectively.

Conclusion

HBP demonstrates high early predictive value for SA-AKI in ICU patients with sepsis, with diagnostic performance superior to that of PCTand IL-6. HBP may serve as a promising early biomarker for the diagnosis of SA-AKI.

Introduction

Sepsis is a potentially life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and is a leading cause of mortality among patients with life-threatening diseases.1,2 In addition, sepsis is a risk factor for acute kidney injury and the primary precipitating factor for sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI), a major clinical consequence of sepsis.3 It is estimated that sepsis affects approximately 19 million people worldwide each year and represents the primary cause of SA-AKI. Consequently, sepsis is also a leading cause of death in patients with AKI.4 Timely diagnostic staging and intervention in SA-AKI are therefore essential to prevent disease progression, improve patient survival, and reduce healthcare costs. Current diagnostic criteria rely on reduced urine output and elevated serum creatinine (SCr) levels, which are subject to various confounding factors and both have considerable delays.5 The 23rd Acute Disease Quality Initiative (ADQI) working group issued a consensus report highlighting that the application of biomarkers reflecting kidney damage and function to enhance the diagnostic sensitivity of SA-AKI represents a future direction.6 The role of heparin-binding protein (HBP) in the development of organ dysfunction in sepsis has been demonstrated in numerous experimental and clinical studies.7–9 As a novel infection biomarker, HBP is closely associated with the occurrence of SA-AKI. Nevertheless, the dynamic changes in HBP levels among patients with SA-AKI have not beenfullycharacterised.10 Despite accumulating evidence supporting the association between elevated HBP levels and the occurrence of SA-AKI, several critical knowledge gaps remain. Most previous studies have measured HBP at a single time point upon ICU admission or at the time of AKI diagnosis, whereas the dynamic trajectory of HBP levels during the early course of sepsis—particularly within the first 72 hours—has not been systematically characterized. Therefore, the present study monitored the dynamics of HBP levels among patients with SA-AKI over time and determined the relevance of HBP and other infection-related markers for the early detection of SA-AKI. The findings are presented below.

Participants and Methods

Participants

This retrospective study was conducted to analyse the clinical data of patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU of our hospital between January 2023 and January 2025 who met the inclusion criteria. The patients were categorised into the SA-AKI group (76 patients) and the non-SA-AKI group (38 patients) according to the presence of AKI. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) diagnosed and identified according to the Sepsis-3.0 criteria developed by the Society of Critical Care Medicine and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine in 2016;2 (b) AKI: diagnosed with the AKI criteria established by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines in 2012;11 (c) first ICU admission with a hospital stay of at least 72 h; (d) Age: ≥18 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) incomplete treatment or treatment interruption for any reason; (b) chronic kidney disease, malignant tumours, or immunodeficiency.

Informed consent: Due to the retrospective design of the study, all clinical data used for analysis were fully de-identified, and the research implementation did not interfere with the routine clinical diagnosis and treatment of patients. Based on this, the Ethics Committee approved a waiver of written informed consent (approval no.: K-2022-012-K01). All data collection, sorting, and statistical analysis procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national medical research norms to strictly protect patient privacy and confidentiality. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Municipal Hospital in Suzhou, China, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Methods Clinical Data Collection

General parameters were recorded for all 114 patients, including sex, age, site of infection, comorbidities, and SOFA scores. Serum HBP levels were collected at ICU admission (within 30 minutes of arrival, T0) and at 24 h (T24), 48 h (T48), and 72 h (T72) thereafter. All samples were processed using point-of-care testing (POCT) and centrifuged within 1 hour of collection, with plasma HBP levels measured immediately thereafter. Infection-related biomarkers, including WBC, PCT, CRP, IL-6, as well as renal function parameters (creatinine and urine output), were measured at admission. Clinical outcomes, including length of ICUstay, 28-day mortality, duration of hospitalisation, and length of mechanical ventilation, were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism10 Software and SPSS 27. Discussion

Sepsis is a multifaceted condition that is caused by an unregulated systemic inflammatory reaction to various infections. It is also associated with high mortality rates, a substantial economic burden, and a marked reduction in quality of life. Moreover, sepsis is also a contributing factor to the development of AKI. A multicentre observational study reported that the mortality rate of SA-AKI is approximately 18% and that SA-AKI is directly associated with poor prognosis in patients with sepsis.12 Therefore, early identification of SA-AKI is crucial, and timely diagnosis is a key step in improving prognosis. Consequently, the search for effective infection biomarkers has become an important focus of international research.13 Traditional biomarkers used in clinical practice are limited by time delays, low sensitivity, and poor specificity, resulting in suboptimal predictive capability for sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction.14,15

To date, HBP has been validated in numerous clinical studies worldwide as a mediator of sepsis and related organ dysfunction, supporting its role as an early biomarker in the diagnosis of SA-AKI.16–18 HBP is a neutrophil granule protein that was initially discovered and isolated by Shafer et al in 1984.19 It is involved in the regulation of inflammatory responses, enhanced vascular endothelial permeability, and promotes chemokine release. HBP is synthesized during neutrophil maturation and is primarily stored in secretory vesicles near the cell membrane. Upon stimulation, it is rapidly released into the extracellular space, with a release rate exceeding that of other neutrophil granule proteins.20 Neutrophils may release HBP in response to various external stimuli such as antigens, cytokines, enzymes, and inflammatory mediators. HBP plays a critical role in enhancing vascular endothelial permeability.21 It interacts with glycosaminoglycans and activates protein kinase C (PKC) and Rho kinase signalling cascades, leading to cellular calcium influx. This culminates in cytoskeletal rearrangement and the formation of gaps between endothelial cells, leading to vascular leakage and neutrophil extravasation. In addition, HBP amplifies monocyte activation and stimulates the production of a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and IL-6, thereby facilitating inflammatory progression and contributing to tissue damage.22

Sepsis is associated with acute kidney injury, and SA-AKI is caused by various processes such as ischemic hypoperfusion, cellular damage caused by cytokines and free radicals, and energy metabolism dysfunction in renal tubular epithelial cells. Among these mechanisms, a marked rise in inflammatory mediators within the kidney is a major contributor to AKI development.23 Immune dysfunction is a hallmark of sepsis. The initial stage is characterized by massive activation of inflammatory cells and release of pro-inflammatory mediators. Over time, the immune system suppresses itself because of the exhaustion of inflammatory mediators and immunocyte death. During the early phases, SA-AKI is typically accompanied by a significant increase in inflammatory mediators. These mediators act on renal tubular epithelial cells, causing cytoskeletal disruption and apoptosis. On this basis, the predictive value of inflammatory mediators for SA-AKI is justifiable. Recent studies have demonstrated that HBP plays a role in the pathophysiology of AKI predicts clinical risk factors for sepsis-induced AKI.24 Fisher et al10 reported that HBP levels were markedly elevated in patients with septic shock and AKI. In their in vitro study of human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells, stimulation with HBP significantly elevated IL-6 concentration in the supernatant, suggesting that HBP may participate in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SA-AKI. According to Xing et al25 HBP plays a significant role in the early inflammatory response associated with SA-AKI by promoting M1 macrophage activation and inhibiting the release of TNF- α and IL-6. They also reported that HBP expression was suppressed by heparin administration, thereby preventing the severity of renal injury and the extent of macrophage infiltration and activation. These findings suggest that early macrophage infiltration or activation reduction could be a new modality for the prevention and treatment of AKI in the case of patients with sepsis.

In the present study, HBP levels were significantly higher in the SA-AKI group than in the non-SA-AKI group. Furthermore, HBP levels in the SA-AKI group increased over time, whereas they decreased in the non-SA-AKI group, with statistically significant differences observed between the groups. HBP levels in the SA-AKI group were significantly higher than those in the non-SA-AKI group throughout the observation period, further supporting the importance of HBP in the early diagnosis of SA-AKI. Among the other four infection biomarkers, PCT and IL-6 differed significantly between the two groups, whereas WBC and CRP levels did not. This finding may be explained by the complexity of the clinical conditions of the patients included in this study, who presented with multiple underlying diseases and a broad age range. Disruptions in homeostasis, stress responses, and imbalances between oxygen supply and consumption may also occur in such patients, thereby increasing WBC and CRP levels. Moreover, WBC was not statistically significant in the two groups, which means that WBC cannot be used as a reliable predictor of the incidence of SA-AKI. This may be associated with bone marrow suppression due to major infection that results in a minor rise or even a fall in the WBC count. Moreover, transient leukopenia is a common occurrence in sepsis. However, it is expected that the decrease in WBC count in patients with septic shock would mitigate the rise in HBP since HBP is an immune response caused by neutrophils. In this study, the HBP levels of patients with low WBC count are still above the cutoff value of 25.16 ng/mL, suggesting that HBP may provide an earlier and more rapid warning about SA-AKI and offer a stronger basis for early diagnosis.

ROC curve analysis showed that HBP demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared with PCT and IL-6, with the highest AUC. This finding suggests that, during the initial stage of infection, other infection markers may be less effective in predicting the onset of SA-AKI and that elevated HBP levels may provide an earlier diagnostic warning. Using the optimal cut-off values for HBP and PCT, HBP exhibited sensitivity comparable to that of PCT but substantially higher specificity for the diagnosis of SA-AKI. Similarly, at the optimal cut-off value of IL-6, although the sensitivity of HBP was comparatively lower than that of IL-6, its specificity was significantly higher. For the other two infection markers (CRP and WBC), no significant differences were observed between the two groups; therefore, ROC curve comparison was not performed. When a plasma HBP level of 25.16 ng/mL was used as the cut-off point for differentiating SA-AKI, the sensitivity and specificity for SA-AKI diagnosis were 73.7% and 86.8%, respectively. This cut-off value differs slightly from the optimal thresholds of SA-AKI reported in other studies. Such discrepancies in diagnostic performance and optimal cut-off values of HBP between this study and others may be attributed to differences in inclusion and exclusion criteria, control group design, sample size, and potential detection errors.

In this retrospective single-center cohort of 114 patients with sepsis, HBP demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC 0.869) for SA-AKI, outperforming PCT and IL-6. However, these findings must be interpreted with caution: the retrospective design, modest sample size, and lack of external validation limit robustness and generalizability, and its incremental value beyond SOFA score remains unclear. The optimal cut-off (25.16 ng/mL) requires external validation in larger prospective multicenter studies before clinical application can be considered.
