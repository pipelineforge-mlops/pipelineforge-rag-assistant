Background

Adnexal torsion is a common gynecologic emergency but is relatively rare during pregnancy, with most cases occurring in the first trimester. Torsion of a normal adnexa without an underlying mass is uncommon, and torsion arising in the second trimester is far less common.

Case Presentation

A 32-year-old pregnant woman (gravida 5, para 0) with a history of left salpingectomy for ectopic pregnancy and laparoscopic left ovarian cystectomy for a benign cyst presented with acute right lower quadrant pain at 19 weeks and 1 day of gestation. Emergency computed tomography (CT) revealed a heterogeneous hyperdense lesion in the right adnexa with possible hemorrhage; torsion could not be excluded. Emergency single-port laparoscopic exploration was performed, which revealed multiple twists of the right ovarian proper ligament and the infundibulopelvic ligament (IP ligament). The right ovary and fallopian tube appeared black-purple and necrotic, while the appendix was grossly normal. Accordingly, right adnexectomy was performed. Postoperative pathology confirmed extensive hemorrhagic necrosis of the fallopian tube and ovarian tissue, consistent with torsion. The pregnancy continued uneventfully, and follow-up during the third trimester showed normal fetal development.

Conclusion

This case indicates that torsion of normal adnexa can occur in the second trimester, and adhesions from prior pelvic surgery may be a predisposing factor. For pregnant patients with acute abdominal pain and a history of pelvic surgery, a high index of suspicion for adnexal torsion is warranted.

Introduction

Adnexal torsion is a common gynecologic emergency, but it is relatively rare during pregnancy. A systematic review of 662 cases reported that most pregnancy-associated adnexal torsions occur in the first trimester (54.63%), with second-trimester and third-trimester torsions accounting for 26.36% and 19.00%, respectively.1 Adnexal torsion typically presents with acute unilateral pelvic pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. The underlying mechanism involves rotation of the adnexa around its vascular axis, leading to venous obstruction, ovarian congestion and edema, which may progress to arterial compromise and ischemic necrosis. During pregnancy, the clinical presentation of adnexal torsion is often non-specific and can mimic acute appendicitis, cholecystitis, renal colic, as well as pregnancy-related conditions such as miscarriage or placental abruption, making the diagnosis particularly challenging.

Most adnexal torsions in pregnancy are associated with underlying adnexal pathology, such as benign germ cell tumors, benign cystic lesions, or benign epithelial tumors.2 Torsion of a normal adnexa without any mass is rare. Here we report a case of torsion of a normal right ovary and fallopian tube in a patient with a history of contralateral pelvic surgery, with a focus on the possible role of adhesions as a predisposing factor.

Case Report

A 32-year-old woman (gravida 5, para 0) was admitted with acute right lower quadrant pain of 4 hours’ duration at 19 weeks and 1 day of gestation. Her obstetric history included three induced abortions, a left salpingectomy for ectopic pregnancy, and laparoscopic left ovarian cystectomy in 2024 for a benign cyst. This pregnancy was spontaneous, with last menstrual period on September 4, 2025, and expected delivery date on June 11, 2026.

Prenatal Course

Early ultrasound confirmed an intrauterine pregnancy with normal adnexa. She had typical first-trimester nausea and vomiting that resolved by 3 months. Nuchal translucency ultrasound (11–13⁺6 weeks) and second-trimester level II ultrasound (17⁺5 weeks) were normal, and non-invasive prenatal testing showed low risk. She had not yet perceived fetal movements.

Presentation

At 19 weeks and 1 day, the patient developed sudden-onset intermittent colicky right lower quadrant pain that progressively worsened, accompanied by nausea and vomiting. There was no referred pain, abdominal tightness, vaginal bleeding, or fluid leakage.

Physical Examination

Temperature 36.8°C, heart rate 75 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, blood pressure 112/77 mmHg. The abdomen was distended but soft, with right lower quadrant tenderness and no rebound tenderness or guarding. Bowel sounds were normal. Fetal heart rate was 140 beats/min, and no uterine contractions were palpated.

Imaging and Laboratory Findings

Emergency CT showed a slightly enlarged right adnexa containing a heterogeneous hyperdense lesion (maximum diameter 3.2 cm) suggestive of hemorrhage; torsion could not be excluded (Figures 1 and 2). A small amount of pelvic fluid was present. The appendix was not well visualized but appeared not dilated. Appendiceal ultrasound showed no definitive abnormalities. Initial blood tests: white blood cell count 13.93×109/L (neutrophils 90.2%), hemoglobin 125 g/L, platelet count 218×109/L, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein 0.91 mg/L. After admission, procalcitonin was 0.09 ng/mL, interleukin-6 6 pg/mL; alanine aminotransferase 93 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 61 U/L; D-dimer 1.23 μg/mL; and repeat white blood cell count 10.56×109/L (neutrophils 94.3%). Repeat ultrasound showed a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass measuring 6.2 cm×3.2 cm in the right adnexa with well-defined borders and no detectable blood flow, which had increased in size compared with the initial CT.

Management

The patient received treatment with phloroglucinol, progesterone, and cefoxitin; however, her pain did not resolve and gradually worsened. After consultation with the gastrointestinal surgery department, acute appendicitis was excluded. Given a high suspicion of right adnexal torsion, emergency single-port laparoscopic exploration was performed on the same day (at 19 weeks and 1 day of gestation). The time from emergency admission to the start of surgery was approximately 3 hours.

Intraoperative findings included the following: approximately 20 mL of unclotted blood in the right paracolic gutter; an enlarged uterus corresponding to about 4 months’ gestational size with a smooth surface; multiple torsions of the right ovarian proper ligament and the right IP ligament, which appeared cord-like and under marked tension; adhesions between the right suspensory ligament and the pelvic sidewall; a black-purple right ovary and fallopian tube, measuring approximately 5 cm× 3 cm × 3 cm; the left adnexa could not be visualized due to obstruction by the gravid uterus; and the appendix appeared grossly normal (Figure 3). Consequently, right adnexectomy was performed. Figure 3 Intraoperative laparoscopic findings revealed torsion and necrosis of the right ovary and fallopian tube, which appeared purplish-black.

Laparoscopic view of a torsed, necrotic right ovary and fallopian tube with a purplish-black appearance.

Pathology

Postoperative pathology confirmed extensive hemorrhagic necrosis of the fallopian tube and ovarian tissue, consistent with torsion.

Follow-Up

Postoperatively, the patient experienced irregular abdominal pain and expressed a strong desire to maintain the pregnancy. Accordingly, magnesium sulfate was administered for fetal neuroprotection, and intramuscular progesterone (20 mg once daily for three consecutive days) was given to inhibit uterine contractions. In addition, cefoxitin and intravenous fluids were provided. The patient recovered uneventfully, and a prenatal follow-up on April 23, 2026, demonstrated normal fetal growth and development.

Discussion

The overall incidence of adnexal torsion in pregnancy is estimated at 3–5 per 10,000 pregnancies. Pregnancy itself is considered an independent risk factor for adnexal torsion, likely due to ovarian hyperstimulation, increased ovarian volume, and the anatomical changes associated with uterine enlargement. Among all cases of adnexal torsion, 8–28% occur during pregnancy. The present case of second-trimester torsion of a normal adnexa is uncommon. The development of adnexal torsion in pregnancy results from an interplay of multiple anatomical, pathological, and physiological factors. Underlying adnexal pathology is the main predisposing factor, such as benign germ cell tumors, benign cystic lesions, or benign epithelial tumors, whereas malignant tumors are rarely involved.2 Meanwhile, literature reports indicate that the incidence of right adnexal torsion is approximately twice that of the left side, likely due to the restricted ovarian mobility caused by the left-sided location of the sigmoid colon compared to the relatively greater mobility of the right adnexa.3 In addition, the mode of conception is also associated with torsion risk: pregnant women who conceive through assisted reproductive technology have a significantly higher rate of adnexal torsion than those with spontaneous conception, possibly due to ovarian hyperstimulation, formation of multiple corpora lutea, and increased ovarian volume.4,5 The vast majority of torsions occur in ovaries with space-occupying lesions, which increase adnexal volume and mass, making torsion more likely to occur in the setting of uterine enlargement and ligamentous relaxation during pregnancy. In our patient, both intraoperative and postoperative pathology confirmed that the torsioned adnexa consisted of normal ovarian and fallopian tube tissue without any space-occupying lesion, representing a rare condition.

Torsion of a normal adnexa is generally attributed to hypermobility, which may be congenital in origin—such as an excessively long ovarian suspensory ligament, elongated fallopian tube, or ballooning of the mesosalpinx. Literature on torsion of a normal adnexa is scarce, with most reported cases occurring in the third trimester. Guennoun et al (2017) reported a case at 8 weeks of gestation managed by detorsion and ovarian aspiration (which yielded negative findings), without oophoropexy, suggesting that conservative surgery may suffice in early pregnancy.6 Li et al (2018) reported a case at 32+5 weeks in which Doppler ultrasound failed to detect ovarian pathology, whereas MRI raised a strong suspicion of adnexal torsion; the patient underwent emergency exploratory laparotomy with resection of the left adnexa and necrotic ovary, highlighting the potential diagnostic advantage of MRI over ultrasound in the early detection of ovarian torsion.7 Ferrari et al (2019) presented a case of normal adnexal torsion at 32 weeks in a dichorionic-diamniotic twin pregnancy, where the enlarged uterus precluded laparoscopic access, necessitating open detorsion and fixation—underscoring the need for flexible surgical route selection in multifetal gestations or marked uterine enlargement.8 Tanaka et al (2018) reported a case at 31 weeks diagnosed by MRI and treated with open surgery, with resection of the torsed ovary and fallopian tube; they emphasized the diagnostic value of MRI, noting that asymmetry in ovarian vein diameter—thinning on the torsed side and compensatory dilation on the contralateral side—may serve as a useful ancillary sign when ultrasound is inconclusive in the third trimester.9 While these reports cover a wide spectrum of clinical scenarios spanning early to late singleton and twin pregnancies, no studies have investigated the potential impact of prior pelvic surgery on normal adnexal torsion. In contrast, our case occurred in the second trimester (20 weeks) in a patient with a history of left-sided ovarian cystectomy and salpingectomy. Intraoperatively, adhesions were observed between the right suspensory ligament and the pelvic sidewall, suggesting that prior pelvic surgery—even on the contralateral side—may alter pelvic anatomy, leading to abnormal mobility or positioning of the right adnexa and thereby increasing torsion risk. Although the torsion occurred on the contralateral side, adhesions from the left-sided surgery may have indirectly affected the stability of the right adnexa through traction or fixation effects. This case enriches the limited literature on normal adnexal torsion across different gestational stages and highlights the importance of a history of pelvic surgery (even contralateral) as a potential contributing factor.

The differential diagnosis of acute abdominal pain during pregnancy is broad, and adnexal torsion—particularly in the second trimester—presents a notable diagnostic challenge. The enlarged gravid uterus displaces the adnexa upward and posteriorly, rendering tenderness atypical and ultrasonographic evaluation difficult. Differential considerations for adnexal torsion include ruptured hemorrhagic ovarian cyst, massive ovarian edema, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, and degenerating leiomyoma.10 Beyond adnexal pathology, although uterine torsion is extremely rare, it should also be considered; defined as rotation of the gravid uterus around its longitudinal cervical axis, this rare entity may compromise uteroplacental perfusion and precipitate fetal distress or placental abruption. Its clinical presentation—acute abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting—is non-specific and often mimics adnexal torsion, and the diagnosis is frequently made incidentally during cesarean section. Therefore, for pregnant patients presenting with acute abdominal pain, particularly those with abnormal fetal heart rate monitoring, uterine torsion merits inclusion in the differential.11 Additionally, in patients with a history of pelvic surgery, the possibility of intra-abdominal hemorrhage resulting from rupture of neovascularized adhesions should be considered. Post-surgical adhesions may harbor proliferative vascular tissue; as the uterus enlarges during pregnancy, these adhesions can be stretched, causing vessel rupture and leading to acute abdomen or hemorrhagic shock.12 Consequently, a detailed history of prior pelvic surgery is indispensable, and this condition should likewise be incorporated into the differential diagnosis.

Although ultrasound is the preferred imaging modality for adnexal torsion during pregnancy, and findings such as ovarian edema, the “whirlpool sign”, and diminished or absent Doppler flow are highly specific for the diagnosis,13–15 it has significant limitations and a relatively low diagnostic accuracy. CT also plays an important role in identifying adnexal torsion and expediting definitive treatment, particularly when the diagnosis is not initially suspected. Typical CT features include ovarian enlargement, edema, and deviation toward the midline; peripheral displacement of ovarian follicles (the “string of pearls” sign); a benign ovarian mass serving as a leading point; surrounding inflammatory changes or free fluid; deviation of the uterus toward the affected side; and hemorrhage or lack of internal enhancement indicating ovarian infarction.5 MRI offers excellent soft-tissue resolution and avoids radiation exposure, making it a valuable complementary tool when ultrasound findings are inconclusive. However, no imaging study should delay surgical intervention when there is a high clinical suspicion of torsion. In our patient, CT and ultrasound revealed an abnormal echogenic mass with hemorrhage, but the classic “whirlpool sign” was not clearly visible, likely due to uterine enlargement and adnexal displacement in the second trimester. D-dimer and white blood cell counts were nonspecific, but serial ultrasound showing an enlarging adnexal mass with loss of blood flow was highly suggestive of torsion.

Once adnexal torsion is highly suspected, prompt surgical intervention is indicated. Laparoscopy has been proven safe and efficacious in the second trimester of pregnancy, particularly in the second trimester. Compared with laparotomy, laparoscopy is associated with less blood loss, fewer wound healing complications, and shorter hospital stays, without adversely affecting obstetric outcomes.16–19 In this case, single-port laparoscopy provided good visualization and allowed successful necrotic adnexectomy without inducing uterine contractions or abortion, supporting its feasibility and safety. Ultrasound-guided cyst aspiration has been reported as an effective conservative treatment in selected cases,20 but it was not applicable here because there was no cyst.

Because the adnexa was already necrotic at the time of surgery, detorsion was not possible, and adnexectomy was performed. If diagnosed early, detorsion with adnexal preservation is the preferred approach to maintain endocrine and reproductive function.21 Ovarian detorsion can be performed, and some reports even describe intraoperative cyst aspiration to reduce the risk of recurrent torsion.22 Therefore, increasing awareness of this condition and shortening the interval from diagnosis to surgery are key to improving prognosis.

In conclusion, this case suggests that adhesions from prior pelvic surgery—even contralateral surgery—may be a potential trigger for torsion of a normal adnexa during the second trimester. Adnexal torsion should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute abdominal pain during pregnancy, even in the absence of an adnexal mass. A high index of suspicion, prompt imaging evaluation, and timely surgical intervention are essential to preserve ovarian function and avoid adverse pregnancy outcomes.
