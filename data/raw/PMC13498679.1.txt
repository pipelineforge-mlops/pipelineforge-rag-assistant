Introduction

Contemporary nursing leadership scholarship has expanded well beyond traditional managerial roles to include systems transformation, workforce innovation and population health strategy (Kies, 2025; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2021; Weiss et al., 2025). Yet one domain remains comparatively underexamined: regulatory governance.

Regulatory rulemaking, often perceived as procedural or bureaucratic, constitutes a powerful yet largely invisible arena of nursing leadership. Decisions embedded in education codes, licensure statutes and accreditation rules shape who enters the profession, how nurses are trained and how workforce capacity evolves (Hancock et al., 2025; Spector et al., 2020). This argument aligns with the State of the world’s nursing 2025 report, which frames nursing workforce strengthening around investment in education, jobs, leadership and service delivery and introduces expanded indicators related to education capacity, advanced practice nursing and remuneration (World Health Organization, 2025). In this Perspective, I argue that regulatory reform represents a critical form of systems-level nursing leadership. Drawing on my engagement in academic administration and regulatory collaboration, I examine the leadership implications of revisions to Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 2, which governs nursing education standards and programme approval in the state of Iowa, United States. Although geographically situated, this case offers globally relevant insights into how nurse leaders influence professional formation through governance infrastructure.

Perspective approach and experiential basis

This manuscript is presented as a perspective paper rather than an empirical outcome evaluation. The experiential basis for the analysis includes academic administrative work related to nursing programme growth, interpretation of state nursing education requirements, clinical placement capacity planning and collaboration with regulatory and practice partners during rule review and implementation. These experiences informed the identification of leadership processes central to the case: boundary spanning across education and practice sectors, policy negotiation around standards and feasibility, and governance stewardship aimed at balancing workforce expansion with public protection. The analysis therefore uses experiential engagement as a source of situated policy insight while grounding interpretive claims in nursing regulation, workforce and simulation scholarship.

Explaining the Iowa Administrative Code for a global audience

For readers outside the United States, it is important to contextualise how nursing education regulation functions structurally. Unlike many countries with centralised national regulation, the United States operates under a state-based licensure model in which each state board of nursing holds statutory authority to approve pre-licensure nursing programmes, establish faculty standards, monitor clinical training quality and ensure public protection (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2021; Russell, 2017; Spector et al., 2018). Administrative rules, such as those codified in the Iowa Administrative Code, translate legislative statutes into operational policy by defining how laws are implemented, enforced and evaluated (Iowa Legislature, 2026).

Rulemaking authority is governed by the Iowa Administrative Procedure Act, which outlines formal processes for adopting, amending or repealing regulations, including public notice, stakeholder engagement, fiscal review and legislative oversight (Iowa Legislature, 2026). Proposed changes are disseminated through Notices of Intended Action, allowing educational institutions, professional associations and community stakeholders to provide formal comment before rules are finalised and codified with the force of law (Iowa Legislature, 2026). This transparent and participatory process ensures that regulatory changes reflect both public protection mandates and workforce realities.

Understanding this governance architecture is essential because it positions regulatory revision not as clerical activity, but as negotiated policy leadership shaping professional education systems.

Chapter 2 reform as a leadership case study

Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 2 governs the approval, monitoring and accountability of nursing education programmes. Revisions to this chapter were driven by converging pressures, including workforce shortages, faculty capacity constraints, evolving clinical training models and calls for stronger programme accountability (Iowa Board of Regents, 2025). From a leadership standpoint, the reform process required balancing competing imperatives: expanding workforce pathway capacity while preserving educational rigour, ensuring public protection while supporting innovation, and strengthening rural workforce distribution while maintaining regulatory consistency.

Key areas of reform included faculty qualification standards, clinical education infrastructure, simulation integration and programme accountability metrics. Updated faculty provisions addressed graduate preparation expectations, clinical expertise requirements and oversight responsibilities. Clinical education revisions focused on preceptor qualifications, placement distribution and academic-practice partnerships, particularly consequential in rural states where placement scarcity constrains enrolment growth. Regulatory modernisation also expanded the allowable use of high-fidelity simulation to supplement direct care hours, reflecting evidence that well-designed simulation can support prelicensure competency development when implemented with appropriate faculty preparation, debriefing and quality controls (Hayden et al., 2014; Spector et al., 2020). Strengthened accountability measures including licensure pass rates, attrition monitoring and outcome reporting reinforced quality assurance mechanisms across programmes.

Regulatory reform as systems leadership

The leadership work embedded in these reforms illustrates regulatory governance as systems leadership in action. Leadership scholarship increasingly emphasises systems thinking, the capacity to influence interconnected structures shaping outcomes across sectors. Chapter 2 reform required boundary-spanning collaboration among academic leaders, clinical partners, workforce boards and policymakers. It demanded adaptive leadership to navigate tensions between workforce expansion and educational quality, policy entrepreneurship to translate workforce data into regulatory proposals, and governance stewardship to protect public trust.

These processes unfolded not within hospitals or classrooms, but within hearings, comment periods, fiscal analyses and legislative briefings, spaces rarely centred in nursing leadership discourse yet profoundly consequential for workforce development. By engaging in regulatory negotiation, nurse leaders shaped structural determinants of education quality and workforce readiness, demonstrating leadership enacted through governance rather than organisational hierarchy.

Workforce and education system impact

Regulatory reform functions as an upstream workforce intervention because changes to education approval standards directly influence supply, distribution and readiness of the nursing workforce. Because state boards of nursing regulate programme approval, faculty expectations, clinical learning conditions and outcome monitoring, changes to these rules can affect both educational capacity and geographic workforce distribution (Russell, 2017; Spector et al., 2018). In Iowa, workforce alignment discussions have emphasised the need to connect academic programme capacity with state labour market and health workforce needs (Iowa Board of Regents, 2025). Within that context, simulation allowances and clinical partnership flexibility may enable programmes to expand enrolment without proportionally increasing clinical site demand, whereas preceptor and partnership provisions can support rural training infrastructure and geographically distributed workforce pathways. Revised faculty qualification pathways may also address educator shortages while maintaining graduate-level preparation expectations.

Expanded approval pathways also created opportunities for community-based and minority-serving institutions to scale enrolment, advancing equity in programme access (American Association of Colleges of Nursing [AACN], 2023; Murray et al., 2022). In this way, regulatory leadership shapes not only education systems but broader workforce equity and health access outcomes. The ripple effects extend beyond academia into hospital staffing capacity, rural health access and population health system resilience.

Locating the case in global leadership literature

Although this case is situated in Iowa, regulatory leadership resonates globally. Comparable governance structures exist across nursing education systems, including national regulatory councils in the United Kingdom, provincial colleges in Canada, accreditation authorities in Australia and emerging regulatory strengthening initiatives across low- and middle-income countries (Chiu et al., 2025; Ortiz-Contreras et al., 2023; Palmer et al., 2024). The State of the world’s nursing 2025 report further underscores that nursing workforce policy must attend to education capacity, leadership and service delivery across regions, reinforcing the relevance of regulatory governance as a global leadership concern (World Health Organization, 2025). Across these contexts, regulatory bodies shape workforce competency, mobility and system readiness.

Yet leadership literature rarely examines the actors driving these governance transformations. By analysing Chapter 2 reform through systems and policy leadership lenses, this perspective contributes to a growing recognition that regulatory arenas function as leadership laboratories influencing health system capacity worldwide. The case illustrates how subnational governance reforms can generate national and international leadership insights.

Advancing nursing leadership science

This case advances nursing leadership science in several ways. Firstly, it expands recognised leadership arenas beyond organisational and clinical settings to include governance infrastructure. Secondly, it identifies regulatory leadership competencies such as policy analysis, stakeholder negotiation, fiscal interpretation and legislative communication, that remain underdeveloped in leadership curricula (Dyess et al., 2016; Ogle et al., 2025). Thirdly, it strengthens the evidence base linking education regulation to workforce supply, quality and distribution.

Reframing rulemaking as leadership praxis rather than administrative compliance broadens how the discipline conceptualises influence and impact. It underscores the need to examine leadership where professional standards, educational access and workforce pathways are structurally defined.

Implications for research, education and practice

The implications for research, education and practice are substantial. Future research should examine comparative international regulatory reforms, workforce outcomes linked to education policy change, simulation regulation impacts on competency and faculty workforce policy interventions. Educationally, doctoral and graduate nursing programmes should incorporate regulatory policy fellowships, governance practicums, legislative advocacy training and administrative law literacy to prepare nurse leaders for board of nursing engagement (Cropley et al., 2022). This recommendation also responds to recent doctoral education debates in the Journal of Advanced Nursing special issue, which foregrounded the need to strengthen the doctoral pathway and reposition nursing research and researchers as central to future health system solutions (McCormack et al., 2026). Regulatory leadership is a complementary but still underdeveloped element of that doctoral preparation agenda.

Practice and academic sectors must co-engage regulatory processes, particularly in strengthening clinical placement infrastructure, preceptor development and workforce pathway alignment. Globally, enhancing regulatory literacy among nurse leaders can support workforce mobility agreements, cross-border education standards, competency harmonisation and implementation of the 2026–2030 workforce priorities identified in State of the world’s nursing 2025 (World Health Organization, 2025). Preparing leaders to function effectively within governance systems is essential to sustaining the profession’s future.

Future directions for nursing leadership theory

Regulatory leadership invites theoretical expansion. Traditional leadership models emphasise organisations, whereas governance leadership operates across policy ecosystems (Palmer, 2026). Integrating systems leadership, complexity theory and policy entrepreneurship frameworks can better explain how nurse leaders influence structural determinants of workforce development. Such theoretical evolution is essential as health systems confront workforce shortages, educational bottlenecks and escalating population health demands. Expanding leadership theory to include regulatory governance ensures that scholarship reflects the full spectrum of influence shaping nursing’s future.

Conclusion: making the invisible visible

Regulatory frameworks constitute the scaffolding upon which nursing education and workforce systems are built, yet the leadership required to design, negotiate and implement these frameworks remains underrecognised. Through the case of Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 2 reform, I argue that rulemaking represents a consequential form of systems leadership; one that shapes professional formation, workforce capacity and ultimately population health outcomes.

If nursing leadership scholarship is to generate a truly contemporary state-of-the-nation and global evidence base, it must expand its analytic lens to include governance arenas where policy, education and workforce futures are negotiated. This expanded lens is consistent with global nursing workforce priorities that connect education, leadership, jobs and service delivery as mutually reinforcing domains (World Health Organization, 2025). Nurse leaders are not only clinicians, executives and scholars; they are also architects of regulatory systems that define the profession’s trajectory. Recognising and studying this leadership domain is essential to advancing the science, practice and global impact of nursing leadership.
