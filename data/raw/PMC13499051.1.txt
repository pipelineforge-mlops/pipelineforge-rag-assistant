Background

Early childhood is a critical window for cardiac development, yet the impact of prenatal factors and early growth patterns on heart structure and function in children with structurally normal hearts remains incompletely described. This study investigates the roles of birth weight (BW), gestational age (GA), birthweight-for-gestational-age (BW/GA) z-score, and weight gain z-score in shaping cardiac dimensions.

Methods

In this longitudinal study we analyzed 1000 echocardiograms from 738 children aged 11 to 83 months. Z-scores for cardiac dimensions and volumes were used to standardize for body surface area (BSA). BW, BW/GA z-score, GA, and weight gain z-score were analyzed as predictors of these parameters.

Results

Higher weight gain z-score was independently associated with smaller cardiac dimension z-scores, particularly in the aortic root, ascending aorta, left atrium diameter, left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole, left ventricular internal diameter at end-systole, end-systolic volume, end-diastolic volume, and left ventricular mass. While birth characteristics influenced some cardiac parameters, postnatal weight gain emerged as a more significant predictor of cardiac size. Ejection fraction and fractional shortening were not influenced by the predictors in our analysis.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that postnatal weight gain was more consistently associated with cardiac structure z-scores than anthropometric characteristics at birth. Specifically, contractile myocardial thicknesses were relatively stable across weight gain patterns, whereas vascular structures showed inverse scaling with higher weight gain.

1 Introduction

Cardiac size and growth patterns during childhood are associated with cardiac health and may have implications for long-term cardiovascular risk [1], [2]. Multiple observational studies have linked lower birth weight (BW) to an increased risk of developing cardiometabolic diseases later in life [3], [4], [5]. Yet, whether this programming effect influences cardiac structure and function in childhood has not been thoroughly investigated.

Cardiovascular structures undergo significant changes in size, hemodynamics, and function from early fetal stages through late adolescence. For example, left ventricular end-diastolic volume (EDV) can increase nearly 200-fold from premature infants to large adolescents. These changes are largely driven by metabolic demands, closely tied to body size and composition [6], [7], [8].

While body size and age are the most researched factors influencing heart size in children, the impact of other factors, such as postnatal weight gain, on cardiac structure and function during early development is less understood [6], [9], [10], [11], [12]. Two studies have demonstrated that individuals born prematurely have smaller cardiac chamber sizes and reduced cardiac mass compared with their term-born peers, with findings reported in children as well as adolescents and young adults [13], [14]. However, findings are not always consistent; for instance, another recent study found that gestational age (GA) had no effect on cardiac structure [15]. Additionally, lower genetically-predicted BW [2] is found to be inversely associated with indexed left chamber volumes and positively associated with greater left ventricular mass (LVM). Conversely, a study focusing solely on observed, rather than predicted, BW found no association with LVM [2], [16].

These differing findings underscore the importance of examining a broader set of variables and considering their potential interactions. Understanding these nuances is crucial for developing a more accurate and holistic view of how perinatal and early life factors influence cardiac structure and function.

In our study, we evaluated whether BW, birthweight-for-gestational-age (BW/GA) z-score, GA at birth or change in weight-for-age (WFA) z-score, which is synonymous with a weight gain z-score, predict changes in 16 echocardiographic measures independent of body size.

Building on previous findings, this research incorporates additional sociodemographic factors that may influence cardiac development. Our study includes 1000 echocardiograms from a diverse group of 738 participants, aged 11 to 83 months, within the New York University Children's Health and Environment Study (NYU CHES) cohort, offering a broad perspective on perinatal and anthropometric factors influencing early cardiac growth.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Ethics statement

All participants provided written, informed consent, and the study received approval from the institutional review board of the NYU School of Medicine (i15-00778; i16-01617).

2.2 Study population

This study includes mother-child pairs enrolled in NYU CHES, a prospective birth cohort designed to evaluate a range of environmental and social factors on fetal and post-natal growth and development. Detailed information about the study has been previously described [17].

This analysis includes a subset of the NYU CHES cohort. With the mother's consent, echocardiograms were conducted on the children during postnatal study visits, specifically targeting those aged approximately 1 to 7 years. Out of 1179 echocardiography attempts, 111 were excluded for inadequate images, poor quality or non-compliance, 7 had missing two dimensional (2D) measurements, 44 were twins, 2 had congenital heart disease, and 15 had missing height/weight. We then analyzed the remaining 1000 echocardiograms of 738 children from singleton pregnancies (Fig. S1).

2.3 Exposure assessments

GA, BW, and infant sex were collected from medical records. Using these measures, we calculated sex-specific BW/GA z-scores according to the INTERGROWTH-21st standard [18]. Weight and height data at the time of each child's echocardiogram visit were also collected as part of the study visit. For 17 children who were missing visit height and weight data, we used measurements from Electronic Health Records, if available within a 12-week window. WFA z-scores were calculated using the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards and the WHO 5–19 years growth reference [19]. We defined WFA z-score change as the difference between the WFA z-score at the time of the echocardiogram visit and the BW/GA z-score. GA, BW, BW/GA z-score, and WFA z-score change were used as independent variables in our analysis in separate models.

2.4 Outcome measures

Echocardiograms were performed for child participants at each visit as part of the NYU CHES study protocol, and not as part of pediatric clinical care (i.e. echocardiograms were not clinically indicated). Echocardiograms were performed by one of four echocardiographers. From these studies, we obtained standard measurements of the following, using inner-to-inner edge method: aortic valve (AV) annulus diameter, aortic root diameter, sinotubular junction diameter, ascending aorta diameter at mid-systole, and maximal left atrial diameter at end-systole [20]. Additional measurements included intraventricular septal diameter at end-diastole (IVSd), intraventricular septal diameter at end-systole (IVSs), left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd), left ventricular internal diameter at end-systole (LVIDs), left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-diastole (LVPWd), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-systole (LVPWs). From these measurements, we calculated the end-diastolic left ventricular volume (EDV), end-systolic left ventricular volume (ESV), left ventricular fractional shortening (FS), and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), applying the Teichholz method [21]. Left Ventricular Mass (LVM) was calculated using a standard formula recommended by the American Society of Echocardiography, 0.8 × 1.04 × [(IVSd + LVIDd + LVPWd)3 - LVIDd3] + 0.6 [22]. All measurements were derived from the parasternal long-axis (PLAX) 2D video clips still frames or motion mode (M-mode) frames when available.

Measurements were collected at four distinct time points: 11–23 months (< 2 years, 339 ECHOs), 24–35 months (≥ 2 years to <3 years, 385 ECHOs), 36–59 months (≥ 3 years to <5 years, 229 ECHOs), and 60–83 months (≥ 5 years to <7 years, 47 ECHOs). Not all participants had measurements at every time point. We analyzed a total of 1000 echocardiograms from 738 children, consisting of 504 children with a single echocardiogram, 206 children with two echocardiograms, and 28 children with three echocardiograms across these distinct timepoints. These timepoints were selected in alignment with NYU CHES objectives and study protocol.

We assigned within-population z-scores for each heart measure, adjusted for body surface area (BSA), which is a commonly used variable for normalizing cardiovascular dimensions. Adjusting cardiac measurements for BSA enhances the accuracy of interpretation and allows for valid comparisons, as body size significantly impacts echocardiographic readings. These z-scores were calculated by adjusting each cardiac measure for BSA using an allometric scaling approach. 2.5 Covariates

Potential confounders were identified based on a thorough literature review. These included self-reported education [28], self-identified race/ethnicity (i.e., Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Asian, Other, or multiple race) [29], maternal age at delivery, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) [30], [31], breastfeeding duration [32], and alcohol consumption (yes/no) [33]. Education level, race/ethnicity, maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and alcohol consumption were collected using questionnaires administered during prenatal study visits, while breastfeeding duration was collected through postnatal questionnaires. Breastfeeding duration was used as a covariate only in models where WFA z-score change was the exposure, as it would not relate to models with birth characteristics. Smoking during pregnancy was not included in our models due to the limited number of smokers in our sample (n = 4).

2.6 Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0. To investigate the associations between GA, BW, BW/GA z-score or WFA z-score change with cardiac metrics, we employed linear mixed-effects models. These models were chosen because they account for the repeated measures across the 1000 echocardiograms, enabling us to manage within-subject correlations effectively, and are well-suited to handle unbalanced data across visit timepoints.

We analyzed four sets of models, each focusing on GA (in weeks), BW (in kilogram), BW/GA z-score (in SDs), or WFA z-score change as a continuous independent variable. For each independent variable, we assessed the association with cardiac measures expressed as z-scores. To visually assess the linearity assumption of our models, we generated scatter plots for the association between each primary predictor and each cardiac z-score (Figs. S2-S9).

We used z-scores for both our exposures and outcomes to allow children of different ages to be compared within the same analyses. As part of our initial statistical approach, we also conducted sensitivity analyses including age at the time of the echocardiogram as a covariate (one model with age as a continuous variable and another with age as a categorical visit time point). The findings remained robust, which is expected, since our primary outcomes are also z-scores and the primary maternal covariates are time-invariant.

The models were run both unadjusted and adjusted for potential confounders. Missing covariate data (overall 1.47% of covariate data points) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. All regressions were performed on the overall sample as well as stratified by sex.

To assess measurement reliability, we evaluated inter- and intraobserver agreement using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) on a randomly selected subset of studies. The analysis demonstrated good to excellent agreement (ICC > 0.75) for most measures. For intra-observer agreement, LVPWd and LVPWs showed moderate agreement, and for inter-observer agreement, LVPWd, IVSs, and LVPWs showed moderate agreement. ICCs and Coefficients of Variation are reported in Table S2. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05 for all tests.

3.2 Analysis results: birth characteristics

In both unadjusted and adjusted models (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), BW, GA, and BW/GA z-score did not show associations with cardiac dimensions in the overall group. When stratified by sex, a few associations were observed, such as between BW/GA z-score and aortic sinus z-score, and between GA and LVM z-score in boys. These findings may suggest sex-specific patterns, but they should be interpreted with caution given the reduced sample size in subgroup analyses. 3.3 Analysis results: weight-for-age z-score change

In the adjusted models, increased WFA z-scoreΔ showed inverse associations with multiple cardiac dimensions: AV annulus z-score (β = −0.23, p < 0.0001), aortic sinus z-score (β = −0.24, p < 0.0001), sinotubular junction z-score (β = −0.23, p < 0.0001), ascending aorta z-score (β = −0.18, p < 0.0001), and LA diameter z-score (β = −0.10, p < 0.0001). In M-mode echocardiographic measurements, significant negative associations with WFA z-score change were also found for LVIDd z-score (β = −0.16, p < 0.0001), LVIDs z-score (β = −0.14, p < 0.0001), ESV z-score (β = −0.17, p < 0.0001), EDV z-score (β = −0.21, p < 0.0001), and LVM z-score (β = −0.15, p < 0.0001). EF and FS, functional indices of myocardial function, were not influenced by any of the predictors in our study.

The unadjusted models showed similar associations across these measurements. Importantly, the direction and magnitude of the beta coefficients remained consistent between unadjusted and adjusted models, suggesting that confounding was not responsible for the observed associations. Because these models evaluate unit-less z-scores, these may be interpreted as follows: for each unit increase in weight gain z-score, the aortic sinus z-score decreases by 0.24. Because an increased WFAzΔ would necessarily correspond to a larger BSA at imaging, versus birth, the z-score of the aortic sinus measurement is lower as a result of a larger denominator. It is important to note that WFAzΔ is not driving the measure itself, but rather the z-score of the measure.

A comprehensive breakdown of the associations for echocardiographic measurements is shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2 for the overall sample. Tables S5 to S12 and Figs. S12 and S13 display these associations for the overall sample, as well as stratified by sex.

To assess whether the observed associations between weight gain z-score and cardiac dimensions were driven by catch-up growth in preterm infants or varied by BW category, we conducted additional analyses stratified by BW (low, normal, and high) while including sex as a covariate (Table S4). Catch-up growth during childhood can significantly influence cardiac structure and function [34]. The inverse associations were replicated within the normal BW group, suggesting that these findings are not solely attributable to preterm-related catch-up growth. However, due to low sample sizes in the low and high BW categories, we were unable to make conclusive inferences for these groups.

3.4 Secondary longitudinal analysis

In a secondary analysis restricted to a subset with repeated echocardiograms, we evaluated whether the change in WFA z-score between visits was associated with corresponding changes in cardiac z-scores. We found that an increased change in WFA z-score was inversely associated with changes in several vascular z-scores, including the aortic sinus (β = −0.32, p < 0.0001), sinotubular junction (β = −0.35, p = 0.0006), and ascending aorta (β = −0.39, p < 0.0001). In contrast, changes in ventricular wall thickness z-scores were not predicted by the change in WFA z-score. These results are presented in Table S13.

4 Discussion

4.1 Summary of key findings

In this study, we found that higher postnatal weight gain z-score was broadly associated with reductions in cardiac dimension z-scores, notably within the aortic valve and root measurements, ascending aorta, LA diameter, LVIDd, LVIDs, ESV, EDV, and LVM. By contrast, birth characteristics exhibited minimal associations with cardiac structure z-scores.

4.2 Interpretation of findings and biological mechanisms

The observation that growth rate, particularly postnatal weight gain, is associated with cardiac development aligns with previous research, although the direction and magnitude of these effects may vary across studies, likely due to differences in the parameters used as indicators of growth. The Dutch Generation R cohort [1] found that early growth trajectories, as reflected by weight, height, BMI, and BSA, are positively associated with various left cardiac structures during the first 2 years of life. This finding is further corroborated by the Finnish Special Turku Coronary Risk Factor Intervention Project for Children study, which demonstrated that current weight was the most influential factor determining LVM in adolescents, accounting for 29% of the variation [35].

While many studies have examined measures of body size at a single time point, such as weight or BMI, our study utilized the weight gain z-score to assess the less-characterized relationship between postnatal changes in relative weight and cardiac structure z-scores.

Our findings suggest a potential discordance between postnatal weight gain trajectory and proportional growth of certain cardiac structures during early childhood. It is possible that increased adiposity or changes in body composition associated with weight gain are not accompanied by corresponding increases in cardiac dimensions, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation [36]. Another consideration is the unique trajectory of growth during the first years of postnatal life, where metabolism may prioritize somatic and weight growth. Cardiac growth may be slower due to innate differences that we are not aware of at this time, and it is possible that these measurements may redistribute over time at later ages resulting in nonsignificant relationships over a longer study period.

We found that wall thickness measures, in contrast to other measures (e.g., the AV annulus), did not show significant changes with WFAzΔ. One possible hypothesis for this observation is that functional contractile tissue may track alterations in weight gain for age, while more fibrous structural tissues may not. Differing from this was our result for the LVM z-score, which showed an inverse association with WFAzΔ. LVM is derived from two wall thickness measures (IVSd and LVPWd) without significant associations, along with LVIDd, which was significantly negatively associated with WFAzΔ. Given that LVM outcome is calculated rather than directly measured, it is difficult to interpret this negative association.

Other groups have noted difficulties in estimating LVM in children with differing body weight [37]. This is part of a broader challenge with how standard normalization methods perform in children with higher adiposity. As has been shown, normalization using BSA can be suboptimal, leaving a residual association with BMI z-score [38], [39]. Specifically, in children with a high BMI-for-age z-score, BSA-based cardiac z-scores tend to be systematically underestimated. Plante et al. has quantified this effect, demonstrating that this underestimation can be greater than 0.8 z-score units in children with higher BMI compared to lean subjects [38]. It is reasonable to infer that, similar to a high BMI z-score, a high weight gain z-score may parallel an increase in adiposity. Therefore, the inverse associations we observed in our cohort could be at least in part explained by this artifact. This known bias underscores the difficulty in prognostication until a clear understanding of expected versus clinically significant changes in cardiac dimension z-scores relative to a child's weight gain z-score is established and ultimately highlights the need for future research to create scaling methods that more robustly account for differences in body composition between individuals [40]. However, it is important to note that we did not observe significant changes in functional measures (EF and FS) with changes in WFA z-scores.

Sex-stratified analyses suggested some differences in associations between exposures and cardiac measure z-scores. Notably, LA diameter was inversely associated with weight gain z-score in girls but not in boys, while GA was associated with LVM z-scores only in boys. These associations should be interpreted with caution and require further validation in larger cohorts.

4.3 Strengths and limitations

Our large sample size, which includes children from diverse backgrounds, strengthens our findings. It should be noted that a considerable proportion of the children in this cohort had higher body weights, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations of exclusively healthy-weight children. However, given the increasing prevalence of overweight body size, our results may have increasing relevance to general populations moving forward. Additionally, our adjustments for covariates such as maternal education, race/ethnicity, age at delivery, breastfeeding, pre-pregnancy BMI, and alcohol use, factors known to influence cardiometabolic risk and cardiac development, help reduce confounding and enhance our understanding of cardiac growth and development during childhood. We also examined an extensive array of cardiac measurements, including both structural and functional metrics such as aortic root diameter, left atrial size, and left ventricular function indices. This approach allows for a more thorough assessment of cardiovascular characteristics and enhances comparability by integrating a wider range of measurements within a single study.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations. A key consideration is our reliance on M-mode echocardiographic measurements to calculate LVM. Our study includes a subset of the NYU CHES cohort, with mothers enrolled between 2016 and 2021, and echocardiograms performed during follow-up visits across childhood; given the time constraints and limited cooperation typical in young pediatric participants, we chose the linear method via M-mode for its practicality, and feasibility across a large sample. Although widely used in pediatric labs, M-mode is generally considered less precise for LVM assessment compared to the 5/6(area)(length) approach and measurements from other views, such as the apical four chamber view, might yield more accurate measurements of LV volumes and mass. The 2010 American Society of Echocardiography pediatric guidelines recommended 2D measurements for improved accuracy, though the 2024 update acknowledges the continued use of M-mode in many settings [20], [41]. While our reliance on M-mode is consistent with common clinical practices, it represents a limitation when compared to studies utilizing 2D measurements for potentially more refined LVM assessments. We further acknowledge that this methodological approach may limit the ability of future studies to directly verify, reproduce, or compare our LVM findings using current 2D or 3D volumetric echocardiographic methods. Additionally, we opted for within-population Z-scores due to challenges in finding external references in comparable cohorts that were fully compatible with our measurements; many existing standards lacked comprehensive parameter coverage, used different echocardiographic techniques, or were comprised of children whose age, race/ethnicity, and BMI were dissimilar from ours. As a result, our approach ensures that the Z-scores are well-aligned with the specific methods and population of our study, even as pediatric echocardiographic standards continue to evolve. Our analysis does not explore the influence of maternal prenatal comorbidities, maternal or participant lifestyle, hormonal levels, or exposures to environmental toxins that may all affect growth trajectories or cardiac development. Future research should aim to evaluate these factors to better explain early cardiac structural changes. In addition, anatomical factors such as chest wall conformation were not assessed in our cohort; while extrinsic effects of thoracic geometry on cardiac chamber dimensions have been described in patients with chest wall deformities [42], [43], whether more subtle variation in thoracic shape influences cardiac size in children without such deformities remains an area for future investigation. Moreover, while we relied on weight gain z-score as a proxy for body composition, using modalities such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, could enhance the characterization of fat and lean mass and improve the assessment of their independent contributions to cardiac development.

4.4 Conclusion

In conclusion, in our study of a general pediatric population, we found that postnatal weight gain was more consistently associated with cardiac structure z-scores than anthropometric characteristics at birth. Specifically, we observed that in our cohort of children aged 0–7 years, higher weight gain z-score, akin to jumping growth curve percentiles, had associated decrease in relative size of cardiac structures, particularly in the aortic root, ascending aorta, and left ventricular mass. Further research should explore whether these associations persist into later life and their potential impact on long-term cardiovascular health.
