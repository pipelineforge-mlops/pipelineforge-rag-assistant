Background

Plasma cell-free DNA (cfDNA)-based chromosomal instability (CIN) profiling is a non-invasive approach for tumor burden monitoring and treatment response assessment. This pilot study included 50 cancer patients (breast, oral, gastric, lung, and other cancers) to evaluate the clinical utility of the Ultrasensitive Chromosome Aberration Detection (UCAD) method, with exploratory analysis of minimal residual disease (MRD) detection in patients with no evidence of disease on imaging.

Methods

Fifty patients with histologically confirmed solid tumors who underwent UCAD testing between January 2023 and March 2024 were enrolled. UCAD uses low-depth (1×) whole-genome sequencing of plasma cfDNA to detect genome-wide copy number variations (CNVs). Tumor-derived CNVs are distinguished from germline variation through normalization against healthy donor controls, with statistically significant deviations identified as somatic events reflecting tumor-associated CIN. A tumor fraction index (TFx) is derived as a quantitative metric of the aggregate CNV burden. Patients were stratified by imaging status into no-evidence-of-disease (NED, n=41) and residual lesions (RL, n=9) subgroups. Outcomes included TFx correlation with clinical status and exploratory MRD-recurrence associations within the NED subgroup.

Results

TFx values ranged from 0.021% to 10.12% (median 0.065%) across the cohort. TFx positively correlated with tumor stage (ρ=0.463, P<0.001) and negatively correlated with treatment response (P<0.01). Among 41 NED patients (median follow-up 22 months), elevated TFx (≥0.1%) was observed in patients with subsequent recurrence, including one case with TFx of 0.102% preceding bone metastasis by 3 months. In 9 RL patients, TFx tracked with disease burden and decreased following effective therapy.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that the UCAD method showed preliminary feasibility across the evaluated cancer types in this pilot cohort, and the TFx value serves as a quantitative indicator of tumor burden and treatment efficacy, warranting further prospective validation in larger cohorts.

Introduction

Cancer has become the second leading cause of mortality worldwide. According to data from the World Health Organization’s International Agency for Research (IARC) on Cancer, there were approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths worldwide in 2022.1 In China, cancer has become the primary cause of mortality, with over 4.5 million new cases and 2.5 million deaths annually, imposing a significant burden on families and society.2 With advancements in medical technology, cancer treatment has evolved from traditional methods like surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy to the era of precision medicine, which includes targeted therapy and immunotherapy. However, post-treatment recurrence and metastasis remain significant clinical challenges.

Minimal residual disease (MRD) refers to the minute quantity of cancer cells or their molecular markers that persist in the body after patients undergo curative-intent treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy. The number of these residual cells is extremely low, making them undetectable by conventional imaging modalities (eg, CT, MRI, PET/CT) or routine laboratory tests.3 Studies have shown that even early-stage cancer patients face a 30–50% risk of recurrence post-treatment, which is closely associated with the presence of MRD.4,5 Therefore, the precise and sensitive detection of MRD is of paramount importance for evaluating treatment efficacy, predicting recurrence risk, and guiding subsequent therapeutic strategies.

Traditional MRD detection methods, including imaging studies, tumor marker assays, and tissue biopsies, each have their limitations. CT scans have limited resolution and may fail to identify millimeter-scale lesions. Tumor markers often exhibit low specificity and sensitivity, necessitating their combined use to enhance diagnostic performance.6–8 Tissue biopsy is invasive and not well-suited for repeated sampling. In recent years, the emergence of liquid biopsy technology has offered a new paradigm for MRD detection. Liquid biopsy primarily analyzes two types of circulating biomarkers: circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and circulating tumor cells (CTCs). ctDNA consists of fragmented DNA released into the bloodstream primarily through tumor cell apoptosis, necrosis, and active secretion, whereas CTCs are intact tumor cells that detach from the primary tumor and enter the circulation. Both are associated with metastasis, but through different mechanisms: CTCs represent the “seeds” of metastasis that may directly colonize distant sites, while ctDNA serves as a molecular biomarker reflecting tumor burden and genomic characteristics. CTCs are thought to be more directly linked to the metastatic process as they are the physical vehicles of dissemination, but their detection is technically challenging due to extremely low abundance. ctDNA is more abundant in circulation and easier to detect, making it more practical for routine MRD monitoring.9,10

The Ultrasensitive Chromosome Aberration Detection (UCAD) method, developed and optimized by the Hongyuan Biotech team, is a liquid biopsy technique based on high-throughput sequencing. By detecting molecular features like copy number variations (CNVs) in plasma cell-free DNA (cfDNA), this method facilitates early screening, efficacy assessment, and recurrence monitoring for various cancers. Compared to conventional liquid biopsy methods, the UCAD method demonstrates higher sensitivity and specificity and requires only a small volume of peripheral blood, significantly enhancing its clinical applicability.11–14 For instance, a UCAD study on metastatic breast cancer revealed that patients with a high chromosomal instability (CIN) score had a median overall survival of 21.2 months (95% CI 14.1–28.3), which was significantly shorter than that of patients with a low CIN score (P = 0.006). Regardless of the treatment regimen, the median progression-free survival for patients with a high CIN score was 7.3 months, markedly worse than the 11.0 months observed in the low CIN score group (P = 0.034).15 Subsequent studies by the Hongyuan Biotech team and collaborators have further validated this LP-WGS approach in hepatocellular carcinoma,16 BRCA1-mutated breast cancer,17 early lung cancer,18 neuroblastoma,19 and B-cell lymphoma.20

Although the UCAD method has demonstrated excellent performance in clinical trials, its value in real-world clinical settings requires further validation. Real-world studies, which collect diverse data from actual clinical, community, or home environments, evaluate the true impact of therapeutic interventions on patient health. They reflect the effectiveness of medical interventions in practical clinical scenarios and serve as a crucial supplement to evidence from randomized controlled trials.21 This study aims to investigate the application value of the UCAD method for MRD detection in a real-world context by analyzing clinical data from 50 patients who underwent UCAD testing, thereby providing real-world pilot evidence to support further prospective validation.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

A total of 50 patients who were treated at our hospital and underwent UCAD testing between January 2023 and March 2024 were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed malignant tumor; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) history of receiving surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other anti-tumor treatments; and (4) voluntary participation with signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pregnancy or lactation; (2) presence of other severe systemic diseases; and (3) diagnosis of a hematological malignancy.

Sample Collection and Processing

For each participant, 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected in specialized cfDNA collection tubes and transported to the laboratory within 2 hours of collection. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1600 g for 10 minutes at 4°C to separate the upper plasma layer, which was then centrifuged again at 16,000 g for 10 minutes to remove residual cellular debris. Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from the plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions strictly. The concentration of the extracted cfDNA was measured using a Qubit fluorometer, and its quality was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.

UCAD Detection Procedure

The UCAD detection was performed according to the standardized operating procedures established by the Hongyuan Biotech team. The main steps included: (1) cfDNA Library Construction: cfDNA libraries were constructed from the extracted cfDNA using a specially optimized low-input WGS kit, including end-repair, A-tailing, and ligation of sequencing adapters. (2) High-Throughput Sequencing: Sequencing was performed on a DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer (MGI) to an average depth of 1×. (3) Bioinformatics Analysis: After quality control, alignment, and deduplication of the raw data, the UCAD algorithm was used to analyze chromosomal copy number variations and calculate the TFx value.

Technical note on analytical sensitivity: Unlike mutation-based MRD assays requiring >10,000× sequencing depth to detect variant allele frequencies <0.1%, UCAD detects MRD by aggregating genome-wide CNV signals across approximately 15,000 genomic bins (200-kb non-overlapping windows). At ~1× coverage, the limit of detection (LOD) for TFx is approximately 0.03–0.05%, based on internal validation using dilution series of tumor-derived DNA into healthy plasma (unpublished validation data, Hongyuan Biotech). The sensitivity is thus derived from the breadth of genomic coverage and the cumulative statistical power of detecting broad aneuploidy patterns, rather than from per-base read depth.

Algorithmic workflow: The bioinformatics pipeline followed standardized procedures established by the Hongyuan Biotech team.12,14 Briefly: (i) Raw reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using BWA-MEM, followed by removal of low-quality reads, duplicates, and reads mapping to blacklisted regions (ENCODE); (ii) The genome was divided into non-overlapping 200-kb bins, and GC-content bias was corrected using a LOESS regression model fitted to the relationship between bin-wise read counts and GC percentage; (iii) Sample-specific read depth profiles were normalized against a panel of healthy donor plasma cfDNA profiles to yield Z-scores: Z = (coverage_raw − mean(coverage_controls)) / stdev(coverage_controls); (iv) Circular binary segmentation (CBS, R package DNAcopy v1.72.0) was applied to identify regions of chromosomal gain (Z > +3) or loss (Z < −3); (v) The tumor fraction index (TFx) was calculated as a composite metric proportional to the sum of absolute log2-copy-number ratios weighted by segment length, calibrated against a training cohort with known tumor fractions (dilution series and matched tumor-normal pairs). The resulting TFx value represents the estimated proportion of tumor-derived DNA in the cfDNA pool.

TFx cutoff derivation: The TFx cutoff values used in this study were based on prior UCAD validation studies. The high-risk threshold of TFx ≥0.1% was established based on studies demonstrating strong association between TFx above this threshold and detectable tumor burden.12,14 The gray-zone threshold of 0.05% was derived from the analytical LOD of the UCAD method at ~1× coverage (~0.03–0.05%). These cutoffs were not derived from the current dataset and were not independently validated, which is acknowledged as a limitation. We selected TFx rather than CIN score or CNV score because TFx provides a continuous, quantitative estimate of tumor fraction that is more directly interpretable in the MRD context; CIN score (an integer count of chromosomal aberrations) is better suited for binary prognostic classification rather than monitoring dynamic changes in tumor burden.

Data Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 and R software (v4.4.2). Given the non-normal distribution of TFx values (confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk test, W=0.166, P<0.001), non-parametric tests were used throughout. Continuous data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) or median (range), and comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U-test, while comparisons among three or more groups used the Kruskal–Wallis H-test. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Categorical data were presented as frequency (percentage). Diagnostic performance was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Distribution of TFx Values

The TFx values for the 50 patients ranged from 0.021% to 10.120%, with a median of 0.065% (IQR: 0.043–0.097%). The distribution was highly right-skewed (Shapiro–Wilk W=0.166, P<0.001). Patients were stratified into three groups based on their TFx values: a high-TFx group (≥0.1%, n=11, 22%), a medium-TFx group (0.05–0.1%, n=18, 36%), and a low-TFx group (<0.05%, n=21, 42%). The high-TFx group included one patient with an exceptionally high TFx value (10.120%, stage IV gastric cancer with liver metastases), while the remaining 10 patients in this group had TFx values ranging from 0.100% to 1.150%.

Differences were observed in TFx value distributions across cancer types (Kruskal–Wallis H=11.51, P=0.042): gastric cancer patients had the highest median TFx value [0.096% (IQR: 0.074–0.182%)], followed by lung cancer [0.095% (IQR: 0.064–0.226%)], while breast cancer [0.049% (IQR: 0.042–0.092%)] and oral cancer [0.048% (IQR: 0.037–0.055%)] had relatively lower values (Figure 1). This difference reached statistical significance; however, given the small and unequal subgroup sizes, this result should be interpreted cautiously. The outlier patient (Stage IV gastric cancer, TFx=10.120%) substantially influenced the overall distribution. Analysis of TFx values by clinical stage revealed a progressive increase: Stage I [median 0.044% (range: 0.028–0.100%)], Stage II [median 0.055% (range: 0.021–0.103%)], Stage III [median 0.104% (range: 0.035–1.150%)], and Stage IV [median 0.090% (range: 0.048–10.120%)]. Patients with TFx ≥0.1% were predominantly those with advanced-stage or progressive disease. In contrast, patients with TFx <0.05% were mostly in early stages or had achieved complete remission after treatment.

Correlation Between UCAD Results and Clinicopathological Features

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis showed a positive correlation between TFx value and tumor stage (ρ = 0.463, P < 0.001) and a positive correlation with treatment response severity (ρ = 0.489, P < 0.001), where worse treatment response (coded as CR=1, PR=2, SD=3, PD=4) was associated with higher TFx values. Patients who responded to treatment (CR or PR) had significantly lower TFx values compared to those with progressive disease [median 0.053% (IQR: 0.041–0.086%) vs 0.102% (IQR: 0.101–0.159%), P = 0.003, Mann–Whitney U-test]. Additionally, patients with NED on imaging had significantly lower TFx values than those with residual lesions [median 0.050% (IQR: 0.041–0.085%) vs 0.102% (IQR: 0.085–0.350%), P = 0.009]. A sensitivity analysis excluding the outlier patient (TFx=10.120%) confirmed that the correlations with stage (ρ = 0.434, P = 0.002) and treatment response (ρ = 0.449, P = 0.001) remained statistically significant.

Recurrence Analysis and Predictive Value of TFx

During the follow-up period, 10 patients (20%) experienced disease recurrence. The recurrence rate varied by cancer type: gastric cancer 3/8 (38%), lung cancer 2/7 (29%), breast cancer 3/16 (19%), oral tumor 1/2 (50%), and cancer of unknown primary 1/1 (100%). No recurrences were observed among oral cancer (0/13), nasopharyngeal cancer (0/2), or multiple myeloma (0/1) patients during the follow-up period. Patients who recurred had significantly higher baseline TFx values than those who did not [median 0.122% (range: 0.048–10.120%) vs 0.050% (range: 0.021–0.350%), P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U-test].

Among the 5 patients with NED on imaging and TFx ≥0.1%, 4 (80%) subsequently experienced disease recurrence, while 1 (20%) remained recurrence-free at 24 months of follow-up. The false positive case was a breast cancer patient (Stage III, CR at time of testing, TFx=0.104%) who has not recurred during 24 months of follow-up. Among patients with NED imaging and TFx <0.05%, 1 patient (oral tumor Stage IV, TFx=0.048%) subsequently recurred at approximately 7 months, representing a false negative result. Using the TFx ≥0.1% threshold for predicting recurrence in the overall cohort, the sensitivity was 80.0%, specificity 92.5%, PPV 72.7%, and NPV 94.9%.

Illustrative Case Studies

Case 1: A male patient with stage IV gastric cancer and liver metastases had a UCAD-detected TFx value of 10.120%, far exceeding the high-risk threshold. At the time of testing, the patient was experiencing disease progression, with conventional imaging showing enlargement of multiple liver metastases and a significantly elevated serum CEA level. After two cycles of intensive chemotherapy, a follow-up UCAD test showed a decrease in the TFx value to 3.45%, consistent with the partial remission observed on imaging.

Case 2: A 33-year-old female patient who had completed adjuvant immunotherapy for breast cancer post-surgery. Her UCAD-detected TFx value was 0.142%, above the high-risk threshold. Clinically, she was assessed as being in complete remission and was asymptomatic with no detectable lesions on imaging. Given the elevated TFx value, close follow-up was recommended. A repeat examination approximately 11 months later revealed bone metastases, and her TFx value had decreased to 0.057% after treatment. This case suggests that UCAD testing may detect recurrence earlier than conventional imaging.

Case 3: A 25-year-old female patient, 2 years post-surgery for breast cancer, undergoing regular follow-up. Her UCAD-detected TFx value was 0.099%, in the gray zone (0.05–0.1%). However, imaging studies showed no abnormalities, and tumor markers were all within the normal range. A repeat UCAD test 2 months later showed that her TFx value had decreased to 0.052%. This transient elevation was considered consistent with non-neoplastic fluctuation rather than true MRD, given the absence of radiological or serological evidence of disease and the spontaneous normalization without anti-tumor therapy. This case underscores the necessity of dynamic monitoring and multi-modal integration when interpreting low-level TFx elevations in the gray zone.

Serial TFx Monitoring

A total of 9 patients (18%) underwent serial UCAD testing with two blood samples per patient, with testing intervals ranging from 1.1 to 23.4 months (median: 4.5 months). Among the 7 non-recurring patients who completed serial monitoring, TFx values exhibited minor fluctuations and remained within the low-to-gray zone range. Specifically, four patients presented slight TFx elevations (0.072%→0.077%, 0.045%→0.063%, 0.048%→0.054%, 0.040%→0.053%), all of which remained below 0.1%, while three patients showed decreased TFx levels (0.050%→0.035%, 0.065%→0.022%, 0.100%→0.048%).

Heterogeneous TFx dynamic changes were observed in the 2 patients who experienced tumor recurrence. One patient with cancer of unknown primary showed an increased TFx from 0.101% to 0.113%. Another breast cancer patient exhibited a TFx decline from 0.142% to 0.057% but subsequently developed bone metastasis; the decreased TFx was presumably attributed to the anti-tumor treatment administered during the 10.9-month interval between the two tests. All serial monitoring data are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

By analyzing real-world data from 50 cancer patients who underwent UCAD testing, this pilot study investigated the preliminary application value of this method in MRD detection. The results suggest that the UCAD method demonstrates preliminary feasibility for MRD detection across the evaluated cancer types in this cohort. The TFx value showed correlations with tumor burden, clinical stage, and treatment response, positioning it as a potentially valuable tool in cancer management that requires further prospective validation.

The UCAD method offers distinct advantages over traditional MRD detection methods. First, its tissue-agnostic approach enables pan-cancer application without requiring prior tumor tissue sequencing, unlike tumor-informed NGS assays (eg, Signatera, Guardant Reveal) that design patient-specific mutation panels from tumor tissue. While tumor-informed assays achieve higher sensitivity (LOD ~0.01%), they are more costly and logistically complex. UCAD’s LP-WGS approach at ~1× coverage provides a cost-effective and accessible alternative with an LOD of ~0.03–0.05%. Second, compared to methylation-based ctDNA assays that require tumor-type-specific classifiers, UCAD is applicable across cancer types without such constraints. Third, compared to CIN score-based approaches that provide binary prognostic classification, the TFx value offers continuous quantitative monitoring more suitable for MRD assessment and treatment response tracking.

In our study, 5 patients with NED on imaging had TFx ≥0.1%, of whom 4 subsequently recurred. This is consistent with findings from Chaudhuri et al, who found that 94% of relapsing patients were ctDNA-positive in their first postoperative blood sample, with a median lead time of 5.2 months over imaging-based detection.22 However, we acknowledge that head-to-head comparison studies between UCAD and other ctDNA MRD assays are not yet available and represent a priority for future research.

The TFx value, as a quantitative metric, demonstrated significant clinical utility in this study. We found a positive correlation between the TFx value and tumor stage, with late-stage patients exhibiting significantly higher TFx values than early-stage patients. This aligns with the findings of Bettegowda et al, as advanced tumors have a larger burden and shed more ctDNA.23 Furthermore, dynamic changes in the TFx value were closely associated with treatment efficacy: patients who responded to therapy showed a significant decrease in TFx, whereas those with progressive disease often had elevated levels. This indicates that the TFx value can serve as a supplementary marker for assessing treatment response, particularly for evaluating early responses to targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

Interpretive framework for TFx values: A critical challenge in the clinical interpretation of low-level TFx elevations is distinguishing true MRD from non-neoplastic “noise”. We propose the following interpretive framework: (1) Dynamic trend over static value: A single TFx measurement in the 0.05–0.1% range (gray zone) should not be interpreted in isolation. A sustained rise on serial testing strongly supports MRD, whereas a transient elevation that normalizes on repeat testing (as in Case 3) favors non-neoplastic confounders such as subclinical inflammation, tissue repair, or clonal hematopoiesis. (2) Correlation with conventional biomarkers: TFx interpretation must be integrated with imaging, serum tumor markers, and clinical context. (3) Threshold-based risk stratification: We define TFx ≥0.1% as high-risk (strongly associated with residual or recurrent disease; PPV 72.7% in this cohort); 0.05%–0.1% as intermediate-risk requiring confirmatory retesting within 4–8 weeks; and <0.05% as low-risk (NPV 94.9%). (4) Exclusion of confounders: Active infection, recent surgery, trauma, or autoimmune flare-up are recognized as potential sources of transient chromosomal instability signal in cfDNA.24,25

Limitations of the UCAD system: Several important limitations of the UCAD method should be acknowledged. First, false positives can occur: inflammation, tissue repair, and clonal hematopoiesis may produce low-level CNV signals that elevate TFx without true MRD, as illustrated by Case 3. In our cohort, 1 of 5 NED-imaging patients with TFx ≥0.1% was a false positive (20%). Second, false negatives also occur: 1 patient with TFx=0.048% (below the 0.05% threshold) subsequently recurred, indicating that low TFx does not entirely exclude MRD. Third, the UCAD method relies on CNV detection and is inherently limited in detecting balanced chromosomal translocations or gene fusions that do not alter copy number. For tumors driven primarily by balanced translocations (eg, certain lymphomas, EML4-ALK-positive lung cancer), UCAD may underestimate tumor fraction. Fourth, tumors with low ctDNA shedding (eg, CNS tumors, indolent tumors) may produce undetectable TFx signals even in the presence of MRD.26,27 Fifth, the LOD of ~0.03–0.05% may be insufficient for detecting very low-level MRD compared to error-corrected NGS methods with LOD <0.01%.

Notably, TFx values differed among cancer types in this cohort, with gastric cancer patients having the highest median TFx. This may be related to the biological characteristics of gastric cancer, as studies have shown that its cells have a higher capacity for ctDNA shedding, and it is often diagnosed at an advanced stage with a larger tumor burden. However, we emphasize that these subgroup observations are exploratory given the small sample sizes (gastric cancer, n=8; lung cancer, n=7). The Kruskal–Wallis test excluding single-patient cancer types was only marginally significant (H=11.51, P=0.042), and this result should be interpreted cautiously given the small and unequal subgroup sizes.

Study limitations: This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of 50 patients is small and insufficient for definitive cancer type-specific analyses or establishment of optimal cutoff values. Second, this is a single-center retrospective study without an independent validation cohort. Third, the cohort included a heterogeneous mix of cancer types with different biological behaviors and ctDNA shedding profiles; while this reflects real-world clinical practice, it complicates MRD-specific interpretations. Fourth, our cohort included patients with residual imaging findings alongside NED patients, which differs from traditional MRD study designs that restrict enrollment to patients with no evidence of disease on imaging. Fifth, the time from treatment completion to sampling was variable (range: 1–900 days), which may confound TFx interpretation as post-surgical inflammation can temporarily elevate TFx. Sixth, serial testing was available for only 9 patients (18%), limiting our ability to draw robust conclusions about TFx dynamics. Seventh, the TFx cutoff values were derived from prior studies and were not validated in the current cohort. Eighth, the UCAD method cannot detect balanced translocations. Ninth, potential confounding by clonal hematopoiesis was not systematically assessed.

Future research should focus on: (1) expanding the sample size and conducting multi-center, prospective studies to validate the clinical utility of UCAD; (2) establishing cancer-specific TFx cutoff values through predefined endpoint studies; (3) conducting head-to-head comparison studies with other ctDNA MRD assays; (4) integrating multi-omics information to enhance detection accuracy and specificity; (5) conducting health economic evaluations; and (6) investigating the role of UCAD in predicting treatment response and resistance.

In conclusion, this pilot real-world study provides preliminary evidence that the UCAD method may have utility in MRD detection across multiple solid tumor types. The TFx value showed correlations with tumor stage and treatment response, and demonstrated preliminary predictive value for recurrence. However, these findings are limited by the small sample size (n=50), single-center design, heterogeneous cancer types, and lack of an independent validation cohort. The TFx cutoff values require prospective validation in larger, cancer-specific cohorts. Future multi-center, prospective studies with predefined endpoints are needed to establish the clinical utility and optimal implementation of UCAD-based MRD monitoring.
