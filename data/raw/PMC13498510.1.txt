Introduction

Elevated lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)) and Lp(a)-raising genetic variants (e.g. rs3798220) are independent cardiovascular risk factors lacking preventive strategies. Given the prothrombotic properties attributed to high Lp(a), aspirin was hypothesized to confer benefit in primary prevention. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the impact of aspirin on cardiovascular and bleeding outcomes in this population.

Methods

MEDLINE, Web of Science and CENTRAL were searched (November 2025) for randomized and observational studies assessing aspirin use in primary prevention among individuals with Lp(a) ≥ 50 mg/dL or Lp(a)-associated genetic variants. The primary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Secondary outcomes included myocardial infarction (MI), coronary artery disease (CAD), cardiovascular mortality, and bleeding. Random-effects meta-analyses pooled the Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.

Results

Seven studies including 6498 participants met inclusion criteria. Aspirin was not associated with a reduction in MACE (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.79–1.24; I2 = 23%; four studies). MACE reduction was associated with aspirin in rs3798220-C carriers (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.19–0.77, two studies). Aspirin was also associated with significant reduction of events regarding MI (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.88, two studies) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.28–0.83, one study), whereas CAD was not significantly reduced (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.59–1.12). Bleeding risk was numerically higher but not statistically significant (HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.89–1.44). Overall certainty of evidence was very low.

Conclusions

Aspirin was not associated with a reduction of MACE among individuals with elevated Lp(a). A potential benefit for MI requires confirmation in adequately designed and powered prospective studies. Pooled data from rs3798220-C carriers suggest a potential significant benefit that warrants further investigation

Key Points

Aspirin use in primary prevention does not significantly reduce overall MACE for individuals with elevated Lp(a).	
Given the low certainty of current evidence, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.	
Further well-designed studies are required to confirm these effects and assess bleeding risks.	

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a distinct low-density lipoprotein-like particle consisting of two major apoproteins covalently bound: apolipoprotein B-100 and apolipoprotein (a) [1]. The distinct variations in Lp(a) concentration in human plasma can be attributed mainly to apo(a) isoform size heterogeneity, which is genetically determined [2]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (rs10455872 and rs3798220) are associated with less kringle IV type 2 (KIV-2) repeats, that inversely correlate with Lp(a) levels [3].

Higher levels of Lp(a) contribute to atherogenesis through mechanisms involving inflammatory response, endothelial dysfunction and increased plaque vulnerability [4].

Therefore, the role of Lp(a) in primary prevention of cardiovascular disease has gained attention over the past few decades owing to its causative role in the development of atherosclerosis, in particular coronary artery disease [5]. Despite the correlation between high Lp(a) levels and cardiovascular risk, the therapeutic options for lowering Lp(a) are currently limited and controversial. LDL-C lowering therapy is recommended for individuals reclassified into higher risk strata as risk management strategy [6].

Aspirin use in primary prevention may be considered for patients at higher risk of cardiovascular events, taking into account that in these patients, the benefits might overcome the bleeding risks [7, 8]. Considering this, we aimed to systematically review the clinical studies evaluating aspirin regarding cardiovascular and bleeding events in patients with high Lp(a) levels or with genetic polymorphisms leading to increased Lp(a).

Methods

This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024520731) and followed the PRISMA guidelines [9].

Eligibility Criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCT), prospective or retrospective cohort studies and case–control studies, providing data of aspirin use or exposure in patients in primary prevention of cardiovascular disease with increased lipoprotein(a) levels (defined as > 50 mg/dL) or with genetic mutations or genetic risk scores which increase the risk of having high levels of Lp(a). Studies had to report any of the clinical outcomes of interest. The primary outcome was the composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular death). The secondary outcomes were bleeding events, all-cause mortality, coronary artery disease and each component of the primary outcome.

Information Sources and Search Strategies

An electronic search was performed through MEDLINE, Web of Science Core Collection and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) with the last updated search in November 2025. No language or publication date restrictions were applied. The search keywords used terms related to ‘aspirin’ and ‘lipoprotein(a)’ and their common variations. The keyword strategy is depicted in the Supplementary Data.

Study Selection and Data Collection

The records obtained from the electronic search were evaluated in two stages: (1) title and abstract screening; (2) full-text assessment. The title and abstract screening were performed independently by two reviewers. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and assessed independently by three reviewers. In both stages, conflicts were solved by a different reviewer. The reasons for the exclusion of articles at this stage were recorded.

A standardized data extraction form was used to retrieve the data, independently, by at least two of all reviewers. Conflicts were solved by a third reviewer.

The following data were retrieved: study identification and overall characteristics (title, authors, year of publication, design, region), definition of high Lp(a) levels or high risk/genetic predisposition of Lp(a) polymorphisms, total sample, exposed/treated with aspirin, control (standard of care, placebo or no treatment), age, proportion of women/men, comorbidities, outcomes (non-adjusted and adjusted if available) and follow-up period.

Risk of Bias

For risk of bias assessment of randomized control trials, two reviewers used independently, the Cochrane collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias for RCT, RoB 2.0. The domains of analysis of this tool were the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result [10].

The risk of bias of observational studies was independently evaluated by two authors using the ROBINS-I tool, and assessed the following domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of intervention, deviations from intervention, missing data, measurement of outcome and selection of reported results. These domains were qualitatively classified as at critical, serious, moderate, or low risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each observational study was divided as critical or non-critical, following ROBINS-I criteria [11].

Synthesis Method and Publication Bias

The data were organized according to the outcomes of interest. Whenever a study reported different estimates for the same outcome for different thresholds of Lp(a) or genetic predisposition, we prioritized the population with Lp(a) > 50 mg/dL (whichever the closest higher threshold) and then the genetic polymorphisms and at last genetic risk score. Also, if direct estimates for aspirin versus no aspirin in the population of interest were not available, we used the Bucher method for indirect treatment comparison if data were available for other comparisons (e.g. Estimates of increased Lp(a) versus no increased Lp(a) in patients treated with aspirin, and estimates of increased Lp(a) versus no increased Lp(a) in patients not treated with aspirin)—Supplementary Fig 1.

STATA 18.0 was used for statistical analysis and to derive forest plots. We used a random-effects model through the restricted maximum likelihood method to pool data. We reported pooled data using the reported hazard (HR) or risk ratio (RR) with the correspondent 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The reported overall estimates for each outcome were provided with the 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2. Heterogeneity was considered to be relevant if I2 > 50%.

Publication/small study effect bias assessment was performed through funnel plot examination assuming that at least 10 studies were included in the analysis. Egger’s and Begg’s test were performed to ascertain/rule out the small study effect bias.

Exploratory analyses were performed with the different populations of patients included. Exploratory jackknife leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results [12].

Assessment of Certainty of Generated Evidence

We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to report the overall quality of evidence. The certainty in the evidence for each outcome was graded as high, moderate, low or very low [13].

Results

The database search yielded 230 records, and after screening and full-text assessment, seven studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The selected studies included one nested case–control study (PHS), four cohort studies (ARIC, CRIC, MESA and NHANES III) and two subgroup analysis of RCT (ASPREE and WHS) [14–19].Fig. 1 Flowchart of studies selection

The ASPREE trial reported estimates for the population carrier of rs3798220-C variant (heterozygotic or homozygotic patients) and for the population with the highest quintile of the 43-variant lipoprotein(a) genetic risk score (LPA-GRS). The primary analysis was done using the data with the rs3798220-C variant.

The cardiovascular outcomes data from the ARIC and MESA cohorts were retrieved from one article (Colantonio et al.) [16]. This article also reported data from a third cohort, the Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS), in which lipoprotein(a) was quantified using an older monoclonal-antibody ELISA that measured only the protein moiety of the Lp(a) particle, rather than whole lipoprotein(a) mass concentration. Colantonio et al. defined high Lp(a) in CHS using a percentile-based calibration (≥8.8 mg/dL of Lp(a) protein, corresponding to the 85th percentile in White participants without CVD, analogous to the 50 mg/dL whole-mass threshold in ARIC and MESA). Because our eligibility criterion required Lp(a) mass concentration ≥50 mg/dL (or its closest equivalent on a comparable assay), and a direct conversion between Lp(a) protein concentration and whole Lp(a) mass concentration is not available, CHS was not included in the present meta-analysis.

Despite the presence of different estimates for MACE in the MESA study (Colantonio et al. and Bhatia et al.), we chose the first estimate because the risk of bias was deemed to be lower. The bleeding estimates of the MESA study was only available from the article by Bhatia et al. [20].

The maximum number of patients with high Lp(a) levels or genetic predisposition was 6498. The number varied according to the population used in the ASPREE (4348 for the primary analysis when the group of rs3798220-C carriers was used in ASPREE; and 6498 when the genetic score was used in the same study in the secondary analysis).

Five studies (MESA, PHS, ARIC, NHANES III and CRIC) used Lp(a) levels as a threshold. In contrast, the other two included studies (WHS and ASPREE) used genetic predisposition, such as the rs3798220-C variant/4399Met allele. The WHS study also provided data for rs3798220-C carrier and Lp(a) higher than 65 mg/dL (which was used for the main analysis). ASPREE also had data from patients with high lipoprotein(a) genetic risk score (LPA-GRS).

Aspirin posology varied across studies and was sometimes inadequately documented. In the MESA study, NHANES IIII, and CRIC, aspirin use (dose unspecified) was self-reported, whereas in the PHS study, a fixed dosage of 325 mg every other day was prescribed.

Risk of Bias

The risk of bias of the RCT were considered to have some concerns, mainly owing to the post-hoc subgroup nature of the analysis of both WHS and ASPREE (Table 1). Regarding the observational studies, all of them were considered to have moderate residual cofounding risk bias (Table 2). For both PHS and ARIC studies, the absence of missing data from aspirin versus control in non-elevated Lp(a) participants, resulted in the data having to be extrapolated, assuming that the results of the full trial were similar to non-elevated Lp(a) participants, which determined that these studies have high risk of bias. Meta-Analysis

Primary Outcome (MACE) and Secondary Outcomes

The primary analysis focused on MACE, with four studies not showing a significant association between aspirin intake and decreased MACE, with an HR of 0.99 [95% CI 0.79, 1.24], without substantial statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 23%).Fig. 2 Forest plot with the results of the meta-analyses of different cardiovascular and bleeding outcomes associated with aspirin in patients with elevated or at high risk of having elevated Lp(a) in primary prevention

Cardiovascular mortality was also solely evaluated by one study (NHANES) proving a HR of 0.48 [95% CI 0.28, 0.83] (Fig. 2). For myocardial infarction risk, the PHS study and CRIC study contributed a pooled HR of 0.60, 95% CI 0.41, 0.88). The risk regarding coronary artery disease showed a non-significant association, with four studies contributing to the analysis (HR 0.82 [95% CI 0.59, 1.12]) (Fig. 2). The increase of bleeding events with aspirin was not statistically significant (HR 1.13, [95% CI 0.89, 1.44]) among three studies (ASPREE, MESA and CRIC) (Fig. 2).

No significant heterogeneity was found across the analyses, although CAD showed a low-to-moderate index (I2 = 44%).

Secondary/Exploratory Analyses

In a subgroup analysis restricted to carriers of the rs3798220-C variant, the pooled data of the WHS and ASPREE trials showed that aspirin was associated with a statistically significant 61% reduction in MACE (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.19–0.77; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3). The analysis was restricted to studies using Lp(a) concentration thresholds, which showed no association (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.88–1.22; I2 = 0%; Supplementary Fig. 3)Fig. 3 Forest plot with the results of the main analysis of MACE for reference and the secondary analyses

Further exploratory analyses examined the impact of alternative estimates from ASPREE and MESA. Substituting the ASPREE genetic risk score (GRS) estimate into the MACE outcome analysis yielded a pooled HR of 1.01 (95% CI 0.84–1.23), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39%). This analysis did not demonstrate any evidence of benefit. Replacing the Colantonio MESA estimate with the Bhatia MESA estimate (HR 0.54 [0.32–0.93]) did not change the overall pooled estimate, which remained not significant (HR 0.73 [0.48–1.12]) and with moderate statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 67%).

Expanding the analysis to include all ischemic events incorporated additional studies beyond the primary MACE analysis, the pooled random-effects estimate showed a non-significant pooled HR of 0.75 (95% CI 0.54–1.04), with moderate statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 68%).

The jackknife leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome MACE revealed that the pooled effect remained stable across exclusions, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the relationship between aspirin treatment and primary prevention of cardiovascular events among individuals with elevated Lp(a) levels or genetic predisposition to high Lp(a). Contrary to prior hypotheses, our primary analysis indicates no significant overall benefit of aspirin in reducing MACE (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.79–1.24) or CAD (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.59–1.12) in this population. However, secondary analyses showed that aspirin was associated with reduced myocardial infarction (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.88) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.28–0.83) raising a potential signal of benefit. Additionally, the subgroup analysis of rs3798220-C carriers showed a significant 61% reduction in MACE (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.19–0.77), suggesting that a genetically defined prothrombotic phenotype may identify individuals most likely to benefit from aspirin in primary prevention.

The results of our systematic review fit into a wider body of research, suggesting that the role for aspirin in the prevention of cardiovascular disease should be considered very cautiously in patients with higher risk of cardiovascular events and low bleeding risk [7, 8, 21]. Prior large meta-analyses published in 2019 consistently reported modest but significant relative risk reductions of approximately 11% for composite cardiovascular events; 15% for myocardial infarction, and 19% for ischaemic stroke [22]. While our findings do not replicate the benefit for composite events (MACE), they do align with the observed reductions in myocardial infarction. These benefits must be carefully weighed against the potential for bleeding, which, although not significantly increased in our analysis (HR 1.13), remains an essential consideration in shared decision-making. This perspective aligns with emerging evidence that cardiovascular risk modifiers such as Lp(a) may justify more tailored preventive strategies [23].

Our pooled findings for MACE in the main analysis are supported by the inclusion of recent observational data, particularly a recent multicohort study with uniformized methodology, that reported no association of aspirin use with significant benefit in individuals with high Lp(a), nor any meaningful difference between individuals with high versus low Lp(a). This analysis integrated publicly available data from adults without established CVD enrolled in ARIC (baseline 1987–1989), CHS (1989–1993) and MESA (2000–2002). The CHS was not included in the analysis because the threshold for considering high Lp(a) levels was 8.8 mg/dL. After adjustment, including 1:1 propensity score matching, multivariable models, inverse probability weighting and multiple sensitivity analyses accounting for multiple potential confounders; the hazard ratios associated with aspirin use were neutral [16, 24].

These findings illustrate that the evidence remains inconclusive and that aspirin should not be routinely recommended for all individuals with elevated Lp(a). Instead, the priority is to identify those most likely to benefit from its specific effects on myocardial infarction. For example, the CRIC study, conducted in patients with chronic kidney disease, showed that individuals with CKD and elevated Lp(a) but without established CVD may derive net benefit from low-dose aspirin for primary prevention of myocardial infarction and progression to ESRD—suggesting that clinical context strongly influences potential benefit [14].

Another important consideration is that findings derived from the small and genetically selected group of rs3798220-C carriers may not be extrapolated to all individuals with elevated Lp(a). This variant represents a relatively uncommon genetic subtype, whereas elevated Lp(a) levels are much more widely distributed in the general population. Therefore, pleiotropic effects of the rs3798220-C variant, beyond its impact on Lp(a) concentration, cannot be excluded. Supporting this distinction, evidence from large primary prevention trials shows a disconnect between genetic and biochemical definitions of elevated Lp(a). In the pooled analysis of both WHS and ASPREE subgroups, aspirin was associated with a significantly reduced risk of MACE with a hazard ratio of 0.39 (95% CI 0.19-0.77) among rs3798220-C carriers. However, the small sample sizes in ASPREE and WHS, the low event rates and the post-hoc nature of both analyses substantially limit the certainty of this observation (low in GRADE). It is also important to emphasize that rs3798220-C carriers represent a rare genetic subtype and that these findings cannot be extrapolated to all individuals with elevated Lp(a).

In this context, the mechanistic findings by Gurevitz et al. added that carriers of the rs3798220-C variant had a more prothrombotic profile with significantly shorter coagulation times and increased platelet aggregation. The authors concluded that thrombogenicity tends to be higher in patients with elevated Lp(a), particularly among rs3798220-C carriers, providing a plausible biological mechanism through which aspirin might confer selective benefit in a genetically defined subgroup [25]. This hypothesis-generating observation warrants confirmation in adequately powered, prospectively designed studies in genetically defined populations.

As for Lp(a), clinical guidelines, including the 2025 ESC/EAS dyslipidaemia update, recommend measuring Lp(a) at least once in a lifetime, considering levels ≥ 50 mg/dL as a risk-enhancing factor that may reclassify individuals into higher-risk categories, alter treatment thresholds, and prompt cascade screening [26]. Despite the ongoing uncertainties, Shapiro and Ruscica considered that aspirin may represent a pragmatic “bridge therapy” for highly selected individuals with markedly elevated Lp(a) while dedicated pharmacological treatments are awaited [24]. Nevertheless, a focus on Lp(a) must not detract from the essential foundation of cardiovascular prevention: aggressive optimisation of traditional risk factors, particularly LDL-cholesterol, blood pressure, and glycaemic control [24].

In addition to these clinical and genetic considerations, several methodological issues within the existing evidence base warrant careful interpretation. The included studies span markedly different designs—from randomized trials or trial emulations to observational cohorts and case–control analyses—which may contribute to divergent estimates of aspirin’s effects. Observational studies relying on “prevalent users,” such as those conducted in MESA and NHANES III, are particularly limited because the timing and indication for aspirin initiation are unknown [15, 16]. As a result, covariates used for adjustment may not reflect conditions at treatment start, reducing the ability to control for confounding by indication. Moreover, prevalent users represent individuals who have tolerated aspirin without early adverse events, potentially biasing bleeding estimates. A further concern is statistical overfitting. In several observational analyses, the number of cardiovascular events was substantially lower than the number of covariates included in multivariable models, which may lead to unstable or biased estimates. These methodological constraints add uncertainty to existing findings and underscore the need for prospective studies with well-defined exposure timing, adequate event rates, and harmonised analytical approaches.

Another important limitation relates to methodological heterogeneity across studies. Older studies such as PHS relied on early-generation laboratory assays for measuring Lp(a), which are prone to greater measurement error and therefore limit comparability with more recent analyses that use standardized, isoform-insensitive methods. Such heterogeneity complicates the synthesis of a coherent narrative regarding the efficacy of aspirin across different Lp(a)-defined risk groups and highlights the need for uniform measurement protocols in future research. Moreover, the varying risk of bias observed among included studies reduces the certainty of the overall evidence base and reinforces the need for more rigorous and adequately powered study designs in subsequent investigations.

Implication for practice and research

The results imply a highly tailored, rather than universal, role for aspirin in the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease in individuals with elevated Lp(a), an area currently lacking in effective therapeutic strategies [23]. Given the lack of significant reduction in composite MACE, advocating for the broad integration of aspirin therapy in preventive regimens for all high-Lp(a) patients cannot be supported by current data. In the other hand there is some support for a potential role of aspirin in rs3798220-C carriers in primary prevention. However, the routine prescription of aspirin in any of these contexts depends on the results of future research through randomized controlled trials. These can be helpful to better delineate the risk-benefit profile of aspirin therapy in patients with either high Lp(a) and/or carriers of rs3798220-C in primary prevention. Well-designed studies focusing on larger sample sizes and incorporating adjudicated outcomes, particularly regarding bleeding risks, are essential to refine our understanding and recommendations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review with meta-analysis does not support a broad utility for aspirin in preventing overall MACE in individuals with high Lp(a) levels. The level of evidence and certainty of results is very low. Further research should be conducted in this setting. The positive association of aspirin with decreased risk of CV mortality and MI in patients with high Lp(a) levels as well as MACE reductionin rs3798220-C carriers also warrants further research.
