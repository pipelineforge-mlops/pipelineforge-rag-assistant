Objective

This study revealed the function and mechanism of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) in a rat model of lateral ankle sprain (LAS).

Methods

The LAS rat model was randomly divided into Low/Medium/High intensity of TENS groups, Sham group, and Model control group. After 1, 2, and 3 weeks of treatment, the improvement of LAS rar was measured by gait analysis, morphology, micro-CT, osteogenesis markers, etc. The expressions of IL-1β/CXCL-10/NF-kB of ankle joint were analyzed by PCR, IF, IHC and WB. Furthermore, comprehensive profiling of differential gene expression in the LAS rat model was conducted through transcriptome sequencing. Following intra-articular reinfusion of chondrocytes induced by TENS and the downregulation of CXCL-10 expression, the improvements were analyzed.

Results

After TENS treatment, compared with the model control group, the ankle degeneration score increased in the medium/high intensity of TENS groups (P < 0.05). The degree of inclined plane test increased in the low intensity of TENS group (P < 0.05). The average step length, step frequency increased in the TENS group (P < 0.05). The bone mineral density (BMD)/ bone mineral content (BMC) increased in the TENS groups. The bio-mechanical indicators of LAS rats increased in the TENS groups (P < 0.05). The cartilage thickness of the LAS rat increased in the TENS groups (P < 0.05). There were 7 gene targets of LAS rat that were closely related to the IL-17 signaling pathway; TENS down-regulated the CXCL-10/IL-1β/NF-kB expressions (P < 0.0). After chondrocyte reinfusion which induced with TENS, similar improvements in LAS rats were obtained.

Conclusion

TENS could improve the motor function and promote the osteogenic and chondrogenic effects in LAS rats. TENS inhibited the inflammation by regulating the IL-1β/CXCL-10/NF-kB expression. The chondrocyte reinfusion with low-expressed CXCL-10, which was induced by TENS, improved the LAS rat. TENS, chondrocyte reinfusion and CXCL-10 inhibitor had a potential effect on LAS in the clinic.

Graphical abstract

Significance

In this study, a lateral ankle sprain (LAS) rat model was duplicated and treated with transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS). After a period of treatment with TENS, the improvement of TENS in LAS rats was analyzed. The different regulated gene of articular cartilage was obtained by transcriptome analysis. In vitro, the CXCL-10/NF-kB expressions were analyzed and prepared as an intra-articular injection for LAS rats. Then, the chondrocyte whose CXCL-10/NF-kB expression were down-regulated and its therapeutic improvement was analyzed. The aim of this study is to elucidate the function and mechanism of TENS in LAS rats. This study found that TENS could improve the motor function and promote the osteogenic and chondrogenic effects on LAS rats. TENS inhibited the inflammation by regulating the IL-1β/CXCL-10/NF-kB expression. Reinfusion of chondrocytes with low levels of CXCL-10 yields comparable effects to TENS in LAS rats. Collectively, TENS, demonstrate a promising clinical potential for the treatment of LAS. Furthermore, chondrocyte reinfusion might become a therapeutic approach, substantial technical barriers currently limit its clinical applicability.

Introduction

The ankle joint serves as a crucial load-bearing structure in the human body and is particularly vulnerable to injury, especially among individuals engaged in frequent sports activities [1]. The lateral ligament complex, comprising the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL), and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), plays an essential role in maintaining ankle stability and normal function [2]. Lateral ankle sprain (LAS), which typically involves damage to the ATFL, PTFL, and CFL, is the most common type of ankle sprain [3]. It is characterized by symptoms such as pain, swelling, and gait disturbances, primarily resulting from violent varus or valgus inversion forces [4]. Studies have reported a higher incidence of ankle sprains in females compared to males, as well as in children compared to adolescents, and in adolescents compared to adults [5]. According to epidemiological data, LAS accounts for over 85% of all ankle injuries and often occurs concurrently with CFL injury or combined CFL and PTFL injuries [6]. Cohort studies have indicated that 10–20% of patients with lateral ankle sprains develop persistent symptoms, including pain, swelling, sensory instability, and recurrent ankle sprains [7]. Chronic ankle instability, defined as persistent symptoms lasting more than six months following repeated sprains, can progress to chronic ankle instability (CAI) [8]. Furthermore, unresolved ankle instability may lead to progressive damage of the periarticular soft tissues, aggravate joint dysfunction and pain, and ultimately contribute to cartilage degeneration and the development of ankle osteoarthritis [9]. Given these sequelae, there is an urgent need to explore novel treatment modalities and potential therapeutic targets to effectively address motor function impairments associated with lateral ankle ligament injuries.

Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (TENS) is a non-invasive complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) technique that delivers low-voltage electrical impulses to modulate pain perception [9]. It typically involves the adjustment of parameters such as current intensity, frequency, and duration, which can be customized to individual patient requirements [10]. TENS has shown potential in reducing both intra-operative and post-operative pain, while also mitigating the side effects associated with opioid analgesics [11]. The mechanism of action involves the activation of multiple afferent nerve fibers and the inhibition of dorsal root reflexes in inflamed tissues [12]. Recent studies have demonstrated that TENS can suppress inflammation, enhance tissue repair, and lower serum levels of inflammatory biomarkers following hip replacement surgery [13]. Furthermore, TENS has been reported to promote fracture healing by improving local blood flow and cellular metabolism, thereby supporting the proliferation and differentiation of osteogenic cells [14].

LAS patients often experience persistent symptoms, including recurrent injury, functional loss, and the development of CAI [15]. LAS is associated with complex pathological processes involving inflammation, soft-tissue repair, and remodeling of bone and cartilage. Inflammation, particularly in osteoarthritic cartilage, is typically characterized by a chronic, low-grade profile and is often mediated by the nuclear factor kB (NF-kB) signaling pathway [16]. NF-kB is a heterodimeric transcription factor composed of p50 and RelA (p65) subunits, which is predominantly sequestered in the cytoplasm through interaction with its inhibitor, IκB-α. Upon injury, activation of the NF-kB pathway occurs via the phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and proteasomal degradation of IκB-α, allowing the transcription factor to translocate to the nucleus and regulate genes involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses. Modulation of the IkB-α/NF-kB axis can suppress the catabolic and apoptotic effects of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [17]. In osteoarthritic cartilage, elevated CXCL10 expression facilitates the chemotaxis of inflammatory macrophages into the synovium, thereby accelerating the progression of osteoarthritis [18]. Activation of NF-kB has been shown to upregulate CXCL10 transcription, increasing its production and promoting inflammatory mediator release, which contributes to the perpetuation of inflammation and joint degradation [19].

The pathological mechanisms underlying LAS injury are complex and involve inflammatory, catabolic, and degenerative processes. TENS has emerged as a promising adjunctive therapeutic strategy for managing LAS. In this study, a LAS rat model was established and treated with TENS. Following treatment, improvements in motor function and tissue repair were assessed. Transcriptomic analysis revealed differentially regulated genes in articular cartilage, prompting an in vitro investigation of CXCL-10 and NF-κB expression. CXCL-10 was locally injected into the joint, and chondrocytes with suppressed CXCL-10/NF-κB signaling were reinfused to evaluate their therapeutic potential in LAS. The primary objective of this study is to elucidate the functional role and molecular mechanisms of TENS in LAS. We hypothesize that TENS enhances motor function and promotes osteogenic and chondrogenic repair by modulating the IL-1β, CXCL-10, and NF-κB signaling pathways. Furthermore, we propose that the reinfusion of chondrocytes with reduced CXCL-10 expression, induced by TENS, may contribute to improved outcomes in LAS. These findings suggest that TENS, chondrocyte reinfusion, and CXCL-10 inhibition hold clinical potential for the treatment of LAS.

Materials and methods

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)

The setting range of TENS (Brain Lab Co., CN) was: 0.375–23.600 v, 0.3675–23.1280 mA, 0.1470–9.2512 A/m2, 25.00 Hz, the sinusoidal waveform; the LAS rat’s ankle was treated with TENS for 20 min/day, 5 times a week [20].

Animals

A total of 100 female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 3 months and weighing 210 ± 20 g, were obtained from Kunming Medical University (license number: SYXK K2020-006) and housed under standard laboratory conditions, including a temperature range of 21–23 °C and a 12-hour light–dark cycle. All animals had unrestricted access to food and distilled water. This study was approved by the Animal Study Committee of Kunming Medical University (license number: KMMU2023MEC203) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guidelines and the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for the ethical treatment and reporting of animal research.

Lateral ankle sprain (LAS) rats

Prior to the operation, the rats were subjected to a 12-hour fasting period and a 6-hour water deprivation period. Subsequently, the rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (0.1%, 35 mg/kg; Merck Ltd., Ger). The LAS model was established through partial resection of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) of the ankle joint (1 cm longitudinal incision on the lateral side; located these two ligaments and transected at the midpoint). Following the operation, ampicillin (6000 IU/kg/d, HYZS Ltd., CN) was administered via intramuscular injection for a duration of 3 days.

Grouping

A total of 60 LAS rats were randomly allocated into the Sham group, the model control (C) group, and the low/medium/high (L/M/H) intensity TENS groups (Low intensity: 3.16 Vpp, 1.58 V, 1.55 mA; Medium intensity: 6.16 Vpp, 3.08 V, 3.02 mA; High intensity: 12.90 Vpp, 6.45 V, 6.32 mA) for 1, 2, or 3 weeks; each group consists of 12 rats. Subsequently, a total of 40 LAS rats were divided into 5 groups; each group consists of 8 rats. The LAS rats were randomly allocated into the cell donor group and receptor group; each group consists of 4 rats. The receptor group was subjected to intra-articular injection of chondrocytes from the donor group which was induced by TENS for 3 weeks.

Intra-articular injection of chondrocyte

The other 20 LAS rats in 5 groups were induced by TENS and the articular cartilage of the ankle jiont surface which was formed by the talus, tibia, and fibula was harvested under anesthesia (0.1% sodium pentobarbital, 35 mg/kg; Merck Ltd., Ger). The tissue was cut as finely as possible and less than 1 mm3, washed twice with PBS. Then transferred to a 15 ml centrifuge tube, placed in a centrifuge at 1,000×g for 2 min, and discarded the upper liquid and suspended tissue. 0.25% trypsin (G3440, Servicebio Co., CN) was added and placed in the incubator at 37℃ for 30 min, and the cartilage tissue was shaken every 10 min to disperse. After culturing for 30 min, the digestion was terminated and centrifuged at 1,000×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. 0.2% collagenase II (GC305014, Servicebio Co., CN) was prepared with 4 ml DMEM + 10% FBS for 3 h at 37 ℃. Digestion was terminated and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The suspended cells were inoculated for 2 days. The extracted chondrocytes were cultured and manufactured as an articular injection of chondrocyte with 1 × 106 for ankle joint .

Micro-CT analysis

The right ankle joint of the LAS rat was measured. Micro-CT analysis was performed according to recent guidelines56 using a SkyScan 1176 micro-CT imaging system (SkyScan, Bruker, Belgium) with a spatial resolution of 17.75 mm (X-ray source 90 kV/270 mA; exposure time 360 ms; magnification ×15; and 1.0 mm aluminum/0.1 mm copper filter). Volumetric reconstructions and analyses were performed using the built-in software NRecon 1.6 and CTAn 1.8. For the analysis of bone regeneration, the volume of interest was measured by the average grayscale value at the specific bone position (minimum to maximum degree: 0-255) [21].

Histopathological evaluation

After micro-CT analysis, these samples were excised and subjected to gradual decalcification. Sections with a thickness of 5 mm were prepared using a Leica RM2245 microtome and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE, G1005, Servicebio Co., CN) and Alcian Blue (AB; G1027, Servicebio Co., CN). HE staining is utilized to assess the general morphology and structure of the tissue, while AB staining is employed to detect the cartilage matrix in joints.

Immunohistological analysis (IHC)

The ankle joint was equilibrated in 0.1 M Tris-buffered saline for 10 min. Following one hour of blocking in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% normal goat serum, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies (IL-1β, 26048-1-AP, dilution 1:100; CXCL10, 10937-1-AP, dilution 1:200; NF-κB, 10745-1-AP, dilution 1:100; Proteintech Co., CN). Subsequently, the samples were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (GB23303, dilution 1:200; Servicebio Co., CN) for 1 h at room temperature. The results were quantified using the Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and the positive-area ratio per unit area was calculated. The positive-area ratio was quantified by loading the histological image, setting the threshold to define the positive regions, and then calculating the ratio of positive area to total image area.

Immunofluorescence analysis (IF)

Cells were cultured onto coverslips and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (G3068, Servicebio Co., CN) for 5 min. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the samples with 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the primary antibody (IL-1β, 26048-1-AP, dilution 1:100; CXCL10, 10937-1-AP, dilution 1:200; NF-κB, 10745-1-AP, dilution 1:100; Proteintech Co., CN) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the samples were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (GB25303, dilution 1:500, Servicebio Co., CN) for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, G1012, Servicebio Co., CN) to visualize cell nuclei and mounted in mounting medium with an antifade reagent.

Bone biomechanics

The samples were obtained from the left ankle joint (the intact joint was harvested and organizational structure is complete.) of the LAS rat and maintained the integrity of the ligament-bone junction. Before starting the measurement, the length, width and thickness of the ankle joint need to be measured (Muromachi co., Japan), and then after bathing and stretching the fitting with normal saline, the two ends of the ankle joint are fixed on the MTS universal materials testing machine to measure compression, bending and tensile related data.

AI gait analysis

Each rat was positioned within a running wheel apparatus, where a camera documented its movement at a frame rate of 30.00 Hz over a period of 1 min. To guarantee precise tracking, key anatomical features were manually annotated in a series of key frames, including the knee (denoted in purple), ankle (denoted in cyan), and paw (denoted in red). The annotated dataset was employed to train a deep-learning model within the DeepLabCut (DLC) framework. Leveraging a convolutional neural network (CNN), the model learned the spatial attributes of each marker, thereby enabling it to identify and track these points in subsequent videos. Once trained, the model was utilized for pose estimation and motion tracking in new videos, generating coordinate data for each annotated body part. For the purpose of analysis, the 1-minute recording was partitioned into three 20-second segments. The following metrics were computed for each segment:

Step length: Defined as the distance between consecutive placements of the paw marker.

Step frequency: Calculated based on the number of steps within each 20-second interval.

Ankle joint angle: Computed as the angle formed between the ankle and paw markers, with the ankle serving as the vertex, offering insights into joint movement during locomotion.

Ankle degeneration score

The ankle degeneration score was designed by modifying and optimizing the Taylor Score, which is originally used to assess the severity of hip osteoarthritis. The ankle degeneration score includes micro-CT imaging features such as joint space narrowing, cartilage damage, and osteophyte formation. In this study, the score was adapted to better reflect the degenerative changes in the ankle joint, incorporating ankle-specific anatomical and pathological characteristics. The ankle degeneration score is elaborated as follows:

0: Absence of active movement in the hind limbs.

1: Minimal movement of the hind limbs, accompanied by an incapacity to bear weight.

2: Frequent or forceful movement of the hind limbs, yet an inability to bear weight or walk.

3: The hind limbs are capable of bearing the body weight and facilitating walking for 1–2 steps, although with an abnormal gait.

4: Weight-bearing and walking with only slight impairment and marked weakness.

5: Normal walking capacity.

Ankle varus and valgus angles analysis

The ankle varus and valgus angles were quantified using a custom-designed fixation system specifically adapted for small animal models. LAS rats were positioned on a standardized measuring platform with their hind limbs in a neutral, extended posture and the plantar surface in contact with the platform. The plantar surface served as the reference plane, and the posterior margin of the tibial plafond was aligned with the baseline. The anterior margin of the ankle joint was identified, typically at the distal tibial articular surface or the lateral margin of the talus. The angle between this anatomical point and the tibial plafond was measured. Each sample was assessed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility, and the average angle was recorded for analysis.

Transcriptome sequencing

The samples were obtained from a portion of the left ankle joint. RNA degradation and contamination were monitored on 1% agarose gels. RNA purity was checked using the NanoPhotometer®spectrophotometer (IMPLEN Co, CA, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies Co., CA, USA). In this assessment, the RNA integrity numbers (RIN) of all samples ranged from 6.4 to 6.7. A total amount of 1 µg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB Co., USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in NEB Next First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5X). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H-). Second-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities. After adenylation of 3’ ends of DNA fragments, NEBNext Adaptor with hairpin loop structure was ligated to prepare for hybridization. In order to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 250–300 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter Co, Beverly, USA). Then 3 µl USER Enzyme was used with size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 5 min at 95° Cbefore PCR. Then PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase. Finally, PCR products were purified and library quality was assessed. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumia Co., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. Raw data in fastq format were firstly processed through in-house Perl scripts. In this step, clean data were obtained by removing reads containing adapters, reads containing ploy-N and low quality reads from raw data. At the same time, Q20, Q30 and GC content of the clean data were calculated (Novogene Co., CN).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

After the bone biomechanics examination, these samples (were obtained from the left ankle including the distal end of the tibia, fibula and talus near the articular surface, calcaneus, joint capsule, ligament) were manufactured for analysis at the nucleic acid and protein levels. The primer was manufactured by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, USA) database. cDNA was prepared from 2 mg of RNA using a Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa Co., Jap) and analyzed with SYBR Green Master Mix (TaKaRa Co., Jap) in an LC480 Real-time PCR System (Roche Ltd., Ger). The data were quantified using the 2ΔΔCt relative quantitation method and were normalized to beta-actin expression in each sample. The sequences of primers used were as follows: IL-1β (Rattus), F’ TTGAGTCTGCACAGTTCCCC, R’ GTCCTGGGGAAGGCATTAGG; NF-κB (Rattus), F’ TGTGAAGAAGCGAGACCTGG, R’ TGCTCCTCTATGGGAACTTGAA; CXCL10 (Rattus), F’ TGTTGGCTGGACCAGATTCA, R’ TGTTGGCTGGACCAGATTCA .

Western blot (WB)

The ankle joint of each rat was collected, the mixture of the distal end of the tibia, fibula and talus near the articular surface, calcaneus, joint capsule, ligament tissue was washed twice with PBS. Then, the total protein was extracted by cleaving the cells on ice, and the protein concentration was analyzed by a BCA (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., USA). After determining the protein concentration, SDS-PAGE was performed at 80 V for 10 min and 120 V for 1.5 h, after which the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked for 15 min, and rinsed with TBST 4 times for 5 min each. Then, the color was developed by chemiluminescence using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Amersham Biosciences Inc., USA). The membrane was exposed and imaged with a luminescent meter. Protein band intensity was quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software and normalized to the corresponding band intensity. The following antibodies were used: anti-ACTIN (GB15003, Servicebio Co., CN), anti-IL-1β (26048-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech Co., CN), anti-CXCL10 (10937-1-AP, 1:1500, Proteintech Co., CN), and anti-NF-κB (10745-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech Co., CN).

Statistical analysis

Data were collected using GraphPad software (Prism 8, USA) and presented in the form of mean±standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons (SPSS 27.0, USA), and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TENS treatment improves LAS in rats

Following the surgical procedure and three weeks’ treatment with TENS, in comparison to the model control group, a significant reduction in weight was observed in the high-intensity TENS group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A). After three and seven days of TENS treatment, when compared with the model control group, the ankle degeneration score exhibited a significant increase in the medium- and high-intensity TENS groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B). After seven days of TENS treatment, relative to the model control group, the varus ankle angle degree decreased significantly in the high-intensity TENS group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C); the valgus ankle angle degree decreased significantly in the medium- and high-intensity TENS groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). After one, three, and seven days of TENS treatment, compared to the model control group, the inclined plane test degree increased significantly in the low-intensity TENS group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E). Post-operation, a significant increase in the average step length was noted compared to the pre-operation state (P < 0.05, Fig. 1F). After three weeks of TENS treatment, in comparison to the model control group, the average step length increased significantly in the medium- and high-intensity TENS groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 1G); the step frequency increased significantly in the high-intensity TENS group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1H); the average ankle angle decreased significantly in the TENS groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 1I).

The bone mineral content (BMC) in the high-intensity TENS group showed a significant increase (P < 0.05. The bone volume (BV) value in the TENS groups increased significantly (P < 0.05). Both the BV and Bone volume/Tissue volume (BV/TV) values in the TENS groups were significantly elevated (P < 0.05). The trabecular number (TB.N) value in the low- and high-intensity TENS groups decreased significantly (P < 0.05). The trabecular thickness (TB. Th) in the TENS groups increased significantly (P < 0.05). The Structure model index (SMI) in the low- and medium-intensity TENS groups decreased significantly (P < 0.05). After three weeks of TENS, in comparison to the model control group, the bio-mechanical distance and maximum power of LAS rats in the TENS groups increased significantly (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C, D). The bio-mechanical stiffness of LAS rats in the medium- and high-intensity TENS groups increased significantly (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E). After a three-week TENS treatment, when compared with the model control group, the cartilage thickness of LAS rats in the TENS groups increased significantly (P < 0.05, Fig. 2F and G). The alcian blue (AB) staining of cartilage thickness in the medium- and high-intensity TENS groups increased significantly (P < 0.05, Fig. 2H and I).

TENS down-regulates CXCL-10/IL-1β/NF-kB expression in LAS rats

After 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, compared to the model control group, there were 657 different genes that were analyzed by transcriptome sequencing in the TENS group; 7 genes of the LAS rat were closely related to the IL-17 signaling pathway; TENS down-regulated the CXCL10/NF-κB expressions (Fig. 3A and B). Discussion

LAS are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries, with at least 40% of individuals experiencing persistent symptoms following a first-time sprain [22]. In this study, a LAS model was established in rats by duplicating and transecting the ATFL and CFL [23]. Post-surgery, rats exhibited impaired ankle function, altered gait, and increased joint instability. The objective of this study was to investigate the functional and mechanistic effects of TENS in a LAS rat model. Following surgery, both the model control and TENS-treated groups showed an increase in body weight. This increase may reflect a protective response, as some studies have suggested that weight-bearing activity, when initiated early, can promote recovery in severe ankle sprains by potentially reducing further damage and supporting tissue repair [24]. However, the role of weight changes in LAS pathophysiology remains less clear, and their impact on ankle instability is not fully understood. Some studies suggest that increased body weight may act as a protective factor in cases of severe ankle sprains, potentially preventing further injury and promoting tissue recovery through weight-bearing activity [25]. The weight is also a double-edged effect on LAS rats and affecting the instability of the ankle is unknown. After 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, compared to the model control group, the weight decreased in the medium/high intensity of TENS groups; compared to the sham group, the weight was similar in the medium/high intensity of TENS groups. TENS could improve LAS rats after 3 weeks at least in terms of weight.

It is particularly noteworthy that the 7-day time point was selected as the endpoint for functional assessment following treatment. If no significant differences were observed among groups after 7 days of treatment, the results at this time point were still included in the study. For instance, the ankle degeneration score is a standard metric used to evaluate limb movement, primarily assessing motor function in quadrupedal animals, with a focus on range of motion, walking, and running [26]. Although the TENS treatment period lasted 3 weeks, the ankle degeneration score was analyzed at the 7-day mark because the scores were similar across all groups at that time. TENS was found to improve the varus angle in LAS rats after 3 weeks of treatment, though its effectiveness in rodents has been reported to be limited in some earlier studies. To facilitate early return to activity and recurrence prevention, various quick and simple tests have been proposed, such as the single-leg loading (SLL) test and the ankle-GO score [27]. These scoring systems are not yet widely used in the analysis of LAS rat models, but they show potential for future application. In terms of anatomical parameters, this study found that the varus angle in the TENS group was greater than that in the sham group, while the valgus angle was similar between the two groups. Despite the treatment, LAS rats remained in a CFL state. Following a 3-week TENS treatment, the LAS rats exhibited improved grip strength, as measured by the inclined plane test [28]. Additionally, a novel AI-based gait analysis was employed to assess gait improvements, capturing parameters such as the displacement of the position axis (X/Y/Z), frequency, and the angle of the ankle. A prior study reported that females with chronic ankle instability (CAI) exhibited greater dynamic joint stiffness (DJS) alterations compared to male CAI counterparts [29]. Building on this, Our own calculated gait analysis revealed that TENS treatment led to significant improvements in both step length and gait frequency in LAS rats. These findings further support the notion that TENS can enhance motor function in LAS rats following a period of treatment.In this study, micro-CT scanning was used to evaluate the ankle structure in a LAS rat model. In human patients, LAS injuries are typically detectable on CT scans, and the imaging findings are considered sufficient for diagnostic confirmation [30]. The results showed that bone mineral density (BMD) and bone mineral content (BMC) increased in the TENS-treated groups compared to the model control group. Furthermore, bone volume (BV) and trabecular thickness (TB.Th) also increased following three weeks of TENS treatment. Interestingly, the BMD and BMC were found to decrease in the sham group when compared to the model control group. Lower regional BMD is significantly associated with an increased risk of trauma or fractures [31]; however, upon reviewing the literature, there is currently no clear evidence regarding whether sham surgery promotes osteogenesis. This remains an area of confusion, and further investigation is warranted, particularly in the absence of external interventions. The biomechanical properties and cartilage thickness of the ankle joint were also improved in the TENS groups, suggesting that TENS may have a dual effect in promoting both osteogenesis and chondrogenesis.

Transcriptomics, a key component of multi-omics analysis, has revealed changes in gene expression following TENS treatment. A comprehensive transcriptomic analysis identified 325 up-regulated and 332 down-regulated genes. This study focused specifically on the anti-inflammatory effects of TENS in a lateral ankle sprain (LAS) rat model. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TENS offers both analgesic and anti-inflammatory benefits. Of particular interest is the gene CXCL-10, which is involved in the NF-κB signaling pathway. Reduced expression of CXCL-10 has been shown to suppress the activation of microglia and astrocytes, effects that appear to be linked to the regulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [32]. Following TENS application, the expression levels of IL-1, CXCL-10, and NF-κB were found to decrease. This reduction in inflammatory markers was associated with improved functional recovery in LAS rats. The osteogenic and chondrogenic effects of TENS will be further explored in a series of upcoming studies conducted by our research team.

Cell reinfusion has emerged as a novel clinical therapy that has shown promising results in treating liver disease, primary breast cancer, and autologous graft-versus-host disease, among other conditions [33]. In particular, chondrocyte reinfusion with low expression of CXCL-10 has demonstrated therapeutic improvements comparable to those observed with TENS treatment. Both TENS and TENS-stimulated chondrocytes have been found to enhance the functional recovery of LAS rats. In this study, we adopted an intra-articular injection approach, inspired by stem cell-based intra-articular therapy, to directly deliver chondrocytes to the affected regions of LAS disease, with the aim of enhancing local therapeutic efficacy. Compared to systemic or intravenous administration, this method offers the potential for improved cell retention and targeted delivery to the disease site [34]. It is important to note that the source of primary chondrocytes must be carefully considered for potential clinical use. Although not observed in this study, the possibility of immune rejection remains a critical factor that should be acknowledged. Research has identified that CXCL-10 inhibitors can suppress the growth of non-small cell lung cancer by targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway [35]. Furthermore, reducing H3R8 or/and H4R3 methylation at the CXCL10 and RSAD2 promoters has been shown to downregulate CXCL10 expression and inhibit osteoclast activity via the RANKL/NF-κB signaling pathway [36]. It is possible that CXCL-10 inhibitors may exert a similar therapeutic benefit as TENS in LAS rats through the RANKL/NF-κB signaling pathway.

In summary, the objectives of this study have been achieved, elucidating the function and mechanism of TENS in LAS rats. However, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the therapeutic effects of TENS, given that the rat model used differs from the patient in physiological characteristics. Notably, the recovery ability of LAS rats after TENS treatment is significantly faster than that of patient as evidenced by some scores and analysis. These results indicated that rats gradually recovered after 5–7 days of TENS treatment and it was a different prognosis that rarely occurs in clinical cases. Although this study set an observation until three weeks, it is crucial to note the need for ongoing assessment when applying TENS therapy for LAS disease in the clinic. The mechanism of TENS on the IL-1beta/CXCL-10/NF-κB expression is still under investigation, and our research team hopes to elucidate not only the anti-inflammatory effects of TENS but also its mechanism in promoting osteogenic and chondrogenic effects.

Conclusion

TENS could improve the motor function and promote the osteogenic and chondrogenic effects in LAS rats. TENS inhibited the inflammation by regulating the IL-1β/CXCL-10/NF-κB expression. Reinfusion of chondrocytes with low levels of CXCL-10 yields comparable effects to TENS in LAS rats. Collectively, TENS, demonstrate a promising clinical potential for the treatment of LAS. Furthermore, chondrocyte reinfusion might become a therapeutic approach, substantial technical barriers currently limit its clinical applicability.
