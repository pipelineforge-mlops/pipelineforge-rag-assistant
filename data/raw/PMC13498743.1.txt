The diagnosis and management of acute hepatocellular injury continue to pose significant challenges. Clinicians depend on both clinical and laboratory findings, alongside the exclusion of other potential aetiologies, to make a diagnosis. We describe a case of transient marked hepatocellular injury in a phase 1 clinical trial participant exposed to anti-HIV monoclonal antibodies. The trial was registered with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR 201808919297244) on 29 August 2018.

Introduction

Hepatocellular injury is an elevation of liver transaminases indicating inflammation or damage to liver cells. It presents with a multitude of potential aetiologies that require meticulous evaluation to identify 1 (Supplemental Table 1). To determine the underlying cause, clinicians must conduct a thorough assessment using clinical observations and laboratory data. Concurrently, potential contributing factors such as exposure to hepatotoxic drugs and agents must be excluded. This diagnostic process often includes the elimination of these contributory factors, underscoring the complexity in establishing a definitive diagnosis. In clinical practice, the availability of specific diagnostic markers for hepatocellular injury is notably limited. Consequently, healthcare providers frequently resort to a combination of laboratory assessments and imaging studies aimed at excluding other pathological conditions that might mimic or contribute to the liver enzyme abnormalities. This comprehensive approach is crucial in settings where patients are exposed to new pharmacological agents, such as those enrolled for clinical trial testing of investigational drugs. We describe a case of transient marked hepatocellular injury in a phase 1 HIV prevention clinical trial. 2

Case report

A 26-year-old healthy HIV-negative female enrolled into the CAPRISA 012A trial 2 (PACTR201808919297244), was administered anti-HIV monoclonal antibodies VRC07-523LS at a dose of 5 mg/kg and PGT121 at 3 mg/kg. Three months later, routine safety bloods showed elevations of alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), with values of 1167 IU/L and 820 IU/L respectively (Figure 1(b)). No consumption of alcohol, recreational drugs, or herbal preparations was reported. She received several concomitant medications (Figure 1(a)), which were stopped. Three days after initial elevations, ALT and AST levels started to steadily decrease.

Twenty-two days later, a second upward trend in liver transaminases was observed (Figure 1(b)). At this visit the participant reported consumption of household white vinegar to treat metrorrhagia, prior to the initial transaminase elevation. Mild elevations in the international normalized ratio, direct bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase and gamma-glutamyl transferase were observed, peaking at 1.47, 10umol/L, 652 IU/L and 70 IU/L respectively. After noting the second upsurge in transaminases, additional tests, including viral hepatitis serology, autoimmune markers, pregnancy testing, and abdominal ultrasound were conducted and were unremarkable (Supplemental Table 1).

During follow-up her liver enzyme profile steadily improved. All symptoms resolved and she was referred to her local healthcare provider.

A cytokine analysis was conducted on all available stored blood samples to better understand her baseline and post-monoclonal antibody inflammatory profiles. From day 3 after monoclonal antibody administration, the majority of the pro-inflammatory (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β), chemokines (MCP-1, MIP-1α) and adaptive cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-4) increased and remained elevated compared to matched control participants, indicating an inflammatory response after antibody administration (Figure 1(c)).

Discussion

This case represents a possible multifactorial aetiology of transient hepatocellular injury. There are currently no reports in the literature of VRC07-523LS and PGT121 related liver abnormalities. Autoimmune hepatitis can be caused by immune responses to monoclonal antibodies, but the pathophysiology remains unclear. Examples of monoclonal antibodies associated with liver injury are infliximab and cemiplimab, used in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, respectively. 3 Antibodies to B cell markers and activation signals such as anti-CD20 rituximab, used to treat non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, have also been associated with hepatotoxicity, and have been attributed to immune modulation and induction of autoimmunity.

Infectious agents such as Hepatitis A, B, C, D and E, cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus and herpes simplex virus can cause liver injury. 4 Recurrent urinary tract infections and bacteriuria have been associated with liver abnormalities. This participant had a history of urinary tract infections and had exposure to several potentially hepatotoxic drugs: metronidazole, fosfomycin and co-amoxiclav, ibuprofen, contraceptive agents’ depo provera, ovral and triphasil, and ferrous sulphate (Figure 1(a)). Metronidazole has been associated with rare immune mediated drug reactions and presents 1–3 months after drug exposure. Severe acute liver injury with slow recovery, followed by fatal recurrence on re-exposure has also been described.

A few cases of liver injury attributed to fosfomycin have been reported. Mild AST elevations which are self-limited occur in 1-2% of patients after a single oral dose of fosfomycin. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is a common cause of liver injury, usually presenting with a cholestatic or mixed pattern. Delayed reactions often occur several weeks after exposure. Ibuprofen can cause asymptomatic elevations in AST and ALT and acute liver failure. Cholestatic liver injury typically arising during the first few months of oestrogens and combined oral contraceptives initiation has also been described but generally resolves rapidly with dose modification or discontinuation. Iron supplementation can also cause acute liver damage, but only when overdosed. The participant reported ingestion of household white vinegar before the initial transaminase elevation. Vinegar contains acetic acid; however, published hepatic toxicity is largely associated with concentrated or high-volume acetic acid exposure, which was not evident in this case. 5 The quantity and frequency of ingestion could not be reliably established. Therefore, vinegar exposure was considered a possible contextual factor but not a likely primary cause.

The exact causes of transient transaminase elevation remain undefined in this case. However, the most probable aetiology is the use of multiple potentially hepatotoxic drugs in combination. Using the RUCAM scoring system, co-amoxiclav, ibuprofen and the oral contraceptive agents are specifically considered the possible causative drugs at 5 points each, with the monoclonal antibodies being an unlikely cause. However, it is hypothesized that the underlying hyperinflammatory state triggered by the monoclonal antibodies could have resulted in predisposition to hepatocellular injury in this transient clinical manifestation.

In a clinical trial setting, the diligent reporting of adverse events associated with the study product is not merely a procedural formality but a crucial aspect of patient safety and research integrity. This particular case underscores the importance of comprehensive history-taking, meticulous clinical examination, and stringent monitoring. This becomes even more imperative when multiple pharmacological agents are involved, as the potential for drug-drug interactions and side effects significantly increases. Effective management of these components is essential to ensure that the safety profiles of the pharmacological agents are accurately understood, and that patient safety is maintained throughout the study.
