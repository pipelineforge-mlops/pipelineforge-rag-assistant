Announcing The Lancet Regional Health—Europe series on the alarming surge of early-onset colorectal cancer: a multidisciplinary call to action

The rising incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC), defined as a diagnosis in adults younger than 50 years, has emerged as a critical global public health challenge.1 While colorectal cancer (CRC) rates in older populations have stabilized or declined due to successful screening programs, the incidence among younger adults is surging at an unprecedented rate.2 Projections suggest that by 2030, one-third of all colorectal cancers will be diagnosed in individuals under the age of 50.3 This “unprecedented tumor epidemic” might be driven by a significant birth cohort effect, suggesting that generational shifts in environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, such as obesity, westernized diets, antibiotic usage, and potentially associated alterations in the microbiome are reshaping the epidemiological landscape of CRC.4

The rising incidence of EOCRC has prompted increasing scientific attention, though substantial knowledge gaps still remain. No specific etiologic factors have been clearly identified, limiting our understanding of disease biology and hindering the development of effective prevention tools and tailored management strategies. Although EOCRC shows distinct clinical and pathological features, clear associations with specific molecular alterations have not yet been demonstrated. As a result, no obvious molecular targets have emerged so far to support the development of dedicated targeted therapies. Moreover, EOCRC affects individuals during the most productive years of life, adding complexity to their clinical management. Patients face a broad range of unmet needs including fertility, relationships, career, and long-term survivorship, which are often insufficiently addressed within current care models. The higher prevalence of hereditary cancer syndromes further complicates management, raising unresolved issues around genetic risk and family counseling. Overall, the absence of dedicated guidelines and standardized care pathways highlights the ongoing challenges in this matter.5,6

In response to this growing burden, this new Series in The Lancet Regional Health Europe provides a comprehensive perspective on EOCRC, integrating epidemiological data, molecular insights, and patient-centered dimensions. The Series is structured around four pivotal series papers designed to inform research, clinical practice, and health policy (Fig. 1):Fig. 1 Structure and key themes of The Lancet Regional Health – Europe Series on Early-Onset Colorectal Cancer. The Series encompasses four complementary papers addressing epidemiology and biology, multidisciplinary care and survivorship, diagnostic pathways, and geographic disparities, complemented by a dedicated Patient Voice Comment.

Series paper 1: epidemiology, biology, and molecular profiles. This Series paper will provide a comprehensive overview of the global rising burden of EOCRC, integrating epidemiological trends with emerging biological insights. It will synthesize data on incidence, mortality, and risk factors, with particular attention to birth cohort effects and life-course exposures. In addition, it will examine the molecular, pathological, and hereditary features that may distinguish EOCRC from late-onset disease, critically evaluating whether EOCRC represents a biologically distinct entity or a phenotypic shift driven by environmental and lifestyle changes. The Series paper will also highlight current gaps in surveillance systems and identify priorities for prevention, early detection, and international research collaboration.

Series paper 2: multidisciplinary care and survivorship. This Series paper will address the clinical complexity of EOCRC by adopting a multidisciplinary, patient-centered perspective that extends beyond conventional oncologic management. It will examine current standards of care across surgery, systemic therapy, and radiotherapy, while critically assessing treatment intensity and long-term toxicity in younger patients. Particular emphasis will be placed on fertility preservation, sexual health, and survivorship, recognizing the profound impact of diagnosis during peak reproductive and working years. The Series paper will also explore the psychosocial and socioeconomic consequences of EOCRC and propose a framework for specialized centers integrating oncology, reproductive medicine, survivorship care, and supportive services. By identifying gaps in care delivery and research, it will outline priorities for improving both clinical outcomes and quality of life.

Series paper 3: diagnostic pathways and barriers. This mixed-methods systematic Series paper will provide a structured and mechanistic understanding of where and why delays occur in the diagnosis of EOCRC. It will map the full trajectory to diagnosis by examining sequential intervals, including appraisal, help-seeking, primary care, and secondary care. By integrating quantitative data, such as duration of diagnostic intervals, risk of emergency presentation, and repeated healthcare visits, with qualitative evidence on symptom appraisal, health-seeking behavior, and clinician decision-making, the Series paper will identify key bottlenecks along the diagnostic pathway. Particular attention will be given to modifiable factors, including low symptom awareness in younger individuals, misattribution of symptoms, and age-related diagnostic bias that may delay referral and investigation.

The Series paper will also consider how differences in national screening policies and healthcare systems influence diagnostic pathways and stage at presentation. By combining evidence across disciplines, it aims to identify actionable targets to reduce diagnostic delays and improve earlier detection of EOCRC.

Series paper 4: geographic disparities and global perspectives. This Series paper will position Europe within the global landscape of EOCRC, examining geographic variation in incidence, clinicopathological features, molecular characteristics, and outcomes. It will compare European patterns with those observed in North America, Asia, Latin America, Autralia, and Africa to determine whether EOCRC reflects a shared global phenotype or a spectrum of regionally modulated disease entities shaped by environmental exposures, genetic susceptibility, and health-system factors.

A key strength of this Series paper will be the integration of data and expertise from the GEOCODE Consortium,7 enabling an harmonized and comparative analysis across regions. Through this collaborative framework, the Series paper will explore the contribution of the exposome, metabolic factors, microbiome alterations, and disparities in screening and diagnostic access to observed geographic differences.

Ultimately, the Series paper will translate these insights into implications for prevention, risk stratification, early diagnosis, and policy development, highlighting opportunities for international collaboration and data harmonization to address EOCRC as a global public health challenge.

Importantly, this Series also incorporates the patient perspective through a dedicated Patient Voice Comment written by patient advocates, complementing the four Series papers and providing direct insight into the lived experience of EOCRC. Integrating these perspectives is essential, as patient input has been shown to highlight gaps in care pathways and improve the relevance of research and clinical strategies.

The impact of this Series lies in its effort to translate emerging biological, clinical, and epidemiological evidence into a more structured understanding of EOCRC. By addressing current gaps in knowledge and care, it aims to provide a framework to guide future research priorities, refine diagnostic pathways, and support the development of more appropriate, age-specific treatment strategies. By combining data-driven evidence with patient-centered insights, this Series seeks to inform the organization of multidisciplinary care models tailored to younger patients, integrating oncology, genetics, reproductive medicine, and survivorship care.
