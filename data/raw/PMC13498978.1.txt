Summary

Bone defects present a major clinical challenge in regenerative medicine, where bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) are widely used but often show limited osteogenic efficacy. In this study, we developed an engineered tissue strategy combining lentivirus-mediated dual-gene regulation and an allogeneic bone scaffold to enhance bone regeneration. BMSCs were modified to overexpress nerve growth factor (NGF) and knock down the p75 neurotrophin receptor (P75NTR), and their effects on osteogenesis and pyroptosis were evaluated in vitro and in a rat femoral condyle defect model. The dual-gene modification suppressed NF-κB nuclear translocation and reduced pyroptosis-related protein expression, while significantly enhancing osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. In vivo, scaffold-assisted delivery of engineered BMSCs promoted new bone formation and improved structural regeneration in critical-sized defects. These findings demonstrate that coordinated regulation of NGF and P75NTR signaling within a biomaterial-based system can enhance stem cell-mediated bone repair and improve functional bone regeneration outcomes.

Introduction

Bone defects are a common condition caused by various factors, including but not limited to inflammation, trauma, infection, and tumor resection, posing a significant clinical concern worldwide.1,2,3 Despite the inherent self-repair ability of bone tissue, such as in cases of transverse fractures, crack fractures, and trabecular fractures,4 when bone defects exceed its natural healing capacity, it leads to severe functional impairment, necessitating medical intervention to restore its structure and function.5 Autologous bone transplantation has long been considered the “gold standard” for bone repair due to its excellent osteogenesis, osteoinduction, and osteoconduction capabilities.6,7 However, limitations such as donor site constraints, additional surgical trauma, and potential complications have hindered its widespread clinical application.8 Given these limitations, researchers and clinicians continuously seek alternative methods to discover safer and more effective bone repair strategies.

Tissue engineering technology has shown promising applications in bone repair in recent years.9,10 This technology combines biologically active materials, growth factors, and cells with osteogenic potential to construct tissue-engineered products that promote bone defect repair.11,12 The U.S. FDA has authorized the clinical application of allogeneic bone materials when used in compliance with specific regulatory requirements. In this study, we chose allogeneic bone as the scaffold material, which possesses good biocompatibility and appropriate degradation rate, providing a suitable three-dimensional architecture to support cell adhesion and growth.13,14 Among the numerous seed cells available for tissue engineering, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) are considered one of the most promising due to their ease of isolation, multilineage differentiation potential, and good immunocompatibility.15 BMSCs can be readily propagated in vitro and possess multipotent differentiation potential, giving rise to osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic lineages.16 However, the use of BMSCs alone fails to fully replicate the osteogenic effects observed with autologous bone transplantation. In recent years, researchers have begun to focus on manipulating the phenotype and function of BMSCs through gene engineering methods to enhance their effectiveness in tissue engineering.17

Nerve growth factor (NGF) belongs to the neurotrophin family and was initially discovered for its influence on the survival and differentiation of neurons.18,19 It interacts with two types of receptors: high-affinity tropomyosin receptor kinases (Trks) and low-affinity p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR).20 Beyond its neurological functions, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that NGF can promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and facilitate sensory nerve ingrowth during bone regeneration, thereby contributing to fracture healing.21,22,23 However, emerging evidence suggests that NGF-mediated signaling may also exert context-dependent effects on BMSCs. In our preliminary study, NGF overexpression in BMSCs was associated with increased activation of pyroptosis-related signaling during bone defect repair, which may be linked to the concomitant upregulation of P75NTR.24 Pyroptosis represents a controlled inflammatory cell death pathway characterized by NF-κB activation in response to pathogens or cellular stress, ultimately leading to the caspase-mediated cleavage of the pore-forming effector gasdermin D (GSDMD).25,26 Within this process, the NF-κB P65 (RelA) subunit plays a critical role in transcriptional activation following its nuclear translocation.27 Importantly, previous studies have shown that inhibition or silencing of p75NTR can enhance the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs.28,29 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that NGF overexpression induced upregulation of P75NTR may activate NF-κB-mediated pyroptosis, thereby partially counteracting the pro-osteogenic effects of NGF. Therefore, simultaneous NGF overexpression and P75NTR inhibition may represent a promising strategy to suppress pyroptosis and improve BMSC-mediated bone regeneration.

By combining genetically engineered BMSCs with allogeneic bone, this study develops a tissue-engineered bone. In vitro, we utilized lentivirus-mediated gene transduction technology to knockdown or overexpress P75NTR and overexpress NGF, suggesting P75NTR+/BMSC may regulate pyroptosis signal through nuclear translocation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, comparing the effects of NGF+/BMSCs, P75NTR−/BMSCs, and P75NTR−+NGF+/BMSCs on osteogenesis of BMSCs, exploring the efficacy of this strategy in promoting the osteogenic activity of BMSCs. In the in vivo study, the constructs were implanted into critical-sized defects created in the rat femoral condyle, and subsequent bone regeneration within the distal femoral defect region was evaluated. We further validated the repair efficacy of this tissue-engineered bone in an animal model, providing insights and methods for clinical treatment. In summary, this study not only provides a bone defect treatment strategy but also offers experimental data and theoretical support for the application of gene engineering technology in bone tissue engineering. These findings are expected to provide scientific evidence and therapeutic approaches for addressing complex bone defect problems in future clinical practice. Results

Characterization of scaffolds and BMSCs

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to characterize the microstructure of the allogeneic bone scaffold (Figure 2A). At low magnification (50×), the scaffold exhibited a highly porous and interconnected three-dimensional trabecular-like architecture, with large, continuous pores. At higher magnifications (200× and 2.00K×), cells were observed to adhere to the scaffold surface. To further evaluate the mechanical properties of the scaffold, both compressive and tensile stress-strain tests were performed (Figure 2B). The results showed that the scaffold exhibited progressive stress increases with increasing strain under both compression and tension, indicating stable mechanical behavior. Notably, the scaffold loaded with cells (ABS-C) demonstrated higher stress-bearing capacity compared to the control group (ABS). Primary rat BMSCs expanded to passage 3 displayed a typical spindle-shaped appearance and formed a swirling growth pattern under microscopic observation (Figure 2C). Following osteogenic induction, the cells developed distinct mineralized nodules, which were visualized by positive alizarin red S (ARS) staining (Figure 2D). Proliferation analysis using the CCK-8 assay demonstrated a time-dependent increase in cellular metabolic activity (Figure 2E). Knocking down P75NTR inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation and reduces pyroptosis

Three days after lentiviral transfection, fluorescence microscopy confirmed successful gene modification. Green fluorescence was observed in the NGF overexpression and P75NTR overexpression groups, whereas the P75NTR knockdown group showed red fluorescence, indicating efficient transfection (Figure 3A). Quantitative analysis of western blot data showed that P75NTR expression was markedly increased in the P75NTR+/BMSCs group (8.43 ± 0.12) compared with the control group (1.00 ± 0.09), while the NGF+/BMSCs group also showed elevated expression (5.85 ± 0.24). In contrast, P75NTR−/BMSCs showed comparable levels to the control (1.05 ± 0.05). The pyroptosis-related proteins GSDMD and NLRP3 were significantly reduced in the P75NTR−/BMSCs group (0.74 ± 0.05 and 0.79 ± 0.02, respectively) compared with the P75NTR+/BMSCs group (1.23 ± 0.02 and 1.14 ± 0.04) and the NGF+/BMSCs group (1.18 ± 0.02 and 1.38 ± 0.04). Similarly, the relative expression of NF-κB p65 was significantly decreased in the P75NTR−/BMSCs group (0.68 ± 0.00) compared with the control (1.00 ± 0.03), P75NTR+/BMSCs (1.13 ± 0.04), and NGF+/BMSCs groups (1.11 ± 0.03) (Figures 3B and 3C). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated distinct subcellular localization patterns of p65 (Figure 3D). In the P75NTR+/BMSCs group, strong nuclear accumulation of p65 was observed, indicating activation of the NF-κB pathway. P75NTR knockdown combined with NGF overexpression reduces pyroptosis and promotes osteogenesis

Protein expression analysis demonstrated distinct differences in pyroptosis-related markers among the experimental groups (Figure 5A). The expression of the inflammasome-related protein NLRP3 was significantly elevated in the NGF+/BMSCs group (2.46 ± 0.12) compared with the control group (1.00 ± 0.07). In contrast, the P75NTR−/BMSCs group (1.04 ± 0.02) showed levels comparable to the control, while the dual-gene group (1.29 ± 0.14) displayed a partial reduction compared with the NGF+/BMSCs group. A consistent pattern was observed for the pyroptosis protein GSDMD, with the highest expression detected in the NGF+/BMSCs group (4.84 ± 0.17). Notably, both the P75NTR−/BMSCs group (2.63 ± 0.03) and the dual-gene group (2.09 ± 0.11) exhibited markedly lower GSDMD expression compared with the NGF+/BMSCs group, indicating suppression of pyroptosis-related signaling following P75NTR knockdown. At the mRNA level, quantitative PCR analysis revealed trends consistent with the protein expression results (Figure 5B), further supporting the regulatory effect of dual-gene modification on pyroptosis-related pathways. Osteogenic differentiation was evaluated by detecting mineralized matrix formation through ARS staining. As shown in Figure 5C, after four weeks of osteogenic induction, the P75NTR−+NGF+/BMSCs group exhibited significantly more calcium deposits and mineralized nodules than the other groups. Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings at the protein level (Figure 5D). P75NTR knockdown combined with NGF improves bone defect healing in rats

Trabecular structure of new bone can be seen in all the 4 groups, and trabecular structure can be seen at the edge of the defect area, and the support material in the middle has not been completely degraded. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis was performed to quantitatively evaluate new bone formation in the defect area (Figure 6B). Among all groups, the P75NTR−+NGF+/BMSCs group exhibited the greatest extent of bone regeneration, as reflected by multiple trabecular parameters. Specifically, bone volume/total volume% (BV/TV%) was markedly higher in the P75NTR−+NGF+/BMSCs group (22.95% ± 1.23%) compared with the control group (11.70% ± 2.38%), the P75NTR−/BMSCs group (14.92% ± 0.31%), and the NGF+/BMSCs group (15.29 % ± 0.55%). Discussion

Bone defects remain a major clinical challenge.5,30 This is particularly the case when there is impaired vascularization or extensive tissue loss, and the bone’s own regenerative capacity is insufficient for complete repair.31,32 A bone graft is required when bone tissue is unable to repair itself completely, resulting in functional impairment.33 Bone tissue engineering has emerged as a promising strategy to address this limitation by integrating seed cells, bioactive cues, and supportive scaffolds.32 In the present study, we focused on enhancing the regenerative capacity of BMSCs through a dual-gene regulatory strategy, in combination with a bone scaffold, to improve bone defect repair outcomes.

BMSCs are widely recognized as ideal seed cells for bone regeneration due to their osteogenic differentiation potential and immunomodulatory properties.34,35 Recent advances in gene engineering have enabled the modulation of intracellular signaling pathways to enhance stem-cell function further.36 NGF has been shown to enhance the osteogenic commitment of stem cells, thereby facilitating bone repair and fracture healing.37,38 However, our findings suggested that NGF overexpression may simultaneously activate p75NTR-mediated signaling, thereby increasing inflammatory pathway activation.24 In addition, previous studies have reported that downregulation of P75NTR improves the viability of BMSCs, and its upregulation may inhibit bone regeneration.39,40 By silencing P75NTR, we may reduce this negative impact. This genetic regulation strategy provides a new perspective for improving the functionality of tissue-engineered bone.

Pyroptosis, as an inflammatory form of programmed cell death, has been increasingly recognized as a critical factor influencing stem cell survival and tissue regeneration.41 Mechanistically, activation of the NF-κB pathway promotes the transcription of inflammasome-related genes and facilitates the cleavage of GSDMD, ultimately leading to membrane disruption and inflammatory mediator release.42,43 In this context, P75NTR appears to function as an upstream regulator linking NGF signaling to NF-κB activation. Consistent with previous reports, our results demonstrate that knockdown of P75NTR suppresses NF-κB p65 activation and reduces pyroptosis, thereby improving the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs. Based on this mechanistic insight, we proposed and validated a dual-gene strategy combining P75NTR knockdown with NGF overexpression. This approach not only preserves the pro-osteogenic effects of NGF but also mitigates its potential pro-inflammatory side effects.

In this study, our in vitro and in vivo results consistently showed that the dual-gene-modified BMSCs exhibited enhanced osteogenic differentiation and superior bone regeneration compared with single-gene modifications, highlighting the importance of targeting pyroptosis as a regulatory axis in bone tissue engineering. Furthermore, an allogeneic bone scaffold was employed as a structural carrier for cell delivery, providing a three-dimensional microenvironment to support cell attachment and retention at the defect site. Future studies incorporating scaffold-only controls and quantitative evaluation of scaffold-cell interactions would further clarify the independent contribution of biomaterials.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates an effective bone defect treatment strategy by combining advanced gene engineering technology with traditional tissue engineering methods. This work provides new insight into the interplay between neurotrophic signaling and inflammatory cell death in bone repair and offers a promising direction for the development of next-generation cell-based therapies. Future research will further explore different combinations of gene regulation and scaffold materials to optimize the construction and functionality of tissue-engineered bone, ultimately realizing its clinical application.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although an allogeneic bone scaffold was used as a structural carrier for cell delivery, a scaffold-only control group was not included, which limits the ability to fully determine the independent contribution of the biomaterial to bone regeneration. Second, degradation behavior of the scaffold was not quantitatively assessed in the present work, and therefore conclusions regarding its degradation properties were based on literature support rather than experimental validation. Future studies incorporating additional control groups and more comprehensive mechanistic analyses will further strengthen these conclusions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

SD rats were used for the in vivo bone defect experiments. All animals were male, aged 8 weeks at the time of surgery. Rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (24 ± 2°C), and ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of GLMC20243172, and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used. Male Sprague–Dawley rats aged 8 weeks were used in this study. Therefore, the potential influence of biological sex on bone regeneration outcomes could not be evaluated. This represents a limitation of the study, and future studies including both male and female animals are needed to determine whether sex-associated differences affect the regenerative efficacy of the engineered BMSC–scaffold constructs.

Cell lines

BMSCs were purchased from OriCell (Cyagen Biosciences, Guangzhou, China; RASMX-01001). According to the manufacturer’s information, these cells are derived from SD rat bone marrow and possess strong proliferative capacity and multipotent differentiation potential, including osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic lineages.

All cells were handled in accordance with standard aseptic procedures. The identity and quality of the purchased BMSCs were authenticated by the supplier. In addition, the mesenchymal phenotype of BMSCs was further confirmed in this study by flow cytometry, showing positive expression of CD29, CD44, and CD90 and negative expression of CD34. The cells were tested and confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma contamination before use.

Method details

Synthesis and characterization of Scaffolds

Cancellous bone allograft scaffolds (Cojoing, Beijing, China) were used as the biomaterial framework in this study. These scaffolds possess a porous three-dimensional trabecular-like microarchitecture that mimics the native bone extracellular matrix and provides a favorable environment for cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation. Bone allografts have been widely used as osteoconductive scaffolds in bone defect repair due to their biocompatibility, structural stability, and interconnected pore network. Prior to cell seeding, the bone scaffolds were handled under sterile conditions according to the manufacturer’s instructions.44 Allogeneic bone is harvested from suitable donors under sterile conditions, following rigorous serological and microbiological screening to rule out infectious diseases. The bone tissue is washed, cut to shape, and subjected to ethanol gradient degreasing and antigen removal to inactivate viruses; it is then preserved following freeze-drying and sterilisation.45,46 Genetically modified BMSCs were seeded onto the scaffolds and cultured for 1, 3, and 5 days to evaluate cell adhesion and proliferation. At each time point, the constructs were gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adherent cells and fixed using 2.5% glutaraldehyde. The samples were subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, vacuum-dried, and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold using a desktop sputter coater (Quorum Technologies, UK). The surface morphology of the scaffolds and cell–scaffold interactions were then examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Zeiss Gemini SEM 300, Germany) operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The compression and tensile experiments were tested using an electronic universal testing machine (CMT 6103, MTS, China).

Origin and culture of BMSCs

Primary BMSCs were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (RASMX-01001, Guangzhou, China). The cells were resuscitated and expanded in low-glucose dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (L-DMEM; Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 and saturated humidity. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days to maintain optimal growth conditions. Once the cell monolayer reached approximately 70–80% confluence, BMSCs were detached and re-plated for subsequent passages.

Identification of BMSCs

Quality authentication of the purchased BMSCs was confirmed by the supplier, with detailed information provided in Document S1. To further verify the BMSC phenotype, flow-cytometric analysis was performed. Cells were washed and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Solarbio, China) at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL, followed by staining with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies targeting typical MSC markers. The antibody panel included CD34-FITC (PA5-85917, eBioscience), CD44-PE (103007, BioLegend), CD90-PE (MA1-80650, eBioscience), and CD29-PE (102207, BioLegend). Fluorescence signals were acquired using a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) and processed with flow cytometry softwaree (FlowJo V10; FlowJo LLC, USA).

BMSCs transfection and grouping

Bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells were subjected to lentiviral modification to regulate the expression of NGF and P75NTR. Lentiviral particles carrying NGF-overexpression constructs or P75NTR-knockdown constructs (GENECHEM, Shanghai, China) were used for transduction. BMSCs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well and cultured until reaching approximately 70–80% confluence. Lentiviral infection mixtures were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol (GENECHEM, Shanghai, China), and the multiplicity of infection (MOI) was set to 50.47,48 The cells were incubated with the viral suspension for 16 h, after which the infection medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium. Transduction efficiency was evaluated 72 h post-infection by detecting fluorescent reporter expression using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was tested by the CCK8 assay in BMSCs. In brief, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours. Following removal of the culture medium, each well was supplemented with 100 μL of CCK-8 working solution prepared by mixing 10% CCK-8 reagent (C0037, Beyotime, China) with 90% complete medium. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo, USA). All sample conditions were tested in triplicate.

Animal model and surgical implantation

Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats aged eight weeks were obtained from SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). Animals were housed individually at the Experimental Animal Center of Guilin Medical College under controlled environmental conditions (24 °C; 12 h light/12 h dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All procedures followed the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guilin Medical College. For anesthesia, rats received 3% (w/v) pentobarbital sodium (0.1 mL/100 g; Ceva, France) before surgery. After shaving and disinfecting the operative site, a longitudinal incision was made over the lateral aspect of the right femoral condyle. The muscles and subcutaneous tissues were separated carefully to expose the distal femur. Following a previously described protocol,49 a circular bone defect (4.5 mm in diameter and 4.5 mm in depth) was created using a dental microdrill (Thomas, France). The defects were then filled with allogeneic bone scaffolds seeded with one of the following cell types: NGF+/BMSCs, P75NTR-/BMSCs, P75NTR-+NGF+/BMSCs, and BMSCs alone. After implantation, the surgical incision was sutured in layers, and animals were returned to individual cages with unrestricted activity. To minimize procedural distress, supplemental pentobarbital anesthesia was administered as needed. Euthanasia was performed 15 min after anesthetic administration by cervical spinal disruption.

Micro-CT analysis

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) was used to evaluate new bone formation within the defect region. Four weeks after implantation, the right distal femurs were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were scanned using a high-resolution 3D X-ray microscope (Zeiss Xradia 510 Versa) at 50 kV and 120 mA, with a voxel size of 9 μm. Reconstruction of the 3D datasets was carried out using CTAN analysis software. A cylindrical region of interest (ROI) corresponding to the original defect size (4.5 mm diameter × 4.5 mm depth) was selected for quantitative analysis. The following bone morphometric indices were calculated: bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV %), trabecular number (Tb.N) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). These parameters were used to assess the degree of bone regeneration and structural recovery within the critical-size defect.

Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining

After decalcification, bone specimens were passed through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) for dehydration and then embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissues were sectioned into 5-μm slices using a microtome. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard procedures. Histological evaluation and bone morphology assessment were performed using a Leica optical microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. For in vitro assays, n represents biologically independent experiments; for animal experiments, n represents the number of rats. Sample sizes for each analysis are stated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons among more than two groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All tests were two-sided. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures and legends as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
