ABSTRACT

Right heart catheterization (RHC) and echocardiography are integral in the assessment of pulmonary hypertension (PH) and cardiac function for lung transplant (LT) candidates. However, the impact of these metrics on post‐LT outcomes remains unclear. We performed a single‐center retrospective study of adult LT recipients from 2010 to 2022 to evaluate how RHC and echocardiographic variables relate to mortality, length of stay (LOS), duration of mechanical ventilation (MV), and rejection. An unsupervised cluster analysis was used to identify hemodynamic subgroups with adverse outcomes. Among 297 patients undergoing single or bilateral LT, most had interstitial lung disease (ILD; 52%), followed by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; 32%) and other diagnoses (16%); mortality did not differ by indication (COPD 51%, ILD 37%, other 45%; p = 0.10), by PH defined as mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg or pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) ≥ 2 Woods Units (p = 0.45 for each), or by PH classification (isolated post‐capillary, combined pre/post‐capillary, or no PH; p = 0.77). However, in COPD, a higher mPAP, right ventricular (RV) filling pressures, and echocardiographic indices of diastolic function and RV pressure overload were associated with greater mortality. Across the cohort, PH was associated with longer LOS, more MV days, and higher rejection rates. Unsupervised cluster analysis identified a subgroup with elevated biventricular filling pressures and RV dysfunction consistent with a congestive hemodynamic footprint that had worse event‐free post‐LT survival (p = 0.049). These findings highlight the value of integrated invasive and noninvasive hemodynamics beyond the assessment of PH in LT recipients.

1 Introduction

Right heart catheterization (RHC) is a standard component of pre‐lung transplant (LT) evaluation, providing invaluable invasive hemodynamic information reflecting heart‐lung physiology, including right ventricular (RV) function and pulmonary hypertension (PH). PH is common in patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), two of the largest groups of patients undergoing LT, and is associated with poorer survival [1, 2, 3]. Prior studies generally report PH predicts worse outcomes in LT recipients with COPD and ILD but data is conflicting and the specific reasons remain unclear [4, 5, 6]. Although the impact of PH in patients with heart failure (HF) has been well described, the role of HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in post‐LT outcomes remains less understood.

Relatively recent changes in PH definitions, including mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg (down from > 25 mmHg) and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of > 2 Wood units (down from 3 WU) have identified patients at increased mortality risk not previously captured by older definitions [7, 8, 9]. The preponderance of evidence suggested an inflection point of mortality and hospitalization risk at the re‐defined thresholds [7]. In addition, the impact of RHC data beyond the presence of PH, particularly diastolic dysfunction (DD) causing elevated filling pressures, or congestion, RV function, and their influence on outcomes post LT remains inadequately defined. Prior studies have incompletely captured DD by utilizing non‐invasive imaging definitions as opposed to standard invasive hemodynamic criteria [10]. The importance of an appropriate definition of DD (i.e., HFpEF) is paramount, particularly in a patient population where clinical symptoms of heart failure such as dyspnea may be difficult to distinguish from symptoms of end stage lung disease.

Therefore, from a large tertiary care center in South Texas, we sought to address the aforementioned limitations in the current literature by: (1) investigating the impact of contemporary definitions of PH among different disease etiologies on post‐LT outcomes, and (2) understanding the relationship of other invasive hemodynamic measurements including congestion and RV dysfunction by RHC as well as noninvasive measurements of cardiac function to gain insight into their influence on post‐LT morbidity and mortality. Using this approach, we attempt using agnostic, machine learning methodologies to define a congestive hemodynamic phenotype, characterized by elevated filling pressures with concurrent RV dysfunction distinct from isolated pulmonary vascular afterload, which we hypothesize impact post‐LT survival.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Study Population

Following institutional IRB approval, data was retrospectively collected on 297 adult patients (age > 18 years) with chronic lung disease who underwent lung transplantation at the University Hospital System in San Antonio, Texas, between 2010 and 2022. All patients had a minimum follow up of 1.5‐years. Data was collected from the electronic medical record system and stored in a secure, browser‐based database (REDCap) in accordance with institutional data governance policies.

Baseline variables included age, sex, race, lung disease etiology, tobacco use, and medical history such as preexisting heart disease and autoimmune conditions. Charleston comorbidity index was calculated in the standard manner based on demographic data. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease (ILD) and miscellaneous lung pathologies (including sarcoidosis and cystic fibrosis) were diagnosed with pulmonary function test (PFT), chest imaging and clinical assessment. PFT data were collected at the time of or near the pre‐transplant RHC and verified by a board‐certified pulmonologist.

2.2 Data Collection

The date of the lung transplant was used as a time reference to extract the most recent right heart catheterization (RHC) pre‐transplant. Standard lab values including complete blood counts and comprehensive metabolic panels were collected as well as basic vital signs at time of RHC or within 30 days of procedure. A full set of RHC values and cardiac outputs by indirect Fick calculations were collected. To assess the reliability of recorded RHC values, all patients (n = 115) with complete waveforms were reviewed in blinded fashion by a cardiologist (MIA). Specifically, the PCWP and PVR values were compared with automatically recorded numbers and there was good agreement (95% of the limits of agreement were within −1.89 to 6.91 mmHg for PCWP and −1.39 to 0.46 for PVR), Figure S1. Pre‐transplant echocardiogram and pulmonary function test (PFT) data were also collected. Post lung transplantation data such as length of stay (LOS), need for mechanical ventilation (MV), transfusion requirements, renal failure, stroke, etc. were individually adjudicated and extracted from the medical record.

2.3 Statistical Analysis

Subjects were divided into categories based on indication for transplant, that is, ILD, COPD, and miscellaneous, as well as PH subgroup, that is, pre‐capillary PH (mPAP > 20 mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, PVR > 2 WU), isolated post‐capillary pulmonary hypertension (Ipc‐PH; mPAP > 20 mmHg, PCWP > 15 mmHg, PVR ≤ 2 WU), and combined pre‐ and post‐capillary pulmonary hypertension (Cpc‐PH; mPAP > 20 mmHg, PCWP > 15 mmHg, PVR > 2 WU). Pre‐transplant data and post‐transplant outcomes were analyzed based on Dunn's multiple comparisons or Fisher's Exact tests where appropriate. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a complete case analysis approach. Participants with missing data for any covariates included in the model were excluded. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to visualize time‐to‐event outcomes.

2.4 Random Survival Forest

To identify features associated with clinical outcomes, we trained a random survival forest (RSF using the sksurv Python package [11]. Model inputs included echocardiographic indices alongside age, sex, race, and BMI. Prior to modeling, missing data were imputed using K‐Nearest Neighbor imputation (scikit‐learn Python package v. 3.8.12), specifically variables with < 50% missing data. The number of neighbors, (k) was treated as a hyperparameter during tuning and the optimal number of neighbors (k) was k = 5. No additional data transformation or scaling was required as RSF is invariant to monotonic transformations due to its tree‐based structure [12]. The dataset was split into training (80%) and test (20%) subsets, with similar rates of the events in each group. Hyperparameters tuning was performed on the training set and included the number of trees, maximum tree depth, minimum number of samples required to split a node, and minimum number of samples per leaf.

Model performance was evaluated using out‐of‐bag error estimates, Harrel's concordance index (c‐index), and time‐varying area under the curve (AUC). Feature importance was derived using a permutation‐based method with 100 iterations. Although the model was not used for outcome prediction, individual patient survival curves were generated using the training data and further subjected to K‐mean clustering to explore subgroup phenotypes of interest. The primary goal of this analysis was for feature selection and identification of clinically relevant variables.

2.5 Cluster Analysis (K‐Means)

Unsupervised cluster analysis was performed using K‐means on a dataset comprising 59 features that included clinical, demographic, echocardiographic, and hemodynamic parameters. To reduce dimensionality and prevent overfitting, regularization was applied, resulting in the exclusion of majority (~60%) of features for the final model. The optimal number of clusters was determined to be three based on the maximization of the silhouette score (ratio of inter‐ vs intra‐cluster variance) and the minimum Bayes Information Criteria.

Comparisons between clusters were performed with a Kruskal Wallis for continuous variables or Fisher's Exact test, for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (Version 15.1, College Station, TX) and the scikit‐learn package in Python (v. 3.8.12). Statistical significance was defined by a two‐sided p < 0.05. Additional methodological details were provided in the supplement.

3 Results

3.1 Summary of Patient Demographics, Comorbidities, Hemodynamics and Outcomes

Pre‐transplant patient demographics, comorbidities, hemodynamics and clinical characteristics of patients in the cohort (n = 297) from each disease etiology are outlined in Table 1. Most transplanted patients had ILD at 52%, 32% had COPD, and the remaining 16% were classified as miscellaneous. PFT data demonstrated the expected range of FEV1 and FEV1/FVC values in COPD patients and TLC values in ILD patients consistent with their pathophysiology. There were notable differences in patient demographics, including age, BMI, and race. Comorbidity burden such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and atrial arrhythmias was no different among categories of lung pathology. Patients with COPD had a lower prevalence of diabetes mellitus, while COPD and ILD patients had a greater prevalence of CAD compared to miscellaneous etiologies. With respect to invasive hemodynamics, right atrial pressure (RAP), RV end diastolic pressure (RVEDP), and PCWP were higher in COPD patients as compared to the other groups, with the pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) being lowest in COPD patients (3.9 vs. 6.3 [ILD] and 6.5 [misc], p = 0.001). RAP/PCWP ratio was higher in COPD and ILD compared to miscellaneous groups, while PVR was lowest in those with COPD and similar in ILD and miscellaneous groups. Length of stay post‐transplant was higher in ILD and miscellaneous lung disease at 16 days versus 13 in COPD patients. There was no significant difference between mortality among the groups (p = 0.10), though numerically higher percentage in COPD (51%) vs ILD (37%) and miscellaneous (45%).

3.2 Predictors of Mortality

We performed Cox regression analyses on all characteristics from Table 1 in our entire cohort to identify which, if any, are associated with clinical outcomes (Table S1). Of all parameters tested, only Charlson Comorbidity Index predicted mortality after adjustment for age, sex, race, and BMI. Of note, disease etiology did not significantly associate with mortality (follow up: 150 months; p = 0.72, Figure 1). Given differences in pathophysiology, we hypothesized that disease etiology was a mediating factor as a reason for the lack of significant differences. To test this, we performed Cox regression analyses in each disease group (Figure 1). This showed that a lower LVEDd, LA diameter, higher E/A ratio, estimated RVSP, RVEDP and mPAP were predictors of mortality in COPD but not in other groups (Table 2). No differences in mortality were attributable to chronic lung disease type (p = 0.72, Figure 2). 3.3 Cohort Analysis by Definition of PH by mPAP and PVR Criteria

We separated our cohort based on presence or absence of PH according to updated criteria based on mPAP and PVR (ESC/ERS 2022 PH Guidelines; Table 3) [8]. Those with PH by mPAP criteria group had lower PAPi values, longer LOS, more days of MV, and a higher percentage of post LT rejection (median 5 vs. 7, p = 0.008; median 16 vs. 12, p = 0.046; and 34% vs. 19%, p = 0.008 respectively). Survival curves by presence of PH defined by mPAP or PVR alone demonstrated no difference in mortality (Figure S2, p = 0.98 by mPAP, p = 0.67 by PVR; log‐rank test).

Breakdown of PH by pre‐capillary PH, Cpc‐PH, Ipc‐PH and absence of PH showed that those with cpc‐PH had higher estimated left‐sided filling pressures by echocardiography as expected, but also higher estimated and actual PA pressures (Table S2). Pulmonary artery pulsatility Index (PAPi) was lowest in Cpc‐PH (median 5.3 [2, 12]) and Ipc‐PH (3.7, [2, 5]), and these groups also had more days of MV post LT. Ipc‐PH had the lowest PAPi and highest RA pressures despite the lowest PVR values. Ipc‐PH tended to have the highest risk of post LT rejection (44%, p = 0.06). Mortality was similar across all groups, and survival curves demonstrated no significant difference in event‐free survival (Figure S3, p = 0.93 by log‐rank test). In exploratory analyses using legacy pulmonary hypertension thresholds, there was no difference in post‐transplant event‐free survival for patients with mPAP ≥ 25 mmHg (p = 0.81) or for those with a PVR ≥ 3 (p = 0.98), similar to our analysis with the current definitions of PH (Figure S4). Patients with mPAP ≥ 25 mmHg had a longer post‐transplant length of stay than those with mPAP < 25 mmHg (16 vs 13 days, p = 0.004). Similarly, patients with PVR ≥ 3 Wood units had a slightly longer length of stay than those with PVR < 3 Wood units (16 vs 14 days, p = 0.049).

3.4 Variable Importance and Cluster Analysis

To identify which features associated with mortality, we trained a random survival forest model to agnostically identify features that were important for risk prediction (Figure 3). The model was found to converge with mean C‐statistic 0.78 (Figure 3B). Patients were then agnostically stratified to one of three risk profiles, low, medium, and high, solely using hemodynamic and other clinical characteristics (Figure 3A). To identify which features were most important for stratifying risk in the cohort, we performed permutation analysis to obtain variable importance. This analysis identified that total lung capacity, intraventricular septal thickness and PCWP pressures were most important for risk stratification (Figure 3A). The fact that these parameters on their own did not linearly associate with survival further suggests that a nonlinear combination of these parameters was necessary for risk stratification.

We then grouped subjects based on clinical, demographic, echocardiographic and hemodynamic parameters by cluster analysis as described above. The model created three clusters: in general, cluster 1 grouped patients with higher mPAP, highest PAPi and PVR, lowest PA compliance and cardiac index and lower PCWP pressures; cluster 2 had the lowest mPAP, PCWP, right sided filling pressures and highest PA compliance; lastly, cluster 3 had the highest overall biventricular filling pressures, particularly PCWP, and lowest PAPi values, demonstrating a congested phenotype with worse RV function (Table 4). Although there were significant differences in the etiology of lung disease between the clusters, each cluster contained subjects with each disease etiology. 4 Discussion

In this single‐center study from a large tertiary care center in South Texas spanning a period of 12 years, we sought to collect relevant clinical parameters including invasive hemodynamics and echocardiographic parameters, to understand if physiologic phenotyping of patients with varied etiologies of lung disease undergoing LT was associated with outcomes post LT. This analysis found that disease etiology modulates prognostication of both invasive and non‐invasive hemodynamics, specifically diastolic dysfunction and elevated right sided filling pressures in predicting mortality in patients with COPD. Through complementary approaches, we observe the association between hemodynamic variables and clinical outcomes is non‐linear; where each hemodynamic variable's effect on mortality alone is not proportional and non‐additive associations between hemodynamic variables are present which stratify risk. Using machine learning based clustering and survival analyses, we were able to demonstrate that a combination of both hemodynamic and pulmonary function testing parameters enhances risk prognostication, and RV function is a central determinant of identifying adverse outcomes in patient subgroups that experience high afterload.

Our study's single‐center cohort consisted of predominantly Hispanic and male LT recipients with ILD. These limitations of biological, genetic and social factors linked to sex and ethnicity should be considered when interpreting our findings of cardiopulmonary hemodynamics and right ventricular adaptation in chronic lung disease and HFpEF in broader transplant populations [13, 14]. Conversely, this population is under represented, and adds value to the existing literature studying prevalence of cardio‐metabolic risk and the impact RV‐pulmonary adaptation in the context of chronic lung disease and HFpEF. Despite the limited generalizability, it nonetheless underscores the additive impact of hemodynamic phenotypes and how they may shape post‐LT outcomes that remain underexplored. Therefore, future multi‐center studies (or protocols) with broader demographic characterization in addition to hemodynamic phenotyping are warranted to determine whether this influences pre‐transplant risk stratification and post‐transplant recovery. When evaluating event free survival between those with PH defined by mPAP > 20 or PVR ≥ 2, our analysis could not distinguish survival up to 150 months post‐LT. Using legacy PH thresholds, neither mPAP ≥ 25 mmHg nor PVR ≥ 3 Wood units was associated with differences in post‐transplant event‐free survival, similar to our primary analyses using contemporary definitions. However, both thresholds were associated with longer post‐transplant length of stay, suggesting that more advanced pulmonary vascular derangement by older criteria may be more relevant for identifying patients at risk of early post‐transplant decompensation and greater resource utilization rather than longer‐term survival. Notably, ROC analysis did not identify a meaningful mPAP or PVR cutoff predictive of survival, supporting the interpretation that a focus on a binary definition of PH as a predictor of survival post‐LT is too narrow a lens through which to assess post‐transplant risk. Prior data focusing on COPD and ILD patients undergoing LT demonstrated the association between PH prevalence and mortality risk, although with mixed results in post‐LT mortality in the ILD despite their high burden of PH [3, 4, 15]. However, PH defined by mPAP alone within our cohort did suggest increased mortality in patients with COPD with a trend towards significance in ILD (p = 0.11).

In our cohort, COPD patients exhibited higher left‐sided filling pressures, worse diastolic dysfunction, and greater prevalence of Cpc‐PH supportive of a pulmonary congestive footprint akin to HFpEF‐like physiology. In such patients, mild elevations in PCWP or diastolic filling ratios (E/A, E/e′) may precipitate pulmonary congestion and impose disproportionate RV dysfunction. Cardiometabolic risk (hypertension, obesity, and metabolic syndrome), common in COPD, may contribute to ventricular stiffness and reduced compliance, making the RV more sensitive to small increments in afterload [16]. Though hypothesis‐ generating, our cluster analysis that identified patients with such a congestive phenotype having higher biventricular filling pressures (elevated RAP and PCWP), with worse RV performance (low PAPi) in the setting of increased pulsatile load [17] (PCWP), which predicted worse mortality in COPD (‘Cluster 3’) consistent with a maladaptive interplay of RV dysfunction, diastolic dysfunction, and pulmonary vascular stiffness evident in advanced HFpEF; this likely reflects a disease‐specific to diastolic stress and afterload sensitivity in COPD. In contrast, ILD patients display predominantly pre‐capillary PH with relatively preserved RV–PA coupling (elevated PAPi and PVR), suggesting that advanced structural lung disease, rather than pure hemodynamic load alone, may dominate outcomes within this group [3, 4, 18]. Unfortunately, we were unable further characterize RV function by RV free wall strain (RVFWS), a more sensitive measure of RV performance [19, 20].

In patients undergoing LT, the presence and severity of PH have been the traditional focus of the hemodynamic evaluation, especially the role of parenchymal lung disease on RV load and adaptation. Our findings indicate that the presence of a congestive phenotype and RV dysfunction are more predictive of adverse post‐LT outcomes than PA pressures alone [3, 4, 18, 21, 22]. In our cohort of patients with preserved ejection fraction and a PCWP > 15 mmHg several exhibited RVD by RHC. The combination of elevated filling pressures and RVD in HFpEF, defined as the congestive hemodynamic footprint, was associated with poor clinical outcomes underscoring the prognostic significance in our patient population [19, 23]. Recognizing these hemodynamics and incorporating indices of biventricular congestion by invasive RHC (e.g., PCWP, RVEDP, and RA pressure) and echocardiographic assessments (e.g., E/a, LA diameter, and lateral E/e') into existing protocols can complement standard PH screening while providing practical implications for pre‐LT risk stratification. Identifying patients with combined features of Cpc‐PH or HFpEF physiology may help refine candidacy decisions, as these patients could be at higher risk for primary graft dysfunction, prolonged postoperative ventilation, or early graft failure [3, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Prior studies have explored PH, RV function, or left ventricular diastolic dysfunction as isolated predictors of primary graft dysfunction, few have examined the integrated impact of Ipc‐PH, Cpc‐PH, pre‐capillary, and congestive hemodynamic phenotypes in end‐stage lung disease [20, 21, 22]. Diastolic dysfunction in transplant recipients with PH defined by echocardiographic criteria alone (per the 2016 ASE/ESC guidelines), in a cohort with a resting mean PCWP < 15 mmHg, demonstrated resolution of diastolic dysfunction post‐LT reported in a single study [10]. While this study suggests dynamic resolution of diastolic abnormalities after transplantation, the study relied on non‐invasive measures, further questioning how parenchymal lung disease influences myocardial relaxation. In contrast, the gold standard for characterizing HFpEF and diastolic dysfunction remains RHC, which provides direct assessment of the ventricular–vascular interplay captured in our dataset [28]. Furthermore, the interaction between RV dilation, interventricular dependence, and diastolic filling pressures in severe pre‐capillary PH is well described, yet heart–lung interactions in end‐stage lung disease under spontaneous respiration remain poorly understood [29]. Our results suggest that these interactions contribute meaningfully to the congestive hemodynamic footprint observed which may represent a key mechanistic bridge between pulmonary vascular disease and myocardial dysfunction. Finally, recognizing this phenotype could lead to clinically actionable insights improving hemodynamic optimization, risk stratification and treatment of HFpEF in the context of perioperative assessment and post‐operative surveillance in LT assessment.

An important limitation when interpreting invasive hemodynamics in any patient is whether measurements were obtained exclusively at rest or included provocative maneuvers such as fluid challenge or exercise testing. This is relevant because while a diagnosis of HFpEF can be made by resting RHC, there are clearly those with diastolic dysfunction only unmasked with such maneuvers [30].

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the importance of invasive hemodynamics in patients undergoing LT with PH by contemporary definitions, indicating those patients with a biventricular congestive phenotype, effectively those with HFpEF and RVD, may have worse overall survival. In addition, PH appears to predict worse outcomes most clearly in patients with COPD.

5 Limitations

This study has several limitations. Despite the relatively large patient cohort, as a single‐center retrospective analysis, our findings are limited in their generalizability and may not reflect broader transplant populations. However, standardization of data collection and hemodynamic interpretation within a single institution strengthens internal validity and reduces variability that can confound multicenter datasets. Our study cohort consisted predominantly of Hispanic participants contributing valuable insights into a historically underrepresented population in the transplant literature. Incomplete availability of invasive hemodynamics, echocardiographic parameters, and clinical data across all patients are inherent challenges of retrospective study designs which impact our findings. Although RHC is the clinical standard for assessing cardiac filling pressures, PCWP may not always reflect left‐sided filling pressures (i.e., left ventricular end diastolic pressure) in select physiologic settings, including dynamic hyperinflation or some valvular heart disease and this relationship may vary across underlying lung disease subtypes.

We acknowledge that the absence of advanced right ventricular indices such as strain or 3D imaging restricts detailed mechanistic interpretation, however, measures including TAPSE, PAPi, and RVEDP were used as validated surrogates of RV performance. Random survival forests and unsupervised clustering were exploratory, not externally validated, and hypothesis‐generating and were used to identify patterns and variables of physiological relevance rather than to build predictive models. Future work incorporating external validation of models will be required to confirm generalizability. Multiple subgroup analyses across disease etiology, PH classification, and hemodynamic clusters raise the risk of type I error; however, multiplicity tests were applied where appropriate to reduce the potential for spurious associations. Unequal subgroup sizes may limit statistical power to detect survival differences by mPAP, PVR, and PH subtypes and the absence of significant differences should be interpreted with caution. Variability in timing between RHC and echocardiography may have introduced bias through fluctuations in hemodynamic parameters; However, this reflects standard clinical protocol in transplant evaluation and a constraint of retrospective study design. Future prospective studies aligning RHC and echocardiograms can minimize temporal effects in predictive models for transplant evaluation. Transplant laterality may have affected outcomes since single and bilateral lung transplantation have been associated with different survival patterns in prior studies. As this was not a primary aim of our study, the impact of laterality cannot be separated from potential confounders such as disease subtype, pulmonary vascular burden, RV function, and surgical selection. Furthermore, the study spanned 12 years; therefore, temporal changes in transplant selection, perioperative management and post‐transplant care may have influenced outcomes. Although the program's approach to invasive hemodynamic assessment by right heart catheterization remained stable during the study period, unmeasured effects over the study period cannot be fully excluded.

Finally, the observed association between congestion phenotype and mortality in COPD but not ILD may reflect both biological heterogeneity and residual confounding related to comorbidities or LV filling pressures. Secondary outcomes such as rejection, length of stay, and mechanical ventilation duration are multifactorial and cannot be attributed solely to pre‐transplant hemodynamics. Despite these limitations, the reproducibility of physiologic trends across analytic approaches supports the robustness and contextual nature of our findings. Future prospective, multicenter studies with external model validation will be essential to confirm and refine the clinical applicability of the congestive hemodynamic footprint.
