==============================
Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a major infectious disease of cloven-hoofed animals that causes substantial economic losses. FMD in buffaloes was neglected in the surveillance program, therefore, this study aimed to identify and characterize the FMD virus (FMDV) and determine its associated risk factors in buffaloes in Bangladesh. A total of 150 tissue samples were purposively collected from 16 FMD-suspected buffalo farms, representing a study population of 501 buffaloes across three areas of Bangladesh. The samples were analyzed for FMDV and further tested for the serotypes (O, A, Asia 1) by RT-PCR test. The VP1 gene was sequenced and phylogenetic trees were studied. Epidemiological data were collected through a structured questionnaire. Logistic regression models were used to identify outbreak-associated risk factors. Overall, 29.94% (150/501) FMD prevalence and three serotypes, include O (n = 19), A (n = 7), and Asia 1(n = 4), were identified by RT-PCR. The case fatality, attack, and mortality rates of buffalo calves were 32.91%, 63.2%, and 20.8%, respectively. Potential risk factors identified for FMD in buffaloes including age (calves; AOR:14.07, 95%CI:6.37–31.07), grazing with cattle (yes; AOR:19.09, 95%CI:4.49–81.18), deworming practices (No, AOR:8.71, 95%CI:2.20–34.51), isolation shed (No; AOR:5.82, 95%CI:1.41–23.95), and grazing area (bush; AOR:20.78, 95%CI:5.07–85.12). Phylogenetic analysis of serotype O revealed a 95% sequence similarity with recent isolates from Bhutan and Nepal, while serotype A exhibited a 99% similarity with isolates from India and Bangladesh. Therefore, implementing appropriate control strategies is essential for reducing risk factors and minimizes the occurrence and spread of FMD.

1 Introduction

Buffalo is an important livestock species that greatly contributes to the livestock sector, especially to Bangladesh's rural economy after cattle (Ali et al., 2025; Habib et al., 2023; Pehan et al., 2025). The global production of buffalo (Bubalus bubalis) for red meat has become a promising economic endeavor, especially in tropical and subtropical countries, including Bangladesh (Maheswarappa et al., 2022). Currently, there are only 1.524 million buffalo in Bangladesh (DLS, 2024). But buffalo farming is popular nowadays for its high-quality milk, nutritious meat, and its effectiveness as a draft animal. However, various constraints badly affect buffalo production in Bangladesh. The widespread distribution of cattle and buffalo diseases across different agro ecological zones results in high annual mortality and increases veterinary costs (Ahmed et al., 2025; Moller et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2022a). The expansion of buffalo production is economically hindered by many infectious and contagious diseases, such as subclinical mastitis, and foot and mouth disease (FMD), which lower productivity and have a high impact on the value chain of the livestock sector (Ali et al., 2025; Govindaraj et al., 2021; Rahman et al., 2020; Singha et al., 2023). FMD is a serious infectious disease of Cloven-hoofed animals, like pigs, cattle, sheep, goats, and buffaloes, caused by the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) (Ali & Giasuddin, 2020; Ali et al., 2025). Seven serotypes of FMDV namely O, A, C, Asia 1, SAT 1, SAT 2, and SAT 3 have been identified throughout the world (Brito et al., 2017). However, only O, A, and Asia 1 serotypes are circulating in Bangladesh (Ali et al., 2019; Islam et al., 2021).

In Asia and Africa, particularly Bangladesh, FMD is extremely prevalent (Brito et al., 2017). This disease directly results in decreased milk supply, reduced draft power, stunted growth, abortion, extended breeding time, and, lastly, young animal mortality. Indirect losses include marketing restrictions, inferior production technology, and expenditure on treatment and control (Humphreys et al., 2025). The morbidity rate is 100% by FMDV in the vulnerable population. There is a 100% mortality rate for young suckling calves, and the risk of dying from myocarditis might rise to 50% (Govindaraj et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). In endemic countries, due to FMD, an annual economic loss of between USD 6.5 and 21 billion (Humphreys et al., 2025) and in Bangladesh approximately USD 2.22 billion (Giasuddin et al., 2020). Diagnosis of this disease is performed by using laboratory techniques such as virus neutralization, complement fixation, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Kouato et al., 2018). The standard diagnostic method for FMDV detection is reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), which is highly sensitive and specific (Ali et al., 2019; Rahman et al., 2022b). The molecular epidemiology of FMDV and the identification of the origin of recently discovered strains have been studied through sequencing of the viral gene that expresses viral protein 1 (VP1) and the creation of phylogenetic trees.

However, there is a lack of substantial scientific evidence regarding buffalo-specific molecular characterization of FMDV and its related risk factors in Bangladesh. Therefore, this study aimed to identify circulating FMDV serotypes and associated risk factors in buffaloes in Bangladesh to support national FMD control programs.

2 Material and methods

2.1 Study areas

A cross-sectional study design with purposive sampling was employed at a single point in time. Samples collected from the primary outbreak zone covering high risk farms at the early outbreak phase (day 0–7) according to guidelines from the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). The study locations were selected based on buffalo population and geographical diversity, including coastal land (Sadar and Charfashion Upazila of Bhola district, and Anowara Upazila of Chattogram district), and char land (Ishwardi Upazila of Pabna district) (Fig. 1). These selections were based on FMD outbreaks reports, particularly in regions with a significant buffalo population, including coastal and char areas.Fig. 1 Map showing the outbreak area in Pabna (A), Bhola (B), and Chattogram (C) districts of Bangladesh. The map was created using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) software ESRI ArcGIS version 10.6.1.

2.2 Data collection

A total of 16 buffalo farms were surveyed based on FMD outbreak notification. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a prestructured questionnaire (Supplementary file 1). The questionnaire includes age, gender, clinical history, data on the farming system, immunization, deworming history, floor structure, pasture land pattern, housing, geographical region, interaction with other animals, quarantine and isolation facilities, disinfectant use, and wildlife and domestic animal connections. The breed of buffaloes included in this study was native types. The farmers are not adequately informed about modern veterinary services. The animals were raised under traditional management systems without adequate housing facilities. They were commonly reared in open pasture fields known as free-ranging systems. On the island, buffaloes share grazing areas with cattle and other wildlife in bush land (Fig. 2). Farmers do not routinely practice vaccination or deworming in buffaloes.Fig. 2 Rearing system of buffaloes in Char land (left), open shelter (middle), bush area (right).

2.3 Tissue sample collection

To our knowledge, there is little published data on the prevalence of FMD in buffalo in Bangladesh, although a lot of relevant studies have been published on cattle. A previous study reported of 20.7% hospital-level prevalence in the Sylhet region of Bangladesh (Ujjal et al., 2018). Assuming an expected prevalence of 20% with a desired absolute precision of 5% and a 95% confidence interval, the expected sample size for this study is 246. The finite population correction formula is used to adjust the sample size, enhancing efficiency and representativeness. The adjusted sample size is 165 after the finite population correction.

Where, n= expected sample size; Z = Z-score (e.g., 1.96 for 95% confidence); P= expected prevalence; d= desired precision (e.g., 0.05 or 5%); nadj= Adjusted sample size; and N= Total buffalo population in the outbreak area.

From a study population of 501 buffaloes across 16 farms, 150 tissue samples (epithelial tissues from oral and interdigital vesicles) were purposively collected. These samples were collected from buffaloes showing pathognomonic clinical signs of FMD, as described by Ali et al. (2025) and Azeem et al. (2020) (Fig. 3). To optimize diagnostic efficiency, these samples were divided into 30 pools (5-in-1 ratio) and subjected to further molecular identification by RT-PCR test. The samples were collected into virus transport medium and shifted to the laboratory in the shortest time, maintaining a cool temperature and stored at −80 °C until processing and testing.Fig. 3 Clinical signs of FMD in buffalo (vesicles in interdigital space and mouth).

2.4 Molecular identification

A required part of the epithelial tissue samples was ground in phosphate-buffered saline solution to prepare 300 µl of supernatant fluid suspension which was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The total genomic RNA was extracted using a commercial Viral RNA extraction kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer's procedure. For the detection of FMDV, a VP1-based PCR diagnostic assay was employed and all extracted RNAs were subjected to RT-PCR test. The RT-PCR assay was performed targeting the 5′ UTR and 3D regions for the detection of FMDV utilizing universal primers (Table 1) and RT-PCR temperature profile (Table 2) with the One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Abclonal, USA). The RT-PCR test protocol was derived from Ali et al. (2019). Then, the positive pooled samples were tested individually for FMDV. Subsequently, serotyping of FMDV in pooled samples was conducted utilizing specific primers for serotypes A, O, and Asia 1 from FMD-positive samples using appropriate primers listed in Table 1. The master mix volume per reaction was 25 μl, and the primers, and thermal profile are described in Table 2. The amplified product was then run on a 1.5% agarose gel together with a 100-bp DNA ladder stained with Ethidium bromide, and the amplified band was visible through the UV transilluminator. 2.5 VP1 gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The PCR-amplified products from three FMDV-positive samples were sent to a commercial entity (Macrogen, Korea) for cycle sequencing of the VP1 conserved region, utilizing primers designed by Knowles et al. (2016) for FMD serotypes O and A. Followed by, sequences were uploaded to the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genebank/) for quality evaluation, followed by editing, trimming, and assembly into a consensus sequence using BioEdit version 7.2. Subsequently, 25 and 40 aligned sequences of FMDV serotype O and serotype A were received from the BLAST database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/), respectively. Ultimately, two distinct phylogenetic trees have been constructed utilizing the Neighbor-Joining method with Tamura–Nei’s model in the MEGA 11 software (Tamura & Nei, 1993). To confirm statistical significance, the constancy of the branches of the phylogenetic tree was tested using 1000 iterations. Missing data were handled using the partial deletion option with a 95% site coverage cutoff, and rate variation among sites was modeled with a gamma distribution.

2.6 Statistical analysis

Logistic regression analysis was applied to investigate the potential risk factors associated with FMD in buffaloes. At first, univariable logistic regression was conducted to determine factors such as location, sex, age, grazing practices, deworming, housing conditions, and isolation practices that could affect disease prevalence. Variables demonstrating a p-value of 0.05 or lower in the univariable analysis were incorporated into a multivariable logistic regression model to address potential confounding factors and identify the most significant predictors. Prior to running the final model, we assessed for multicollinearity by calculating the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for all continuous and categorical predictor variables, where VIF values below 5 were considered acceptable. Furthermore, based on initial findings of extremely high odds ratios and wide confidence intervals indicative of sparse data or quasi-complete separation, the final multivariable model was analyzed using Firth's Penalized Maximum Likelihood Estimation (Firth, 1993). The findings were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) together with 95% confidence intervals (CI), with a p-value of 0.05 or lower considered statistically significant. The information on the population at risk, mortality, attack rate, and case fatality rate was also calculated. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 14.2 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). A Sankey diagram was generated using the highcharter package in R (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) based on the categorical variables including animal age, sex, and location (Fig. 4). We calculated the attack rate, mortality, and case fatality of buffalo calves due to FMD by the following formulae:Attack rate= (Number of FMD cases/Total population) × 100;

Mortality rate= (Number of death/Total population) × 100;

Case fatality rate= (Number of death/ Number of FMD cases) × 100

In univariable logistic regression analysis (Table 5), the prevalence rates of FMD in buffalo in Chattogram, Bhola, and Pabna were 16.47% (95% CI: 9.3–26.08), 26.78% (95% CI: 20.51–33.80), and 35.62% (95% CI: 29.47–42.13), respectively. 3.4 Phylogenetic analysis of FMDV serotypes O and A

Phylogenetic analyses of the VP1 gene of FMDV serotypes O and A (Fig. 6) have been conducted using MEGA 11 and the Neighbor-Joining method. The BAN/BLRI/CTG6/2024 isolate from Chittagong, serotype O clustered closely with recent isolates from Bhutan (MT276848/BHU/9/2019) and Nepal (MT276850/NEP/4/2019), as indicated by high bootstrap values (95%), suggesting regional circulation of similar strains. This isolate exhibited variance from the older Bangladeshi isolate (e.g., O/BAN/TA/Dh-299/2016), indicating genetic divergence over time. Similarly, the A serotype BAN/BLRI/PAB12/2024 and BAN/BLRI/BHO1/2024 isolates from Pabna and Bhola were found to cluster with the recent Indian isolate (A/ICFMD196/2022) and the Bangladeshi isolate (BAN/DH/Ke-391/2020), demonstrating bootstrap support (99%). The divergence of these isolates from the older Bangladeshi isolate (BD/BAU/ML3/2013) might underscore the evolutionary dynamics of FMDV. Following that, the sequences were added to GenBank with accession numbers PQ667751 (Serotype A), PQ667752 (Serotype A), and PQ667750 (Serotype O). 4 Discussion

Globally, FMD is a transboundary viral disease of cattle, swine, sheep, deer, goats, buffaloes, and other animals with divided hooves, causing significant losses in the livestock sector (Azeem et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2023; Woldemariyam et al., 2022). In Bangladesh, FMD virus has been reported in multiple susceptible livestock species, including cattle, buffaloes, goats, sheep, and pigs (Ali et al., 2019; Ali et al., 2025; Rahman et al., 2022b). Unlike most previous investigations that focused solely on serological or molecular screening, this study is among the few in Bangladesh to integrate epidemiological risk factor analysis with RT-PCR-based molecular detection of FMDV specifically in buffaloes. This study is particularly novel because buffaloes-despite being highly susceptible to FMD and economically important in Bangladesh-remain severely underrepresented in regional FMD research compared with cattle. By focusing exclusively on buffalo populations across diverse ecological zones, this study fills an important knowledge gap in South Asia.

4.1 Prevalence and burden of FMD in buffaloes

The overall prevalence of FMD in this investigation in buffaloes was 29.94%. Similar results were reported in two studies at 23.3% and 20.71% of FMD in buffaloes across different areas of Bangladesh, respectively (Rahman et al., 2020; Ujjal et al., 2018). In the current study, buffalo calves (<1 year) were more susceptible to FMD-related mortality and had a higher case fatality rates. Other recent studies also found that both calves and adults were infected but mortality occurred only in calves (Sahoo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). In addition, another study reported a high fatality rate among cattle and buffalo calves (Abd-Ellatieff et al., 2023). Younger animals are more vulnerable to FMDV infection because of an underdeveloped humoral immune response, which can reduce herd immunity and facilitate the introduction and transmission of FMDV (Chakraborty et al., 2014; Islam et al., 2021).

4.2 Risk factors

In the current study, location, and age of the animals are significantly responsible for differences in the FMD prevalence. Specially, among the three districts, Pabna district had a significantly higher FMD prevalence (35.62%) than the Chattogram and Bhola districts. Similarly, another study reported the highest number of cases in the Chattogram division (Rahman et al., 2020), highlighting the potential influence of regional factors. The difference may result from variations in climate, farming practices, animal management systems, and sample sizes (Ahmed et al., 2025; Ullah et al., 2023). In addition, calves were more prone to FMD than heifers and adults. These findings were supported by Giasuddin et al. (2017), who reported that young age animals were more vulnerable than adults and older age groups (Giasuddin et al., 2017). Younger animals are more susceptible to FMDV infection due to weak humoral defense, which may decrease herd immunity and hence allow for the entry and spread of FMDV (Chakraborty et al., 2014; Elnekave et al., 2015). Another reason behind this is that calves are more susceptible due to irregular or no vaccination history, resulting in the absence of maternal antibodies, as maternal antibodies provides passive immunity against pathogens (Bok et al., 2018). On the other hand, Ujjal et al. (2018) reported a higher prevalence of FMD in young buffaloes (36.08%) than in adults (15.10%) and calves (5.60%). This is because their maternally derived antibodies persist in their bodies before they reach the weaning age (Ujjal et al., 2018).

According to findings, buffaloes that share their grazing land with cattle were significantly affected by FMD more than those that do not share their grazing land (Table 5). This statement was supported by other study findings, such as common grazing areas being more prone to FMD transmission in the Southern part of Bangladesh (Ali et al., 2019; Rahman et al., 2020). Buffaloes that were not dewormed were significantly more affected by FMD than dewormed buffaloes. Regular administration of anthelmintic (levamisole) alongside each FMD vaccine dosage is crucial to boosting the buffalo’s immune system response against different FMD virus serotypes (El-shahedy et al., 2015). Isolation of infected animals from sick animals can reduce disease spread. Farmers who had used an isolation shed had significantly lower FMD incidence and spread in buffaloes than those who didn’t use one. Separation of healthy and infected animals by natural boundaries, rivers, mountain passes, quarantine, or control of animal movements can be an effective way to limit the spread of the disease (Rahman et al., 2020). Grazing in bush areas with wildlife contact was another potential risk factor for FMD in buffaloes in Bangladesh. Buffaloes that grazed in bush areas were significantly more affected by FMD than Charland (25.43%). Likewise, Dubie and Negash (2021) identified contact between domestic animals and hoofed wildlife as a significant risk factor for disease occurrence. Moreover, in Ethiopia, Molla et al. (2010) also found a higher prevalence of FMD in animals that were regularly in contact with wildlife (Molla et al., 2010). In this study, maintaining quarantine of new buffaloes had a significant effect on FMD spread in buffaloes. It is feasible to control FMD through risk-based vaccination programs, quarantine procedures, animal transportation restrictions, isolation of affected animals, and appropriate carcass disposal (Rahman et al., 2020).

In this study, the authors found three serotypes of FMD virus namely O, A, and Asia-1 where the prevalence of the O serotypes was very high. These findings align with Siddique et al. (2018), who stated that serotype O is most prevalent in Bangladesh among these three serotypes of FMD virus. Additionally, three serotypes of FMDV, namely serotype A, O, and Asia 1 are currently circulating in Bangladesh with proportions of 10–15%, 80–85%, and 5%, respectively (Nandi et al., 2015).

4.3 Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic placement of recent Bangladeshi FMDV isolates from buffaloes indicates continued regional viral exchange and temporal evolution. The serotype O isolate BAN/BLRI/CTG6/2024 grouped with recent strains from Bhutan and Nepal, whereas the serotype A isolates BAN/BLRI/PAB12/2024 and BAN/BLRI/BHO1/2024 showed clustered with Indian and recent Bangladeshi strains, suggesting patterns of regional transmission and genetic evolution (Ali et al., 2025). The variation observed in older Bangladeshi isolates highlights the continuous adaptation of FMDV (Hossain et al., 2023). The results highlight the importance of ongoing molecular surveillance to track the evolution of emerging lineages and sub-lineages, assess vaccine effectiveness, and enhance control strategies. Future investigations on cross-species transmission and spillover of FMDV will improve comprehension of FMDV evolution and epidemiology. In addition, a whole genome sequence is needed to investigate the genomic differences between buffaloes and other species.

A limitation of this study was the limited scope of molecular screening of all 501 buffaloes due to resource and logistical restrictions. Although molecular confirmation was conducted on a subgroup of clinical buffaloes, subsequent research should employ thorough screening at the individual animal level to identify subclinical infections or buffaloes with low clinical signs. Furthermore, sampling was restricted to buffaloes, while other susceptible species, such as cattle and goats, were not included in the study, thereby limiting opportunities for interspecies comparisons. A limited number of sequenced samples in this study may also constrain molecular inference. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into FMD occurrence in high-risk areas and contributes to the understanding of disease dynamics in buffaloes. Future research may address these constraints by employing larger or stratified sampling frameworks and exploring broader geographic coverage to enhance representativeness.

5 Conclusion

Buffaloes are important to Bangladesh's livestock industry, although FMD poses a significant challenge due to extensive rearing practices. Calves under one year are particularly susceptible, with risk increased by sharing grazing land with cattle, inadequate deworming practices, insufficient isolation facilities, and exposure to bushy environment. Because of the virus's transboundary nature and its genetic evolution, ongoing surveillance and investigation of regional transmission dynamics are essential for managing future outbreaks.

Strengths of the study

This study provides one of the most comprehensive assessments of FMD in buffaloes in Bangladesh by combining clinical outbreak investigation, field epidemiology, RT-PCR-based serotype identification, and VP1 gene phylogenetics. The integration of molecular and epidemiological evidence strengthens the interpretation of disease risk patterns and highlights the role of buffalo-specific management practices in FMD transmission. A key strength of this work is the linkage between field-level outbreak epidemiology and VP1 gene characterization, which allowed us to relate risk factors to circulating viral lineages despite the limited number of sequenced samples.
