==============================
This study aims to investigate the counteracting effects of cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) addition on protein oxidation and its induced human gut microbiota disturbance in baking dough. Our results indicated that C3G turned dough purple, transformed bound water into weakly bound water and free water, reduced protein aggregation, and altered α-helix and β-turn to random coil. Additionally, C3G varied protein oxidation product contents: it decreased carbonyls, advanced glycation end products and Schiff bases, and concurrently elevated free sulfhydryl and free amino groups. Specific amino acid contents increased, whereas amino acid oxidation product contents decreased. Moreover, C3G enhanced hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, protein digestibility, and free radical scavenging capacity. Furthermore, under in vitro anaerobic fermentation with human gut microbiota, the C3G-supplemented dough proteins increased beneficial and decreased harmful bacterial abundance. Therefore, C3G can ameliorate baking-induced dough protein oxidation, and also alleviate human gut microbiota disturbance induced by the oxidized proteins in vitro.

1 Introduction

Flour products serve as a crucial dietary protein source (protein content ranging from 8% to 15%) for populations in many regions worldwide (China, Germany, Italy, etc.) (Day et al., 2022). Among various methods for processing flour products, baking is a prevalent technique primarily used to produce products such as bread and biscuits (Zhang, 2020). The color changes of dough during baking mainly result from the Maillard reaction and caramelization, two processes involving protein-sugar cross-linking and gluten macropolymer structural modification (Celik et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2022; Ogilvie et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Baking causes physicochemical changes that decrease protein digestibility and result in the accumulation of protein and amino acid oxidation products, potentially elevating risks of chronic diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases (Kehm et al., 2021; Ogilvie et al., 2021). Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and carbonyls increase in butter cookies and dried tofu after baking (Chen et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2022). The amino acid content of bread decreases during baking (Montevecchi et al., 2022), suggesting the occurrence of amino acid oxidation and protein cross-linking. Our recent studies demonstrate that the contents of protein oxidation products (carbonyls, AGEs) and amino acid oxidation products (dityrosine, N′-formylkynurenine, kynurenine) in dough increase with prolonged baking time (Liu et al., 2023). Changes in food protein structure and composition are important factors influencing changes in gut microbiota (Duncan et al., 2021). Wu et al. (2025) revealed that intake of oxidized proteins from refrigerated rice and steamed bread decreases gut probiotic abundance, and increases the abundance of harmful bacteria. Supplementation with soybean meal containing oxidized protein increases the abundance of pro-inflammatory bacteria (Turicibacter), indicating increased intestinal disorder risks in rats (Wang et al., 2024). Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore green and healthy approaches to mitigate protein oxidation in baking dough, and thereby reduce the associated adverse effects on gut microbiota.

The cortical layers of whole grains contain active phytochemicals with free radical-scavenging and antioxidant properties, making them vital for healthy diets (Guo et al., 2022). Previous studies extensively investigated the role of bran (Wang et al., 2025c), which is rich in dietary fiber, anthocyanins, tocopherols, carotenoids, and other bioactive compounds (Gamel et al., 2023; Martín-Diana et al., 2021; Saini et al., 2023). The incorporation of bran into dough enhances its antioxidant capacity and increases the free radical scavenging rate (Onipe et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). However, bran incorporation negatively affects taste and shelf life, limiting its practical use (Wang et al., 2026). Thus, there is a need for a natural antioxidant substitute for bran. Anthocyanins, a class of natural plant compounds, have garnered significant attention for their outstanding antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-aging properties (Dhalaria et al., 2020). Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G), an anthocyanin monomer with strong antioxidant capacity, has been widely studied and has been identified in various foods, such as colored wheat (e.g., black and purple wheat), black beans, and blueberries (Deepa et al., 2023). Anthocyanin-protein binding modifies protein secondary structure, improving the stability and antioxidant capacity of the complex, and conferring higher biological activity, bioaccessibility and bioavailability than free anthocyanins alone (Li et al., 2020; Zang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). C3G improves β-lactoglobulin digestibility by modulating hydrolysis and reducing agglomeration (Khalifa et al., 2022). Additionally, C3G specifically scavenges highly reactive carbonyl compounds from carbohydrate metabolism, blocking their crosslinking with protein side-chain amino acids to inhibit AGE formation (Zhang et al., 2021). Through in vitro anaerobic fermentation, C3G modulates gut microbiota by reducing Escherichia-Shigella and increasing Fusobacterium (Wang et al., 2024). C3G supplementation can also increase Lactobacillus and Alistipes in the intestines of ovalbumin-induced allergic mice (Li et al., 2022). However, whether C3G addition can mitigate protein oxidation in baking dough and consequently alleviate gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by the oxidized proteins remains unclear, requiring further investigation.

Therefore, we hypothesize that C3G addition can counteract protein oxidation in baking dough and consequently alleviate human gut microbiota disturbance induced by the oxidized proteins. To test this hypothesis, the dough color, moisture migration, protein structure, protein oxidation products, non-covalent interactions, surface hydrophobicity, digestibility, and free-radical-scavenging capacity were assessed to evaluate the degree of protein oxidation. In addition, an in vitro anaerobic fermentation model combined with 16S rDNA gene sequencing was used to evaluate changes in gut microbiota composition. This study provides a scientific basis for the addition of C3G in flour-based food processing from the perspective of mitigating protein oxidation and the associated gut microbial health risks.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Materials

The wheat flour was acquired from Yihai Kerry Golden Dragonfish (Zhengzhou, China). Bran was obtained through inhouse milling using a mill (Exakt 120SPLUS, Suzhou Weinai Intelligent Technology, Suzhou, China). C3G (98% purity) was sourced from Xi′an Ruilin Biotechnology (Xi′an, China). 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), pepsin and trypsin were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). KBr, yeast extract powder, rhodamine B, HCl, trichloroacetic acid, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), guanidine hydrochloride, sodium tetraborate, Tween 20, Tween 80, and phenol were purchased from Komeo Chemical Reagent (Tianjin, China). NaCl and ethanol were purchased from Tianli Chemical Reagent (Tianjin, China). 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) and o-phthalaldehyde were purchased from McLean Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). Glycine and L-serine were purchased from Beijing Soleberg Technology (Beijing, China). Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris), dithiothreitol, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were acquired from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The Bradford protein assay kit was purchased from Nanjing Institute of Biological Engineering (Nanjing, China). The HiPure Soil DNA Kit was purchased from Maigen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All reagents used were of analytical grade unless otherwise specified.

2.2 Samples preparation

Equal masses of dough (15 g) were prepared for the control and baking groups using a flour-to-water ratio of 2:1 (w/v). For the 15% bran group (positive control), flour and bran were accurately weighed at a ratio of 8.5:1.5 (w/w), thoroughly blended, and kneaded with water (2:1, w/v). For the 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% C3G groups, flour was precisely weighed, blended with C3G at the respective concentrations (w/w), and kneaded with water (2:1, w/v). All samples except the control group were baked in an oven at 200 °C for 20 min, and then freeze-dried, ground, sieved, and subjected to subsequent analyses. The above procedure was repeated three times to ensure the reliability of the results.

2.3 Determination of the characteristic indicators of dough

2.3.1 Determination of color

The dough color was determined using a colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) (Atudorei et al., 2021). The color changes of the samples were recorded and expressed as lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) values.

2.3.2 Determination of moisture migration

The method of Yang et al., 2026 served as the basis for the experiment, with minor modifications. Samples were baked and analyzed for moisture migration using Low-Field Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (LF-NMR, MicriMR-CL-I, Shanghai Niumag Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Transverse relaxation times (T2) and the ratio of peak integral areas (A2) were measured using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill sequence.

2.3.3 Determination of non-covalent interaction forces

Non-covalent interactions were determined according to the method reported by Wang et al., 2017, incorporating suitable modifications. Dough samples (250 mg) were separately mixed with 5 mL four different reagents (0.05 M NaCl, 0.6 M NaCl, 0.6 M NaCl + 1.5 M urea, 0.6 M NaCl + 8 M urea). The mixtures were incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 2 h to extract dough proteins. The protein content was measured using Bradford protein assay kits.

2.3.4 Determination of surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity was determined using the method reported by Hao et al., 2022 with slight modifications. Each 100 mg dough sample was mixed with 2 mL PBS and 20 μL 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (8 mmol/L), with PBS as control. After 15 min of incubation in the dark, the fluorescence intensity was measured at an excitation wavelength (Ex) of 390 nm and an emission wavelength (Em) of 470 nm.

2.3.5 Determination of protein digestibility

Protein digestibility was assessed according to a modified version of the method reported by Khalesi et al. (2021). The digestibility was calculated using the following formula: Digestibility (%) = (m0 – m1)/m0 × 100. Where: m0 is pre-digestion protein content (g) of the dough sample, and m1 is the post-digestion protein content (g) of the dough sample.

2.3.6 Determination of free radical scavenging ability

The ABTS+ and DPPH radical scavenging activities of the samples were determined according to a slightly modified method based on Rizzello et al., 2017. For ABTS+, 0.6 mL supernatant (10 mg/mL, obtained from 2.3.5) was mixed with 2.4 mL ABTS+ solution, shaken, incubated in darkness for 6 min, and absorbance measured at 734 nm. For DPPH, 2 mL supernatant (10 mg/mL, obtained from 2.3.5) was mixed with 2 mL DPPH solution, incubated in darkness for 30 min, and absorbance measured at 517 nm. The scavenging rates were calculated using the following equations: ABTS+ scavenging rate (%) = (A₀ – A)/A₀ × 100, where A₀ is absorbance of ABTS+ with ethanol and A with sample; and DPPH scavenging rate (%) = (1 – (Aᵢ – Aⱼ)/Ac) × 100, where Aᵢ is absorbance of sample-DPPH mixture, Aⱼ of sample-ethanol mixture, and Ac of ethanol-DPPH mixture.

2.4 Determination of dough protein structure

2.4.1 Determination of dough protein secondary structure

The secondary structure of the dough proteins was characterized via Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, APHA, Bruker, Germany), following the method reported by Fevzioglu et al., 2020. Flour and KBr (1:100) were ground in an agate mortar, pressed lightly, and scanned using OPUS 7.5 across 4000-400 cm-1 at 32-1 cm-1 resolution with 16 scans.

2.4.2 Determination of dough protein network structure

The protein network structure of samples was analyzed based on the method of Han et al. (2019), with minor adjustments. Frozen dough sliced (15 μm), and was stained with 0.0001% Rhodamine B in the dark for 20 min, then rinsed and visualized under Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM, FV300, Olympus, Japan) and AngioTool.

2.5 Determination of protein oxidation products

2.5.1 Determination of carbonyls content

The carbonyl content was determined according to the method reported by Zhang et al., 2020, with appropriate modifications. Briefly, 1.5 mL 10 mg/mL sample solution was mixed with 5 mL 10 mM DNPH, incubated in darkness for 1 h (control: 5 mL 2 M HCl), followed by the addition of 0.5 mL 20% trichloroacetic acid. After centrifugation (10,000g, 15 min, 4 °C), the precipitate was washed three times with C2H5OH-C4H8O2 (1:1), dissolved in 1.5 mL guanidine hydrochloride, reacted in darkness for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 367 nm. Carbonyl content (nmol/mg Pro) = (A367 × 109)/(Mr × c), where A367 is absorbance at 367 nm, Mr is the extinction coefficient (22000 M-1 cm-1), and c is the protein concentration (determined using a Bradford protein assay kit).

2.5.2 Determination of free sulfhydryl content

The free sulfhydryl (SH) group was determined using a modified method based on that described by Zhou et al. (2019). Each 150 mg samples were mixed with 5 mL buffer solution (10 mM EDTA, 0.2 M glycine and 20 mM Tris) and 50 μL 4 mM DTNB. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm. SH (μmol/g Pro) = (73.53 × A412 × D) / c. Where: A412 is the absorbance of free SH at 412 nm, D is the dilution factor, and c denotes the protein concentration (determined using a Bradford protein assay kit).

2.5.3 Determination of free amino group content

The assay was performed following the method of Qian et al. (2024) with minor modifications. Each 200μL samples (1 mg/mL) were mixed with buffer solution (sodium tetraborate, SDS, o-phthalaldehyde), diluted with water, and reacted with dithiothreitol in darkness for 3 min. The absorbance at 340 nm was measured, and the free amino acid content was calculated using an L-serine standard curve.

2.5.4 Determination of AGEs and protein oxidation products content

The AGEs content was determined with reference to the method of Wang et al. (2023) and Walayat et al. (Nawaz et al., 2021). A 200 mg sample was mixed with 5 mL buffer (0.05% Tween 20, 1% SDS, 5% mercaptoethanol, pH 7.4, 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl), incubated at 25 °C for 12 h, and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 20 min. Fluorescence intensities of dityrosine, AGEs, kynurenine, and N′-formylkynurenine were measured at Ex/Em wavelengths of 330/415, 325/440, 365/480, and 325/434 nm.

2.5.5 Determination of Schiff base content

The Schiff base content was determined using the method of Li et al. (2022), with minor adjustments. Each 250 mg dough sample was mixed with 5 mL PBS (pH 7.0), centrifuged at 8000g for 5 min. The supernatant was analyzed with Ex 360 nm and slit at 5 nm, and Em scanned from 380 to 600 nm.

2.6 Determination of amino acid composition

2.6.1 Amino acid composition analysis

The amino acid content was analyzed using a method adapted from Zhou et al. (2015). A 100 mg sample was mixed with 10 mL 6 M HCl, purged with nitrogen for 2 min, sealed, hydrolyzed at 110 °C for 22 h, cooled, filtered. The filtrate was rotary-evaporated and then 1 mL diluent was added. The final filtrate was used for amino acid analysis using an automatic amino acid analyzer (SykamS433D, Sykams, Germany).

2.6.2 Determination of tryptophan content

The tryptophan content was analyzed using a method adapted from Ritota et al. (2020). Each 250 mg samples was subsequently dissolved in 5 mL PBS (pH 7.0). Following a water bath at 37 °C for 3 h, the solution was centrifuged at 8000g for 5 min. The fluorescence spectra were scanned with Ex of 283 nm, Em range of 250-400 nm, and an emission slit width of 5 nm, at a scanning speed of 1000 nm/min.

2.7 In vitro fermentation assays

2.7.1 Preparation of dough protein and basal medium

Dough proteins were extracted following Wang et al. (2023). The culture medium was prepared following Amorim et al., 2020. The mother liquor of the medium was made by mixing 2 g yeast extract powder, 0.01 g CaCl2·6H2O, 2 g NaHCO3, 0.1 g NaCl, 0.04 g K2HPO4, 0.05 g C34H32ClFeN4O4, 0.04 g K3PO4, 0.5 g L-cysteine hydrochloride, 0.01 g MgSO4·7H2O, 0.5 g bile salts, 2 mL Tween 80, and 10 μL vitamin K1 in 800 mL ultrapure water, adjusting pH to 7.5 and sterilizing. Then, 8 mL medium were boiled, cooled, and flushed with nitrogen for 2 min.

2.7.2 Preparation of bacterial suspensions

Volunteer recruitment: Six volunteers (3 males, 3 females, 18-25) from Henan University of Technology were enrolled after signing the informed consent form, undergoing physical exams, and completing lifestyle and diet questionnaires. All had comparable backgrounds, no genetic disorders, and no antibiotic use in the past 3 months. This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Henan University of Technology (HAUTETHI-20230411).

Formulation of bacterial solution: Bacterial solutions were prepared using a slightly modified method from Gao et al. (2024). Fresh feces (3 g) were mixed with PBS (27 mL, pH 7.0), filtered and centrifuged at 5000g for 20 min to obtain the supernatant (bacterial suspension), which was used to prepare the microbial culture medium.

2.7.3 Cultivation of gut microorganisms

Gut microorganism culture was performed using a slightly modified method from Li et al. (2015). 1 mL bacterial suspension and 1 mL protein solutions were added to fermentation tubes with 8 mL medium (8:1:1), anaerobically incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, and centrifuged (5000g, 20 min). The broth was used for DNA analysis; the precipitate was stored at -80 °C.

2.7.4 DNA extraction from intestinal microorganisms and gene sequencing

Total DNA from fermentation broth was extracted using HiPure Soil DNA Kit, quantified with Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit, and standardized for sequencing. The 16S rDNA V3-V4 regions were PCR-amplified, and products were analyzed via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis for 600 bp fragments (Gai et al., 2023). OTU sequences were taxonomically classified using the Bayesian algorithm of the RDP classifier. Bioinformatic analyses (OTU Venn, dilution/rank curves, α-diversity and β-diversity) were performed with R (v3.1.1) and QIIME (v1.9.1); the heat maps were generated using Heml 1.0.3.3.

2.8 Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 23 and shown as mean ± SEM. Significance was assessed via one-way ANOVA; post-hoc tests were Tukey′s test (equal variances) or Tamhane′s T2 (unequal variances). Significant differences were marked by distinct lowercase letters (p < 0.05).

3 Results and discussion

3.1 Effects of C3G addition on the color and moisture migration of baking dough

Fig. 1A illustrates the sample processing steps and grouping. The baking group′s dough turned brown, with bran deepening the color. The color of the C3G-supplemented dough intensified with increasing C3G dosage. Compared to the control group, the baking group showed a significant decrease in L*, and significant increases in a* and b* (Fig. 1B, p < 0.05), attributed to the glycation reaction between proteins and carbohydrates in the dough matrix (Liu et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2024). Compared with the baking group, the bran group showed a further significant decrease in L* and increases in a* and b* (p < 0.05), likely due to the inherent brown pigments in bran and the enhanced Maillard reaction caused by bran-derived reducing sugars and free amino acids during baking (Qi et al., 2025). Varying C3G concentrations resulted in differential decreases in L* and b* and increases in a*, with the 0.5% C3G group showing the most significant color changes (p < 0.05), which can be explained by the natural color of C3G. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported that adding anthocyanin-rich purple rice flour to bread and biscuits reduces L* and b* (Hu et al., 2025; Klunklin et al., 2018).Fig. 1 Impact of C3G addition on baking dough color and moisture migration. (A) Experimental design schematic; (B) Color metrics; (C) T2 spectral profiles; (D)-(F) T2 and A2 values for bound, weakly bound, and free water.

Fig. 1

To examine the effects of C3G addition on moisture migration during baking, LF-NMR was selected to measure the T2 and A2 of dough samples. Moisture migration affects dough color during baking via Maillard reactant concentrations and relates to protein structure (Dufour et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2022; Mior Zakuan Azmi et al., 2019). The T2 curve shows peaks reflecting water molecule mobility: T21 (0.1-10 ms), T22 (10-100 ms), and T23 (over 100 ms) indicate bound water, weakly bound water, and free water (Ge et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2019), respectively. The peak area ratios of bound water, weakly bound water, and free water are represented by A21, A22, and A23, respectively, and these indices signify the proportions of water types (Li et al., 2024). Compared to the control group, the baking group exhibited significantly decreased T22, T23, A22, and A23, and significantly increased A21 (Fig. 1C–F, p < 0.05). Compared to the baking group, the bran group failed to effectively mitigate baking-induced moisture migration, probably due to bran′s high absorption capacity. Moreover, compared with the baking group, adding 0.5% C3G significantly reduced T21, T22, T23, and A21, while significantly increasing A22 and A23 (p < 0.05), likely because C3G disrupts the gluten network. Previous studies support our findings, showing that purple sweet potato flour and tea polyphenols in dough can decrease A21 and T22 while increasing A23 (Liu et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2022). The results indicate that C3G can influence moisture migration in baking dough, implying that C3G may influence the protein structure.

3.2 Effects of C3G addition on the protein structure of baking dough

3.2.1 Effects of C3G addition on the secondary structure of baking dough proteins

To investigate the impacts of C3G addition on the protein secondary structure of baking dough, FTIR spectroscopy was employed. α-helices, β-sheets, β-turns, and random coil are common indicators of protein secondary structure, reflecting alterations in protein structure and function (Bu et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2023). Compared to the control group, the baking group showed significant rises in β-sheet and β-turn, and declines in α-helix and random coil (Fig. 2C, p < 0.05), which was due to high-temperature baking disrupting the hydrogen bonds in α-helices and thereby converting them into β-sheets (Yao et al., 2024). Notably, compared with the baking group, the 0.3% and 0.5% C3G groups showed significantly increased random coil, and significantly decreased α-helices, β-sheets, and β-turns (p < 0.05). The combination of anthocyanins from black soybean hulls and black rice with soy protein isolate (SPI) led to a significant reduction in the α-helix and β-sheet contents (Chen et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018), a finding consistent with our results. No significant differences in the proportions of α-helices, β-turns, and β-sheets were observed in the bran group as compared to the baking group. Therefore, C3G can alleviate the baking-induced alterations in dough protein secondary structure.Fig. 2 Effects of C3G addition on secondary structure of baking dough proteins. 3.2.2 Effects of C3G addition on the network structure of gluten proteins in baking dough

To further visually investigate protein microstructural changes, CLSM was employed. Protein oxidation in food typically manifests as macromolecular aggregation and cross-linking, leading to structural changes (Feng et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2026). As shown in Fig. 3, after baking, the gluten proteins in the baking group exhibited obvious macromolecular aggregation compared to the control group, with an irregular distribution of the network structure and poor connectivity. Compared with the baking group, the 0.3% C3G group showed enhanced gluten network continuity and compactness, reducing fractures and pores. These findings concur with prior studies: tannic acid formed a dense gluten network via hydrogen bonding with gluten proteins (Nam et al., 2025), while appropriate purple cabbage anthocyanin extract increased the protein chain height and promoted network formation (Xie et al., 2024). Compared with the baking group, the addition of 0.1% C3G attenuated gluten network aggregation, though the effect was less pronounced, likely due to the low concentration of C3G. However, adding 0.5% C3G reduced gluten network continuity and compactness and increased fractures and pores. This can be attributed to the competitive binding of excessive C3G molecules to hydrogen-bonding sites on gluten proteins. The occupation of these sites sterically hinders the close approach and proper alignment of adjacent protein chains, thereby preventing the formation of intermolecular disulfide bonds and hydrogen bonds that are essential for gluten cross-linking (Guo et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024). Girard et al. (2018) demonstrated that proanthocyanidins inhibit the formation of disulfide cross-links, and this inhibitory effect weakens the gluten network, which is consistent with the phenomenon observed in our study. Bran addition also alleviated gluten aggregation, with a more pronounced effect than that of C3G. This was because the interaction of dietary fiber and polysaccharides in bran with gluten proteins formed a stable network that reduced gluten protein aggregation; this mechanism may be more effective than the chemical modification of gluten protein conformation by C3G (Guo et al., 2024; Han et al., 2019). Our results indicate that adding C3G at an appropriate concentration inhibits baking-induced protein aggregation, reducing protein oxidation.Fig. 3 Effects of C3G addition on gluten protein network structure in baking dough. CLSM (50 μm scale bar) and AngioTool images (blue: gluten junctions; red: gluten skeleton).

3.3 Effects of C3G addition on the protein oxidation in baking dough

3.3.1 Effects of C3G addition on the protein oxidation products in baking dough

To evaluate the extent to which C3G mitigates dough protein oxidation during baking, typical markers of protein oxidation were determined. Carbonyls, formed by reactive oxygen species (ROS) acting on amino groups, are early markers of oxidative amino acid modifications in protein oxidation (Manoharan et al., 2024). AGEs are harmful compounds from Maillard reactions of proteins and reducing sugars, indicating protein cross-linking and aggregation (Haque et al., 2020). Schiff bases result from the condensation of aldehyde groups from oxidation and proteins′ free amino groups, indicating the protein oxidation level (Xia et al., 2021). Free amino groups are easily attacked by ROS and oxidize to form carbonyl derivatives, reducing amino group content and reflecting the extent of protein oxidation (Kehm et al., 2021). Free SH groups are prone to free radical attack, fostering disulfide bond formation under oxidative stress and reflecting the extent of protein oxidative damage (Feng et al., 2022). As depicted in Fig. 4A–F, the levels of carbonyls, AGEs, and Schiff bases in the baking group demonstrated a significant increase compared to those in the control group, while the contents of free amino groups and free SH groups showed a significant decrease (p < 0.05). Compared with those in the baking group, the contents of carbonyls, AGEs, and Schiff bases in groups with varying concentrations of C3G decreased by 16.25%-46.78%, 13.64%-57.79%, and 15.28%-57.56%, respectively, and the contents of free SH and free amino groups increased by 8.97%-66.03% and 36.80%-77.68%, respectively. C3G from mulberries can reduce the formation of AGEs by both scavenging glyoxal and blocking lysyl residues in β-lactoglobulin (Khalifa et al., 2020), and caffeic acid can effectively decrease the content of Schiff bases in sausages (Cheng et al., 2021), both findings that are consistent with our results. Bran addition had similar effects, but the Schiff base content in the bran group was significantly higher than that in other groups (p < 0.05). This may be attributed to the high fat content in bran: the aldehydes generated from fat oxidation react with protein amino groups to form Schiff bases during baking (Zhou et al., 2020). Collectively, these results indicate that C3G can effectively inhibit the formation of protein oxidation products in dough and exhibits superior efficacy compared to bran addition in alleviating protein oxidation.Fig. 4 Effects of C3G addition on the protein oxidation products in baking dough. 3.3.2 Effects of C3G addition on amino acid composition and amino acid oxidation products in baking dough

To investigate the impacts of C3G addition on amino acid oxidation in baking dough, the amino acid compositions and the contents of representative amino acid oxidation products were examined. Food protein oxidation is often accompanied by amino acid oxidation, cross-linking, and the accumulation of amino acid oxidation products (Hellwig, 2020; Sulaiman et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 5A, the baking group exhibited significantly reduced levels of aspartic acid, glutamic acid, glycine, cysteine, valine, isoleucine, leucine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, histidine, lysine, and arginine compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Compared with the baking group, the contents of aspartic acid, glutamic acid, cysteine, valine, isoleucine, leucine, phenylalanine, histidine, lysine, and arginine significantly increased with increasing C3G concentration (p < 0.05), indicating that C3G inhibited amino acid oxidation. Although bran addition significantly increased the contents of aspartic acid, threonine, glutamic acid, alanine, valine, methionine, isoleucine, leucine, phenylalanine, and lysine compared with the baking group (p < 0.05), this effect was not as significant as that of C3G addition. In addition, we analyzed the content of tryptophan, which is an essential amino acid that is highly susceptible to oxidation (Zhu et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 5C, the tryptophan fluorescence intensity in the baking group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.05), which was consistent with the trends observed for the other amino acids mentioned above. The fluorescence intensity of tryptophan significantly decreased in the 0.3% and 0.5% C3G groups compared to the baking group (p < 0.05), which may be attributed to the strong quenching effect of C3G on amino acid fluorescence through a combination of static and dynamic quenching mechanisms (Zhang et al., 2024). Sui et al. (2018) noted similar fluorescence quenching in anthocyanin-soy protein interactions, suggesting that anthocyanin oxidation yields a quinone ring carbon atom that nucleophilically reacts with protein free amino nitrogen to form a C-N bond, reducing chromophore activity. A similar effect was observed after adding bran. These results demonstrate that C3G effectively mitigated amino acid oxidation induced by baking.Fig. 5 Effects of C3G addition on amino acid composition and oxidation products in baking dough. To further elucidate the impacts of C3G addition on amino acid oxidation in dough induced by baking, content analysis was conducted on amino acid oxidation products. Dityrosine (a tyrosine oxidation product), as well as kynurenine and N′-formylkynurenine (intermediates in tryptophan breakdown), are commonly used to assess the degree of amino acid oxidation (Li et al., 2023; Nettis et al., 2023). As depicted in Fig. 5D–F, the baking group exhibited significantly elevated levels of dityrosine, kynurenine, and N′-formylkynurenine compared to the control group (p < 0.05). With the addition of different concentrations of C3G, compared with the baking group, dityrosine, N′-formylkynurenine, and kynurenine contents decreased by 11.98%-61.75%, 12.57%-58.08%, and 26.46%-66.14%, with the reduction becoming more pronounced with increasing C3G concentration, which was primarily due to C3G′s potent antioxidant activity. Studies indicate that Toona sinensis seed polyphenols reduced dityrosine in porcine myofibrillar protein (Wang et al., 2025), and quercetin inhibited dityrosine, N′-formylkynurenine, and kynurenine formation in steamed bread (Lin et al., 2018), supporting our conclusion. Bran addition significantly reduced the contents of amino acid oxidation products compared to those in the baking group (p < 0.05), but its effect was less effectively than C3G. Collectively, these results further demonstrate that the addition of C3G effectively mitigates baking-induced amino acid oxidation, showing superior efficacy compared to bran.

3.4 Effects of C3G addition on certain characteristic index in baking dough

3.4.1 Effects of C3G addition on non-covalent interactions and surface hydrophobicity in baking dough

To further elucidate the effects of C3G addition on the protein functionality of dough during baking, non-covalent interactions and surface hydrophobicity of dough proteins were determined. Non-covalent interactions are vital for stabilizing protein conformation and ensuring proper protein function (Kekez et al., 2021). Baking increased ionic bonds and significantly decreased hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions compared to the control group (Fig. 6A, p < 0.05). Compared to the baking group, all C3G groups significantly enhanced hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions (p < 0.05). This was attributed to C3G forming complexes with carbonyls and binding to hydrophobic amino acids, thereby preventing hydrogen bond loss during baking and strengthening non-covalent interactions (Qian et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023). Compared to the baking group, the bran group significantly decreased hydrogen bonds and significantly increased hydrophobic interactions (p < 0.05). This was likely due to the steric hindrance of bran fibers, which block protein aggregation and gluten network formation, stretch the protein network, and expose hydrophobic groups (Zhang et al., 2024). Fig. 6B shows that baking significantly raised protein surface hydrophobicity compared to the control group (p < 0.05). C3G addition significantly reduced protein surface hydrophobicity compared with the baking group (p < 0.05). The binding of C3G to lentil protein isolate and β-lactoglobulin reduced the surface hydrophobicity of the proteins (Qian et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021), supporting our findings. The effects of bran supplementation were less pronounced compared to those of C3G. These results indicate that C3G can mitigate changes in non-covalent interactions and surface hydrophobicity of proteins caused by baking.Fig. 6 Effects of C3G addition on non-covalent interactions (A), surface hydrophobicity (B), in vitro protein digestibility (C), and ABTS+ (D) and DPPH (E) free radical scavenging capacities in baking dough.

3.4.2 Effects of C3G addition on protein digestibility in baking dough

Protein oxidation is typically characterized by decreased protein digestibility due to protein aggregation and cross-linking (Ding et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022). As illustrated in Fig. 6C, compared to the control group, the baking group′s protein digestibility was significantly reduced (p < 0.05). Compared to the baking group, protein digestibility increased with increasing C3G concentration. This may be attributed to C3G inhibiting protein aggregation and cross-linking, thereby increasing the accessibility of sensitive peptide bonds and facilitating protein digestion by digestive enzymes (Cao et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2019). Black rice anthocyanins complexed with SPI exhibited improved digestibility compared to the proteins alone (Jiang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018), a finding consistent with our results. However, protein digestibility was significantly decreased by bran addition as compared to the baking group, likely because bran dietary fiber inhibits the interaction between proteolytic enzymes and proteins (Saini et al., 2023). These results indicate that C3G can enhance protein digestibility, further supporting its role in mitigating protein oxidation.

3.4.3 Effects of C3G addition on free radical scavenging capacity in baking dough

To elucidate the reasons for C3G′s inhibition of protein oxidation, the radical scavenging capacity of baking dough was examined. Free radical attack is the primary cause of protein oxidation (Andrés et al., 2024). The radical scavenging capacity reflects antioxidant potential and contributes to elucidating the extent of dough protein oxidation. As illustrated in Fig. 6D, E, the baking group′s ABTS+ and DPPH scavenging abilities significantly decreased in comparison to the control group (p < 0.05). Compared with the baking group, the ABTS+ and DPPH scavenging abilities increased with increasing C3G concentration. Bran addition exhibited a similar trend, although to a lesser extent than C3G. This might be attributed to the strong antioxidant capacity of C3G, which, unlike the polyphenols in wheat bran that predominantly exist in bound forms with limited bioavailability, is readily available to exert antioxidant effects (Wang et al., 2024). Additionally, owing to its polyphenolic hydroxyl groups and stable resonance structures, C3G can act as an efficient electron or hydrogen atom donor, directly neutralizing a variety of ROS and free radicals, thereby reducing protein oxidation (Rao et al., 2025). Under heat treatment conditions, the complexes formed by whey protein isolate and bovine serum protein with blueberry anthocyanins can enhance the antioxidant activity (Zang et al., 2021), which supports our conclusions. These results indicate that C3G addition can enhance free radical scavenging capacities, which may represent one of the key mechanisms underlying its inhibitory effect on protein oxidation in baking dough.

3.5 Effects of C3G addition on gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by oxidized dough proteins under in vitro anaerobic fermentation conditions

Dietary proteins, acting as nitrogen sources for gut microbiota, are vital for its growth and reproduction. In the intestinal environment, oxidized dietary proteins can interact with gut microbiota, potentially altering its composition and consequently impacting intestinal and overall body health (Zhang, 2022). To clarify the influences of C3G addition on human gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by baking dough proteins, an in vitro anaerobic fermentation was conducted using dough proteins as the only nitrogen source for human intestinal bacteria. The gut microbiota composition in the fermentation broth was then examined through 16S rDNA gene sequencing.

3.5.1 Effects of C3G addition on OTUs, α-Diversity, and β-Diversity

As illustrated in Fig. 7A, a total of 523 OTUs were identified across all samples (419 OTUs for the control group, 415 OTUs for the baking group, 416 OTUs for the 15% bran group, 412 OTUs for the 0.1% C3G group, 416 OTUs for the 0.3% C3G group, and 410 OTUs for the 0.5% C3G group). As shown in Fig. 7B, C, the sequence count rose linearly to 20,000 and plateaued beyond 80,000, suggesting high sample representativeness and adequate sampling depth. The rank abundance curve revealed a relatively consistent pattern of species abundance and evenness across all groups. α-diversity measures species diversity and richness in a community (Cai et al., 2026). Abundance-based coverage estimator (ACE) and Chao1 focus on rare species for richness estimation; the Simpson index focuses on abundant species; and the Shannon index reflects both richness and community evenness (Fang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). As illustrated in Fig. 7D-G, the baking group had significantly lower Shannon and Simpson indices than the control group (p < 0.05). Compared with the baking group, the addition of C3G led to an increasing trend in both the Shannon index and the Simpson index, but no significant difference was observed (p > 0.05). Bran addition exhibited similar effects, but these effects were less pronounced compared to those of C3G. β-diversity is employed to assess differences in microbial communities among different groups (Zhang et al., 2023). 3.5.2 Effects of C3G addition on the relative abundance of microbiota at phylum level

Fig. 8A illustrates that the dominant microbial communities in each group at the phylum level include Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Fusobacteriota, and Desulfobacterota. The baking group showed a significantly increased abundance of Proteobacteria in comparison to the control group, with significant decreases in the abundance of Bacteroidota, Fusobacteriota, Desulfobacterota, and Euryarchaeota (Fig. 8C, D, p < 0.05). Compared to the baking group, the groups with different concentrations of C3G showed varying reductions in the abundance of Proteobacteria and varying increases in the abundances of Fusobacteriota, Desulfobacterota, and Euryarchaeota. 3.5.3 Effects of C3G addition on the relative abundance of microbiota at genus level

Additionally, the microbial composition in the fermentation broth was further analyzed at the genus level. As shown in Fig. 9A, the heatmap displays 47 bacterial genera with relative abundances exceeding 0.1%. 13 bacterial genera with significant differences were selected for further investigation (p < 0.05). Fusobacterium, Bilophila, and Sutterella are recognized as pro-inflammatory bacteria that induce intestinal inflammation by disrupting intestinal barrier function and leading to intestinal microecological imbalance (Chu et al., 2025, Jiang et al., 2023, Li et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2024). Bacteroides, Lachnoclostridium, Phascolarctobacterium, Hungatella, and Alistipes are recognized as anti-inflammatory bacteria that inhibit the onset and progression of intestinal inflammation (Cui et al., 2024, Dai et al., 2022, Wang, Wang and Li, 2025b, Xing et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2026). As illustrated in Fig. 9B–D, the relative abundances of Bacteroides, Lachnoclostridium, Phascolarctobacterium, Hungatella, and Alistipes was significantly decreased in the baking group in comparison to the control group, and the relative abundance of Fusobacterium, Bilophila, and Sutterella were significantly increased (p < 0.05). In agreement with our current results, Perez-Burillo et al. (2018) revealed through in vitro fermentation assays that baked bread elevated the abundance of pro-inflammatory bacteria relative to unbaked bread. Following supplementation with varying C3G concentrations, the relative abundances of Lachnoclostridium and Phascolarctobacterium increased significantly, while those of Fusobacterium, Bilophila, and Sutterella decreased significantly compared to the baking group (p < 0.05), suggesting C3G promotes beneficial bacteria and reduces potential pathogens by inhibiting protein oxidation. These microbial changes can be attributed to the following reasons: due to the addition of C3G, the protein structure and oxidative damage in proteins from C3G-supplemented baked dough were repaired to varying degrees, making them more susceptible to digestion by gastrointestinal enzymes. Consequently, fewer undigested proteins entered the colon for fermentation, which altered the composition and proportion of nitrogenous substrates available to the gut microbiota, ultimately leading to the aforementioned changes in microbial community structure. Supporting our viewpoint is a previous study demonstrating that fermentation improved the structure and composition of soybean proteins, and that feeding the soybean proteins to rats increased the abundance of beneficial bacteria in the gut, thereby contributing to the maintenance of intestinal health (Miao et al., 2024). Bran addition also conferred a similar mitigating effect, but it was not as effective as C3G. 4 Conclusion

The results indicate that C3G supplementation reversed baking-induced changes in dough protein structure and function, and inhibited the accumulation of protein and amino acid oxidation products. Proteins from C3G-supplemented baking dough exhibited an increase in beneficial bacterial abundance and a decrease in harmful bacterial abundance under in vitro anaerobic fermentation conditions. Bran addition was less effective than C3G addition, especially at 0.3% and 0.5% concentrations. C3G outperformed bran in counteracting effects on protein oxidation and its induced human gut microbiota disturbance in baking dough, making it a superior alternative to bran for inhibiting protein oxidation during baking. While this study highlights C3G′s potential in bakery products to inhibit protein oxidation and provides scientific backing for functional food development, it is limited by an in vitro model and single-condition testing of pure C3G, leaving the effects of natural extracts unexplored. Future research should examine C3G′s stability and bioavailability across baking processes, compare pure C3G with natural extracts in real products, and confirm its effects on intestinal health and chronic disease prevention via in vivo or clinical trials.
