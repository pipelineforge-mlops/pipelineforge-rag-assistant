The malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum relies on coordinated signaling cascades to control progression through its complex life cycle in the mammalian host and insect vector. Mobilization of cytosolic Ca2+ ([Ca2+]c) is central to many of these processes and is linked to the second messengers cGMP, cyclic AMP and inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate. During the symptomatic blood stage these signaling pathways play key roles in erythrocyte invasion, maturation and egress of the parasite. Intraerythrocytic P. falciparum expressing the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator GCaMP3 were used to investigate [Ca2+]c signals elicited by the cGMP phosphodiesterase inhibitor zaprinast during the late trophozoite and schizont stages, using post hoc Hoechst staining to categorize the parasites. Zaprinast elicited two distinct phases of [Ca2+]c increase with the first appearing only in parasites in which DNA replication was already underway and the second arising later in schizogony. Both phases were dependent on activation of protein kinase G and could be mimicked by the cGMP analog 8-Br-cGMP. The first phase occurred as a transient [Ca2+]c increase derived from cyclopiazonic acid-sensitive endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ stores and was suppressed by the 1,4,5-trisphosphate signaling inhibitor 2-aminoethoxydiphenyl borate. The second phase [Ca2+]c increase induced by zaprinast was kinetically distinct and unaffected by cyclopiazonic acid, but was selectively eliminated by chloroquine, suggesting that it is derived from the digestive vacuole. These findings demonstrate that two separate Ca2+ mobilization pathways under the control of cGMP contribute to the [Ca2+]c signals that become active during schizogony in preparation for egress from the erythrocyte.

Malaria is a vector-borne protozoan parasitic infectious disease that has a major global impact with more than 263 million new infections and 573,000 deaths recorded in 2023. Approximately 70% of these deaths are reported for children under 5 years of age, who have poor immunity against the disease (1). Among the five species of Plasmodium that are known to infect humans, Plasmodium falciparum is the most lethal (1, 2). The life cycle of the malaria parasite alternates between sexual reproduction in the mosquito vector and asexual replication in an intermediate vertebrate host, with P. falciparum exclusively infecting humans. The symptomatic phase of malaria occurs during a massive expansion of the parasite through repeated cycles of invasion, replication and lysis of host red blood cells (RBCs). The intraerythrocytic development cycle for P. falciparum takes 48 h, during which the parasites differentiate through morphologically defined stages from ring to trophozoite and then mature into schizonts that release 16 to 32 daughter merozoites to invade new erythrocytes (3). Both egress and invasion processes require coordinated signaling events and interactions between the parasite surface proteins and host cell membranes (4). Parasites also remodel the host RBC and surround themselves within a protective parasitophorous vacuole membrane, maintaining tight control over ion and solute transport (5).

Changes in cytosolic free Ca2+ ([Ca2+]c) are a key signal in the control of malaria parasite development, egress and invasion during the erythrocytic stage and during gametogenesis in the mosquito vector (6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14). There is a Plasmodium phosphoinositide phospholipase C (PLC) that hydrolyses phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (10, 15, 16, 17), and IP3 functions as a second messenger to release Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (10, 18, 19, 20). However, no canonical malaria IP3 receptor has been identified. Plasmodia also contain additional intracellular Ca2+ stores and a store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) pathway, analogous to those in higher organisms (13, 18, 21). A rise in [Ca2+]c has been shown in schizonts before egress (9, 11, 14), and there is a large elevation of [Ca2+]c when free merozoites sense low extracellular K+ (9). We have previously shown that the G protein-coupled receptor -like protein PfSR25 plays a role in sensing the K+ change, and pharmacological evidence indicates that this is coupled to PLC activation and Ca2+ release from intracellular stores (9, 22).

In addition to Ca2+, the cyclic nucleotides adenosine 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate (cAMP) and guanosine 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate (cGMP) play key roles in the development of Apicomplexan parasites, as reviewed by Baker et al. (23). The cAMP signaling pathway plays a role in amplifying the Ca2+-dependent effects of melatonin on parasite cell cycle progression (24). The Plasmodium cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG) is of particular interest because it plays essential roles in both schizont maturation and egress during the erythrocytic development cycle (25), as well as in sporozoite invasion and development in the liver (26, 27). PKG also triggers gametocytogenesis (28), ookinete maturation, and motility (29), indicating that this kinase orchestrates multiple aspects of parasite function (23, 30). In the intraerythrocytic life cycle of the parasite, activation of the cGMP-PKG pathway is upstream of the [Ca2+]c increase that occurs shortly before the release of newly formed merozoites, and previous work has shown that inhibition of PKG with compound 1 or ML10 prevents parasite egress and the progression of parasitemia in vivo and in vitro (30, 31). Consistent with its key role in parasite egress, indirect activation of PKG with the cGMP-specific phosphodiesterase inhibitor zaprinast (Zap) can elicit the release of immature merozoites (8, 25, 32). Thus, the convergence of cGMP-PKG activation and the resulting mobilization of intracellular Ca2+ at the correct stage of parasite development is key to the initiation of egress at the end of the schizont phase.

Activation of PKG in the parasite is governed by the relative activity of guanylyl cyclase (GC), which converts GTP into cGMP, and phosphodiesterase (PDE), which hydrolyses cGMP into the inactive 5′GMP. In Plasmodium parasites, two GC (GCα and GCβ) and four PDE (PDEα, PDEβ, PDEδ, and PDEγ) isoforms have been annotated, but only GCα, PDEα, and PDEβ are expressed during the intraerythrocytic asexual development stages, as reviewed in (23). Inhibition of PKG has been shown to clear the malaria infection in rodents and block transmission to the mosquito vector (30, 33). Furthermore, it has been shown that PKG-dependent stimulation of Ca2+ signaling leads to the activation of Ca2+-dependent protein kinases, which are major effectors at multiple life cycle stages of the parasite across both the mosquito and human host (25, 34, 35, 36). In P. falciparum, knocking down PfCa2+-dependent protein kinase 5 arrested the intraerythrocytic parasites at the schizont stage by preventing membrane rupture without disrupting the maturation of egress proteins, including PfSUB1 and PfSERA5. However, treatment with the PKG inhibitor compound 1 severely affects PfMSP1 processing by PfSUB1, a crucial step in the parasitophorous vacuole membrane rupture that precedes the egress of parasites from the red blood cell (35).

Phosphoproteomic approaches have identified many potential targets for PfPKG and its associated signaling, including those that regulate gene expression, vesicular trafficking, components of signaling pathways, inositol lipid metabolism, membrane transporters, and the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) (31, 37). Downstream of the cGMP-PKG pathway, lipidomic analysis reveals that PDE inhibitor Zap induces an increased hydrolysis of the IP3 precursor PIP2 (31, 38). Although it is not yet possible to establish a sequence of the signaling pathways in their entirety, cGMP-PKG activity is clearly linked to an increase in [Ca2+]c derived from intracellular stores at key steps in the life cycle of malaria parasites (31, 37, 39, 40). However, the Ca2+ storage organelle(s) involved in PKG signaling that lead to this [Ca2+]c increase are not well understood.

The present study investigates Ca2+ signaling and the intracellular Ca2+ pools that are mobilized in response to cGMP elevation in the intraerythrocytic stages of P. falciparum parasites expressing the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator GCaMP3 (PfGCaMP3). The PfGCaMP3 is expressed selectively in the Plasmodium cytosol, avoiding complications due to signals derived from the host RBC or intracellular organelles of the parasite resulting from compartmentalization of the chemical Ca2+ indicator dyes used in previous studies. The PfGCaMP3 also offers better signal to noise and greater sensitivity with less Ca2+ buffering (13, 41). We show that the increase in [Ca2+]c associated with elevated cGMP levels following inhibition of parasite PDE with Zap originates from two separate Ca2+ pools, each with a distinct stage-dependent time of appearance in late trophozoites/schizonts. The first pool yields a rapid but transient [Ca2+]c increase from the ER, which is sensitive to inhibition of SERCA and can also be suppressed by inhibition of the putative IP3-dependent Ca2+ signaling pathway. The second cGMP-sensitive Ca2+ pool is active at a later stage in parasite development and gives rise to a continuous increase in [Ca2+]c. The latter is independent of ER Ca2+ but is inhibited by chloroquine, an antimalarial drug that disrupts the function of the lysosome-like digestive vacuole (DV). The effects of Zap can be mimicked by cGMP analogs and blocked by PKG inhibitors. These studies provide important new insights into the mechanisms by which the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway mobilizes Ca2+ into the cytosol of the parasite during the final intraerythrocytic stages of development. Specifically, as blood stage P. falciparum passes through its 48 h life cycle, the well characterized morphological, trophic and proliferative developmental stages are accompanied by evolving PKG-dependent Ca2+ signaling that culminates in robust Ca2+ mobilization at the end of schizogony to drive exoneme release and initiate parasite egress to begin the next cycle of RBC invasion.

Results

Zaprinast induces stage-dependent biphasic [Ca2+]c increases in P. falciparum

The phosphodiesterase inhibitor Zap has been shown to increase cGMP and cause a rapid rise in [Ca2+]c that is dependent on PKG activation and can lead to premature host cell rupture and parasite egress in Toxoplasma gondii (42, 43, 44, 45, 46) and P. falciparum (32, 47). We investigated the effect of Zap on [Ca2+]c of P. falciparum within the host RBCs by single cell imaging of GCaMP3 in PfGCaMP3 parasites at the late trophozoite to schizont stage. During this phase of the intraerythrocytic life cycle, multiple cycles of asexual DNA replication occur as the trophozoites transition to schizonts with 16 to 32 nuclei. The DNA content can be determined on a cell-by-cell basis by post-staining with Hoechst, and this gives an index of the relative maturation state for each parasite in the imaged field (Fig. 1, A and B). An initial analysis of the [Ca2+]c changes following addition of 100 μM Zap revealed a range of responses, from no detectable [Ca2+]c change to a large spike and subsequent sustained increase in [Ca2+]c (Figs. 1C and S1, Video S1). However, further exploration of the diverse [Ca2+]c signal profiles led to the conclusion that these are correlated with maturation stage as defined by DNA content. To investigate the stage-dependent differences in Ca2+ mobilization, the parasites within each experiment were assigned to groups based on the Hoechst staining intensity relative to mature schizonts in the same field (see methods): the lowest intensity (0–15%), two intermediate intensities (15–30% and 30–70%), and the highest intensity (70–100%, late schizonts), as shown in the histogram of Figure 1B. The [Ca2+]c response profiles to Zap for these groups are presented as the mean [Ca2+]c response averaged from all parasites in each group from a representative experiment in Figure 1C, and an ensemble of individual [Ca2+]c traces for each group is presented in Fig. S2. These data show a clear progression from no [Ca2+]c increase in the lowest DNA content group (0–15%), the appearance of a small [Ca2+]c spike in the next group (15–30%), which increased in amplitude in the most mature stages (30–70% and 70–100%). Moreover, a second slower phase of [Ca2+]c increase arising after the initial [Ca2+]c spike was observed in the latter two groups, with the greatest amplitude of this second phase appearing in the highest Hoechst fluorescence group (70–100%), representing the late schizont stage. The second phase [Ca2+]c response persisted for >30 min after Zap addition (Fig. S3). The initial peak [Ca2+]c responses to Zap from 5 similar experiments are summarized in Figure 1D, and the maximum amplitude of the second phase [Ca2+]c responses are shown in Figure 1E.

Role of ER Ca2+ store in zaprinast-induced [Ca2+]c increases

The ER is a well-characterized Ca2+ store in Plasmodium sp, that depends on a cyclopiazonic acid (CPA)-sensitive SERCA Ca2+ pump and can be mobilized by IP3 (10, 12, 13, 19, 48). We investigated the role of the ER as a source of Zap-induced Ca2+ mobilization using CPA to block the intraerythrocytic SERCA and release the ER Ca2+ store (Figs. 1 and 2). As shown in Figure 1F, the amplitude of the CPA-induced [Ca2+]c increase was reduced in the most mature parasite stages that gave the greatest response to Zap, consistent with a role for the ER Ca2+ store as a source for the Ca2+ mobilized by Zap. There was a clear stage-dependent effect on the duration of the [Ca2+]c signal elicited by CPA in the absence of Zap (Fig. 2A), which was manifest in a faster rate of [Ca2+]c decline from the peak release (rate of fall) and reduced width at half-height in the later stages, with no difference in the initial rate of [Ca2+]c rise (Table 1A). This may reflect an increased capacity for Ca2+ uptake into other intracellular Ca2+ stores or Ca2+ pumping out of the cell in the Zap-responsive parasites. When CPA was added after Zap treatment, the width at half-height was prolonged and the rate of [Ca2+]c decline decreased in these later stage parasites, such that there was no significant difference in the kinetics of [Ca2+]c recovery after CPA between responsive and nonresponsive parasites, despite the decreased amplitude of [Ca2+]c increase in the former group (Fig. 2B, Table 1B). As a more direct approach to investigate the role of the ER as the source of Zap-induced [Ca2+]c increase, the sequence of additions was reversed with CPA added first, followed by Zap (Fig. 2C). Under these conditions, the initial transient [Ca2+]c response to Zap was eliminated, but the slower phase of [Ca2+]c increase observed in the most mature stages was still present. The magnitude of the overall Zap-induced [Ca2+]c increases in the absence and presence of CPA pretreatment are quantitated as the Area Under Curve (AUC) for each of the Hoechst staining groups expressed as ΔF/Fmax.s above the basal value for the duration of the Zap treatment (Fig. 2, F and F). To separately quantify the effect of CPA on the first and second phases of the Zap [Ca2+]c response, we further analyzed data from the group of parasites that showed the largest second phase (identified by the 70–100% intensity range for Hoechst staining). The mean [Ca2+]c response profiles from a representative experiment with Zap added before and after CPA are shown in Figure 2, G and H. The period of Zap exposure was subdivided into two segments that correspond to the first 200 s after Zap addition (shaded blue, green) and the subsequent 400 s of Zap response (shaded red, purple). The [Ca2+]c increases were quantitated for these two periods as AUC (Fig. 2I). Pretreatment with CPA abolished the first phase of Zap response but there was no significant effect of CPA on the second phase. These data confirm that the Zap-induced [Ca2+]c increase is partially attributed to the ER of the intraerythrocytic parasites, which drives the initial [Ca2+]c increase. However, a distinct source of Ca2+ that is not sensitive to CPA is responsible for the second phase of the Zap [Ca2+]c response.

Both phases of [Ca2+]c increase in response to zaprinast are sensitive to inhibition of PKG and depend on cGMP but not cAMP accumulation

Previous studies have shown that the effects of Zap on gametogenesis, sporozoite motility, schizogony and merozoite egress in Plasmodium sp. can all be blocked by the PKG-specific inhibitor compound 1 (25, 31, 32, 38). In order to investigate the role of PKG on the two phases of Zap-induced Ca2+ mobilization in the intraerythrocytic PfGCaMP3 parasites, the infected erythrocytes were preincubated with 2.5 μM compound 1 or the DMSO vehicle control for 30 min prior to initiating the Ca2+ imaging experiment (Fig. 3). These experiments were carried out using the two groups of parasites that showed both [Ca2+]c increase phases, characterized by the 30 to 100% intensity range for Hoechst staining, as defined in Figure 1. The presence of compound 1 suppressed the [Ca2+]c increase elicited by 100 μM Zap throughout the duration of treatment (Fig. 3, A and B). Separate quantitation of the first and second phases of the Zap-induced [Ca2+]c response demonstrated a complete inhibition of the initial fast [Ca2+]c increase (Fig. 3C), and approximately 70% inhibition of the slower extended second phase [Ca2+]c increase (Fig. 3D). In order to investigate whether this residual Ca2+ release might be due to incomplete inhibition of the second phase by compound 1, we tested the more potent PKG inhibitor ML10 at 500 nM (Fig. 4) (30). The results for the two PKG inhibitors were similar for both the first- and second-phase responses. The residual slow Ca2+ release after inhibition of PKG could also reflect an off-target effect of the relatively high 100 μM Zap dose, although this is similar to the concentrations used in most other malaria studies (31, 38, 42). Zap selectively inhibits cGMP hydrolysis by PDEδ and with lower affinity PDEα (23, 33, 49, 50). Under the conditions used in the present studies, Zap increased cGMP levels from 0.61 ± 0.31 to 3.06 ± 1.31 pmol/ml (average ± SD, n = 3). While PDEα is specific for cGMP, PDEβ has dual specificity for cAMP and cGMP and is also present in blood-stage malaria, such that there is potential for crosstalk between these second messengers (23, 33, 50). Previous studies have shown that Zap does not affect cAMP levels in P. falciparum schizonts (39). Nevertheless, we investigated the effect of the phosphodiesterase inhibitor, 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX), which causes an increase in cAMP levels in blood stage P. falciparum (24) but has no effect on PDE-mediated cGMP hydrolysis in isolated P. falciparum membranes (49), to determine whether it mimics the effect of zaprinast on [Ca2+]c. As shown in Figure 5A, 100 μM IBMX had no effect on [Ca2+]c in the Zap-sensitive parasites when added alone, and did not modify the subsequent response to Zap added after IBMX when compared to Zap added first (Fig. 5B). Moreover, analysis of the first and second phases of the Zap-induced [Ca2+]c response showed no difference in either phase (Fig. 5, C and D), suggesting that both are mediated by cGMP. Taken together, the data presented above provide strong evidence for Ca2+ mobilization from two sources in P. falciparum in response to Zap, each driven by cGMP-dependent activation of PKG without a role for cAMP.

Zaprinast-induced [Ca2+]c signals arise via IP3-linked Ca2+ mobilization from the ER, and a separate Ca2+ pool associated with the digestive vacuole

Previous studies have demonstrated a role for inositol lipid hydrolysis and IP3 in ER Ca2+ mobilization and linked this to cGMP-dependent PKG activation in Plasmodium berghei, and P. falciparum (10, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 31, 38, 39). Similar findings have been reported for PLC, IP3 and cGMP-PKG in T. gondii (42, 43, 44, 45). To further characterize the role of the ER in the first phase of the [Ca2+]c response to Zap in intraerythrocytic late phase trophozoite and schizont PfGCaMP3 parasites, we utilized the IP3 blocker 2-aminoethyl diphenylborinate (2-APB) (52). We and others have shown that 2-APB abolishes the IP3-linked [Ca2+]c increase elicited with melatonin in P. falciparum parasites (12, 53, 54), and 2-APB and its dimerized analogs DPB162-AE and DPB163-AE also inhibit SOCE (18). Intraerythrocytic PfGCaMP3 parasites were preincubated for 30 min with DMSO vehicle control or 100 μM 2-APB prior to Zap addition. We have shown previously that depletion of intracellular Ca2+ stores can lead to activation of Ca2+ entry at the parasite plasma membrane in isolated blood-stage trophozoites of P. falciparum, apparently through a SOCE mechanism (13, 18, 52), and recent studies in extracellular tachyzoites of T. gondii have demonstrated a role for cGMP-PKG in stimulating Ca2+ entry secondary to ER Ca2+ mobilization (44). It might be anticipated that the Ca2+ levels at the external face of the parasite plasma membrane would be too low to contribute significant Ca2+ entry during intracellular development within the host cell prior to egress. However, we have shown that the parasitophorous vacuole that surrounds intraerythrocytic P. falciparum maintains a substantially higher [Ca2+] in the order of 40 μM compared to the nanomolar [Ca2+]c of the host RBC, and this is required to support normal parasite growth (7). As an alternative approach to determine whether the second phase of the [Ca2+]c response to Zap might involve activation of a parasite plasma membrane Ca2+ influx pathway in the intraerythrocytic PfGCaMP3, we tested the effect of buffering [Ca2+]c with the Ca2+ chelator 1,2-Bis(o-aminophenoxy)ethane-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (BAPTA). The infected RBCs were loaded with cell-permeant BAPTA-AM (50 μM for 30 min) prior to stimulation with Zap in the presence of physiological Ca2+ levels in the incubation medium. Under these conditions BAPTA is expected to predominantly suppress Ca2+ mobilization from intracellular stores, because parasitophorous vacuole Ca2+ levels are maintained due to continuity with the extracellular Ca2+ of the host RBC (7, 55). In addition to the ER, another Ca2+ store in blood stage malaria is the DV, which accumulates Ca2+ in a H+-dependent manner (13, 21, 56, 57, 58, 59). The DV is a large lysosome-like acidic compartment responsible for the degradation of hemoglobin from the host RBC, which provides amino acids for parasite protein synthesis, serves as a carbon and nitrogen source for parasite metabolism, growth and reproduction, and sequesters the residual heme as hemozoin (60, 61). The DV is the target of the antimalarial drug chloroquine, which is a weak base that accumulates in this acidic compartment and interferes with multiple aspects of its function (62, 63), including disrupting the H+-dependent Ca2+ uptake pathway (64). Discussion

Calcium signaling is involved in the regulation of cellular functions in virtually all eukaryotic organisms (65), and in the malaria parasite there are key roles for [Ca2+]c at multiple life-cycle stages in both the human host and mosquito vector (12, 20, 40, 59, 66, 67). In blood-stage P. falciparum, increases in [Ca2+]c are associated with melatonin-induced synchronization of life-cycle progression (6, 10, 24, 68), and Ca2+ mobilization is also a trigger for the egress of merozoites from RBCs (9, 11, 14). The [Ca2+]c increase in schizonts that precedes RBC membrane rupture and merozoite release is dependent on the cGMP-PKG pathway, and can be mimicked with the PDE inhibitor Zap (31, 32, 38), consistent with the well-established role of PKG in the egress process (25, 37, 39, 69, 70). Blocking the cGMP-PKG pathway in infected rodents can clear the infection and prevent transmission to the mosquito vector (30). Given the importance of cGMP-PKG pathways in malaria, the objective of this work was to understand the downstream [Ca2+]c signaling and Ca2+ stores affected by cGMP elevation and the consequent PKG activation.

The increase in [Ca2+]c elicited by Zap in P. falciparum during growth and replication in the RBC appears in a stage-dependent manner, and consists of two distinct phases arising from different intracellular Ca2+ pools within the intraerythrocytic parasite. We used the DNA stain Hoechst for post hoc categorization of later stage trophozoites and schizonts from synchronized cultures to evaluate the extent of DNA replication in each parasitized RBC as an indicator of relative progression through the life cycle. Thus, the Hoechst staining was used to categorize the individual parasites and create groups based on their DNA content. One approach to this would be to standardize to single copy DNA in the earlier trophozoite stages, but this signal is relatively weak and since Hoechst staining is also dependent on DNA compaction state, we chose to standardize instead on the most highly stained schizont stages. There was no [Ca2+]c response to Zap in the lowest Hoechst-stained group, indicating that cGMP-dependent Ca2+ signals begin to arise only after DNA replication is already underway. Zap caused a small transient [Ca2+]c increase in the next Hoechst-stained group, and the amplitude of this Ca2+ mobilization increased further in the more highly stained groups such that parasites with the largest [Ca2+]c response are those with the highest DNA content. Moreover, the parasites with higher DNA content present two phases of Zap response, a rapid transient [Ca2+]c spike with a duration of about 3 min, followed by a much slower persistent [Ca2+]c increase that was sustained for >30 min.

In blood-stage P. falciparum, DNA replication initiates in the late trophozoite stage and proceeds during schizogony to generate the multinucleate schizont stage (71). Although the Hoechst staining method identified parasites with multiple nuclei, the widefield Ca2+ imaging system used for the present studies did not provide sufficient resolution to count all discrete nuclei or determine exactly which parasites had progressed from the trophozoite to the schizont stage. Based on the Giemsa staining and time in synchronized culture, the range of parasites in the field was identified to include late trophozoites from 34 h post-infection to schizonts. Thus, the Hoechst staining groups with higher DNA content are identified to be in the schizont stage and we conclude that the full biphasic [Ca2+]c increases elicited by Zap are restricted to schizonts. This may reflect the increasing expression of GCα in schizonts prior to merozoite release (23).

Direct evidence that the biphasic increase in [Ca2+]c elicited by Zap was due to activation of the cGMP-PKG pathway comes from our finding that the cell-permeant cGMP analogue 8-Br-cGMP could mimic this effect, albeit with slower kinetics. Moreover, prior stimulation with 8Br-cGMP decreased the [Ca2+]c response to subsequent Zap addition, showing that they act on the same intracellular Ca2+ pools. Further evidence for a role of PKG comes from the observation that the PKG inhibitors compound 1 and ML10 largely ablated the [Ca2+]c increases elicited by Zap. The finding that these agents blocked both the initial and sustained phases of the Zap response provides evidence that both depend on the cGMP-PKG pathway, but does not necessarily exclude that there is a single site of PKG action and one Ca2+ mobilization response is secondary to the other. However, two distinct sites of PKG action are indicated by the fact that only the transient first phase of [Ca2+]c increase is eliminated after ER Ca2+ release by the SERCA inhibitor CPA, and only the prolonged second phase of Ca2+ release is suppressed after targeting the DV with the antimalarial chloroquine.

The ER is the most well characterized intracellular Ca2+ store for the Plasmodium species that have been investigated, and it is well-established that this Ca2+ pool can be mobilized to increase [Ca2+]c at multiple life cycle stages (6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14). It has been proposed that cGMP-PKG-dependent Ca2+ mobilization from the ER occurs via a PLC-IP3-mediated signaling pathway that may be downstream of PKG (31, 37, 38, 40). We have previously shown release of ER Ca2+ stores by IP3 in permeabilized trophozoites and using caged IP3 in intact infected RBCs, and there is also pharmacological evidence that IP3 generation culminates in Ca2+ mobilization from the ER (10, 13, 18, 19, 54). Although the mechanism underlying PLC activation and IP3 generation in Plasmodium is not understood, PKG-dependent PIP2 hydrolysis has been implicated (31, 38). The absence of a canonical IP3R in P. falciparum is a further impediment to understanding IP3-dependent Ca2+ signaling in malaria, although recent reports have linked the multi-pass membrane protein ICM1 to PKG-mediated Ca2+ mobilization and loss of this protein impacts egress and invasion of P. falciparum (38). An IP3 affinity-based assay has also revealed binding to the transmembrane multidrug-resistant protein MDR1 (72). However, neither of these proteins appears to meet the criteria for an IP3R-activated ER Ca2+ release channel. Nevertheless, multiple studies have shown that the IP3R antagonist 2-APB can block ER Ca2+ mobilization in response to stimuli believed to act through IP3 (18, 53, 54, 73), and the present results show that this compound also blocks the first phase of the Zap-induced [Ca2+]c increase. Taken together with our finding that CPA also blocks the first phase of Zap response, these data further support the role of ER Ca2+ release in driving the initial transient [Ca2+]c signal elicited by cGMP elevation and PKG activation.

Since the second phase of the Zap-induced [Ca2+]c increase was unaffected by prior depletion of ER Ca2+ with CPA, we investigated alternative intracellular Ca2+ stores, focusing on the DV, which has been shown to have a large capacity for Ca2+ sequestration (13, 21, 48, 56, 57, 58, 59). The DV is the pharmacological target of the antimalarial drug chloroquine, which accumulates into this acidic compartment as a weak base and disrupts the detoxification of heme to form hemozoin (60, 61, 62, 63). At higher concentrations, chloroquine partially collapses the DV pH gradient, lowering the H+ driving force for Ca2+ uptake into this compartment. The second phase of the Zap-induced [Ca2+]c increase was significantly suppressed in the presence of chloroquine, whereas the first ER-derived phase was unaffected. This pharmacological evidence points to the DV as the second source of Ca2+ release. While it is clear there is a separate activation pathway for Ca2+ mobilization from the DV and from the ER, this does not mean that the ER and vacuolar Ca2+ mobilization pathways are entirely independent. In view of the temporal phase separation between the ER and DV phases, it is possible that there is transfer of Ca2+ from the ER to the DV during the initial phase of Ca2+ release. Indeed, previous studies have shown that Ca2+ released via the ER can be transferred to the DV in Plasmodium and that the ER may play a key role in loading other intracellular compartments in T. gondii (48, 74).

Interestingly, the capacity of alternative Ca2+ stores, including potentially pumping Ca2+ from the parasite cytosol into the parasitophorous vacuole, appears to increase as the late stage intraerythrocytic P. falciparum matures. Thus, while the rate and amplitude of ER Ca2+ release in response to CPA is relatively similar across the range of Hoechst staining levels, the subsequent rate of [Ca2+]c decline, reflecting Ca2+ pumping into other compartments, is significantly faster in the most mature stages (Table 1). Moreover, this enhanced rate of Ca2+ removal from the cytosol is lost after Zap treatment, consistent with the suggestion that the cGMP-PKG pathway reduces Ca2+ pumping into, or enhances Ca2+ release from, one or more of these non-ER Ca2+ pools. We propose that this reflects an increase in Ca2+ mobilization from the DV as it becomes activated by the cGMP-PKG pathway.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that pharmacological activation of the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway acts through at least two internal Ca2+ stores, the ER and the DV. This yields a brief initial [Ca2+]c transient and a longer-lived sustained [Ca2+]c increase, which together play a role in progression through schizogony to merozoite release from the host RBC. The stage-dependent appearance of this biphasic Ca2+ response, with the full-strength signal occurring in the latter stages of schizogony, provides choreography of cyclic nucleotide and Ca2+ signaling timed to coincide with the well-established Ca2+-dependent steps of parasite egress from the host RBC. The IP3-dependent pathway of ER Ca2+ mobilization remains to be fully elucidated, and it is yet to be determined what downstream signaling targets of PKG mediate the mobilization of Ca2+ from the DV. However, this detailed analysis of the developmental time-course and maturation of blood-stage malaria Ca2+ signaling extends our knowledge of the essential cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, and identifies possible new targets to interfere with the parasite lifecycle to treat the disease.

Experimental procedures

P. falciparum culture

P. falciparum strains were maintained at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 0.5% AlbuMAX I (Gibco) (75). Cultures were grown under a 5% CO2, 5% O2, and 90% N2 atmosphere. The culture was synchronized with 5% sorbitol (76) to obtain synchronous parasites. P. falciparum parasites expressing the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator GCaMP3 as an episomal vector (PfGCaMP3) were maintained by the presence of WR99210 at 5 nM (13, 41).

Live-cell Ca2+ imaging

We used the previously generated P. falciparum PfGCaMP3 transgenic parasites expressing GFP-based GCaMP3 Ca2+ sensor to monitor Ca2+ dynamics (41). Synchronized late stage trophozoite to mid-stage schizont parasites from 34 to 40 h post-infection, as defined by Giemsa staining, were enriched using a VarioMACS magnetic column (Miltenyi Biotec) (77) and washed with modified Hank's buffer (in mM: 25 Hepes, 121 NaCl, 5 NaHCO3, 4.7 KCl, 1.2 KH2PO4, 1.2 MgSO4, 2 CaCl2, 10 glucose, pH 7.4). Infected erythrocytes were allowed to adhere to Cell-Tak (Corning) coated borosilicate glass coverslip for 30 min at 37 °C. Coverslips with adhered parasites were mounted in an Attofluor cell chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed to remove non-adhered cells, and filled with 3 ml Hank's buffer. The cell chamber was placed on a 37 °C temperature-controlled stage of a wide-field epifluorescence inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse TE300). Ca2+ imaging was performed using a 40X oil immersion objective and an EMCCD camera (Hamamatsu Photonics ImageEM; www.microscope.healthcare.nikon.com/products/software/nis-elements); PfGCaMP3 images (excitation 488 nm, emission 510 nm long band pass filter) were acquired at 1 Hz using the data acquisition software NIS Elements (Nikon). After each experiment, the parasitized RBCs were incubated in place on the microscope stage with the DNA stain Hoechst at 5 μM for 5 min, and a fluorescence image was acquired at 380 nm excitation.

Cells were treated with the following drugs, as described in the experimental section: zaprinast (100 μM, 5-(2-propoxyphenyl) − 2,3-dihydrotriazolo[4,5-days]pyrimidin-7-one; Sigma), cyclopiazonic acid (CPA; 10 μM), Cayman Chem Co), ionomycin (Iono; 10 μM, Cayman Chem Co), 8-bromoguanosine 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate (8Br-cGMP; 200 μM, Sigma), 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX; 100 μM, Sigma), 2-aminoethoxydiphenyl borate (2-APB; 100 μM), chloroquine (CQ, 10 μM; Sigma), Compound 1 (2.5 μM, pyrrole 4-[2-(4-fluorophenyl)-5-(1-methylpiperidine-4-yl)-1H-pyrrol-3-yl] pyridine, MedChem), BAPTA-AM (50 μM, Cayman), N-[5-[3-[2-(Cyclopropylmethylamino)pyrimidin-4-yl]-7-[(dimethylamino)methyl]-6-methylimidazo[1,2-a]pyridin-2-yl]-2-fluorophenyl]methanesulfonamide) (ML10, 500 nM, BEI resources). In most cases the vehicle control was an equivalent volume of DMSO.

Data analysis

Ca2+ imaging data were acquired and processed using NIS Elements software and analyzed using a custom in-house program built in MATLAB. Data were normalized to the maximum Iono Ca2+ response (F/Fmax). The maximum intensity of the Iono response was also used to define a region of interest (ROI) encompassing each P. falciparum-infected RBC and the ROIs were set to the same area for each parasite using the spot detection algorithm of the NIS Elements software. A typical experiment included 400 to 1000 individual parasitized RBCs in the microscope field. To group the parasites by developmental stage based on DNA content, the fluorescence intensity for Hoechst was corrected for background and then stratified from the highest to the lowest intensity. Since each preparation included some mature schizont-stage parasites, these were used to standardize the Hoechst fluorescence signals from experiment to experiment. The average of the 20 parasites with the highest Hoechst intensity was used to set the 100% level and calculate the range of each group based on their fluorescence relative to this maximum as follows: 0 to 15%, 15 to 30%, 30 to 70%, and 70 to 100%. Any extracellular parasites or intraerythrocytic parasites with unstable baseline Ca2+ signals or that did not respond to CPA or Iono, were excluded from the analysis. Using MATLAB, the individual parasite GCaMP3 [Ca2+]c traces derived from each ROI were normalized to the peak value obtained with Iono for that cell. Traces were smoothed using a point-by-point nearest neighbor running average matrix weighted 1:4:1. Where data are presented as area under curve (AUC), the values are expressed as ΔF/Fmax.s, which is calculate using the MATLAB trapezoid function by numerical integration of the F/Fmax values through the duration of the relevant treatment after subtraction of the basal value. The baseline for calculation of AUC and peak amplitude was based on the initial average fluorescence intensity during at least 50 s prior to the addition of the test compounds. Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism, including unpaired t-tests and ANOVA as appropriate.

Cyclic-GMP quantification

P. falciparum culture was resuspended at a final concentration of 3 × 107 cells/ml. Enriched parasites were incubated for 30 min with 100 μM Zap and control DMSO under standard culture conditions. Sample preparation was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Enzo Biochem). Briefly, following treatment, parasite pellets were resuspended in 0.1 M HCl and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to induce cell lysis. The supernatants were used fresh for the ELISA experiment, and acetylated overnight for 24 h at 4 °C under shaking conditions. The concentration of cGMP from the samples was determined from the standard curve using the optical density values. The experiment was performed independently in triplicate.
