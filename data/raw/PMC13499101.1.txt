Navigating complex presentations of central nervous system tuberculosis: A two-case analysis

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health concern, and the COVID-19 pandemic has negatively impacted TB control. This report presents 2 challenging cases of central nervous system (CNS) tuberculosis with atypical presentation in adult patients. The first case involved a young male patient with disseminated tuberculosis who presented with meningoencephalitis, multiple brain abscesses, vasculitis, and extensive myelopathy, resulting in paraplegia. The patient required multiple neurosurgical interventions and experienced complications such as drug-induced hepatotoxicity and neurogenic bladder. The second case describes an elderly male patient with an oncological history of tuberculous meningitis that mimicked tumor dissemination and presented with multiple hemorrhagic intraventricular nodular lesions. In both cases, the integration of advanced neuroimaging techniques and molecular methods, such as PCR for Mycobacterium tuberculosis in the cerebrospinal fluid, was crucial for confirming the diagnosis. Despite the early initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy and steroids, both patients experienced significant residual neurological morbidity. These cases highlight the importance of considering tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of atypical neurological lesions, even in immunocompetent patients, and the need for rapid diagnostic tests to reduce complications and permanent sequelae of the disease. A multidisciplinary approach and close follow-up are essential for managing complex cases of CNS tuberculosis.

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) involvement in tuberculosis (TB), known as neurotuberculosis (NTB), is one of the most devastating manifestations of extrapulmonary TB, occurring in 5%-10% of these cases and accounting for approximately 1% of all TB cases [1]. It is associated with high rates of mortality and neurological morbidity [2]. Although there are typical findings on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), such as tuberculous meningitis (TBM), hydrocephalus, and parenchymal tuberculomas, a significant number of patients may present with atypical findings that complicate diagnosis and delay treatment [2]. The definitive diagnosis of neurotuberculosis, particularly TBM, is based on the detection of M tuberculosis in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [[4], [5], [6]]. Nucleic acid amplification techniques (NAA), such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), are valuable tools because of their high specificity (99%) and ability to detect mycobacterial DNA even after treatment, although their sensitivity is variable (56%-82%) [6,7]. Contrast-enhanced MRI is the modality of choice for assessing the extent of lesions [7].

Case 1: Disseminated neurotuberculosis presenting with multiple cerebral abscesses, vasculitis, hydrocephalus, and longitudinal extensive myelitis

Clinical presentation

A 23-year-old man with a history of tobacco smoking and recreational tetrahydrocannabinol and cocaine use presented with a 1-week history of headache, fever, recurrent vomiting, gait instability, progressive functional decline, psychomotor agitation, and paranoid delusions (Fig. 1).Fig. 1 Timeline of clinical events, diagnostic workup, treatment, neurosurgical management, and follow-up in case 1.

Fig 1 – dummy alt text

Initial laboratory and cerebrospinal fluid evaluation

Initial contrast-enhanced brain computed tomography (CT) demonstrated preserved corticomedullary differentiation without evidence of acute ischemia, intracranial hemorrhage, abnormal meningeal enhancement, or vascular malformation. Lumbar puncture revealed clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with 210 leukocytes/µL (80% polymorphonuclear cells), protein concentration of 295 mg/dL, glucose concentration of 3.23 mmol/L, and lactate concentration of 10.7 mmol/L. Gram stain, FilmArray meningitis/encephalitis panel, and VDRL were negative. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detected M tuberculosis DNA at low levels, with no rifampicin resistance identified.

Neuroimaging findings

Brain MRI was performed on a 1.5-T scanner and included axial and sagittal T1-weighted, T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and postcontrast T1-weighted sequences. Contrast-enhanced MR angiography and whole-spine MRI were also obtained. Brain MRI (Fig. 2) demonstrated at least 30 supra- and infratentorial microabscesses involving all cerebral lobes, the corpus callosum, posterior limb of the internal capsule, pons, and left cerebellar hemisphere. Several lesions were associated with surrounding vasogenic edema. Acute communicating hydrocephalus with imaging findings of intracranial hypertension was present. In addition, multifocal narrowing and irregularity of the left middle and anterior cerebral arteries were consistent with infectious vasculitis. Microbiological confirmation and treatment

To evaluate for disseminated disease, bronchoalveolar lavage was performed, and PCR was also positive for M tuberculosis, confirming disseminated tuberculosis. Standard anti-tuberculous therapy with rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol was initiated together with adjunctive corticosteroid therapy.

Neurosurgical management

Because of acute communicating hydrocephalus, an external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed. Recurrent EVD dysfunction associated with intraventricular hemorrhage required repeat neurosurgical intervention, and definitive cerebrospinal fluid diversion was achieved with implantation of a programmable ventriculoatrial shunt.

Follow-up imaging

Follow-up contrast-enhanced brain MRI demonstrated resolution of hydrocephalus, recovery of the caliber of the left anterior and middle cerebral arteries, decreased leptomeningeal enhancement, and reduction in both the size and enhancement of multiple tuberculomas. Residual postoperative bifrontal changes, late subacute intraventricular blood products, and thin bilateral subdural hematomas were also identified. Follow-up spine MRI showed marked improvement of the longitudinal myelitis, with only mild residual cord T2 hyperintensity and resolution of intramedullary enhancement.

Clinical outcome

The patient's systemic condition progressively improved following treatment. At the most recent follow-up, he continued to have dense paraplegia and neurogenic bladder, while dysphagia secondary to soft palate paresis had partially improved with rehabilitation.

Case 2: Intraventricular neurotuberculosis mimicking disseminated central nervous system malignancy

Clinical presentation

A 68-year-old man with a medical history of arterial hypertension, obesity, chronic vertigo, and previously treated renal and bladder carcinoma in remission presented with 2 episodes of transient loss of consciousness accompanied by headache and recurrent vomiting. MRI demonstrated multiple contrast-enhancing nodular lesions distributed along the supra- and infratentorial ependymal surfaces, involving the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles, septum pellucidum, right caudate nucleus, thalamus, and cerebellar peduncles. The lesion involving the right caudate nucleus demonstrated a focal hemorrhagic component, and several lesions exhibited partial diffusion restriction. Multivoxel proton MR spectroscopy demonstrated an increased choline peak, decreased N-acetylaspartate (NAA), and a prominent lipid peak. Although a small focal hemorrhagic component was present within the right caudate lesion, it did not preclude interpretation of the spectroscopic findings, which remained highly suggestive of a granulomatous infectious process. Collectively, the imaging findings, including the perfusion and spectroscopic characteristics, initially favored disseminated high-grade neoplasm or primary central nervous system lymphoma.

Cerebrospinal fluid evaluation and diagnostic reasoning

Lumbar puncture revealed lymphocytic pleocytosis (12 cells/µL; 91% lymphocytes), marked hyperproteinorrachia (315 mg/dL), a glucose concentration of 74 mg/dL, elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels (10.6 U/L), and a positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for M tuberculosis. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing was negative.

Although the patient's previous history of renal and bladder malignancies initially raised concern for metastatic disease or primary central nervous system lymphoma, several findings progressively favored an infectious etiology. The cerebrospinal fluid profile, characterized by lymphocytic pleocytosis, marked hyperproteinorrachia, elevated ADA levels, and positive PCR for M tuberculosis, was considerably more consistent with tuberculous meningitis than with neoplastic leptomeningeal dissemination. Furthermore, the characteristic multifocal subependymal and intraventricular distribution of the lesions, together with the absence of clinical or radiological evidence of recurrent systemic malignancy, further reduced the likelihood of metastatic disease. The integration of advanced neuroimaging findings with microbiological confirmation therefore shifted the diagnostic probability away from neoplasia and strongly supported central nervous system tuberculosis.

Management

The patient experienced recurrent episodes of severe headache and vomiting suggestive of intracranial hypertension despite the absence of hydrocephalus on neuroimaging. Because microbiological confirmation had already been established by CSF PCR and the imaging findings were concordant with neurotuberculosis, brain biopsy was considered unnecessary, as histopathological confirmation was unlikely to alter therapeutic management. Standard weight-adjusted anti-tuberculous therapy together with adjunctive corticosteroid therapy was initiated promptly.

Clinical outcome

The patient demonstrated progressive clinical improvement following treatment, with complete resolution of headache and vomiting. Follow-up neuroimaging showed a reduction in both the size and contrast enhancement of the intraventricular and subependymal lesions, supporting the diagnosis of central nervous system tuberculosis and confirming an appropriate therapeutic response.

Discussion

Neurotuberculosis (NTB) is one of the most severe manifestations of extrapulmonary tuberculosis and remains a major cause of neurological morbidity and mortality, particularly in endemic regions. Approximately 5%-10% of patients with extrapulmonary tuberculosis develop central nervous system (CNS) involvement, whereas extra-neural tuberculosis is present in nearly half of affected individuals [1,2,8]. Hematogenous dissemination leads to the formation of caseating granulomas (Rich foci) within the brain, spinal cord, and meninges. Subsequent rupture of these lesions into the subarachnoid space initiates the inflammatory cascade responsible for tuberculous meningitis, hydrocephalus, vasculitis, and parenchymal disease [1,4,6,7]. Although children and immunocompromised individuals remain at greatest risk, NTB should also be considered in immunocompetent adults presenting with atypical neurological syndromes [1].

The neuroradiological spectrum of NTB is remarkably broad, encompassing meningeal, parenchymal, spinal, and miscellaneous manifestations [3,9]. While basal meningeal enhancement, hydrocephalus, infarctions, and tuberculomas represent the classical imaging findings, atypical presentations such as intraventricular tuberculomas, ependymal involvement, diffuse cerebritis, spinal pachymeningitis, extensive myelitis, or multifocal microabscesses may closely resemble inflammatory, infectious, or neoplastic disorders [3,9,10]. These atypical imaging appearances require a systematic radiological approach because isolated imaging findings may be misleading. Similar diagnostic challenges have been described in other uncommon neurological diseases, where unusual MRI features initially suggested alternative diagnoses before careful neuroradiological interpretation established the correct diagnosis [11].

Contrast-enhanced MRI remains the imaging modality of choice for evaluating CNS tuberculosis because of its superior sensitivity for detecting meningeal inflammation, parenchymal lesions, vascular complications, cranial nerve involvement, and spinal disease [1,3,8]. In addition to conventional sequences, advanced techniques including diffusion-weighted imaging, perfusion imaging, and proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) substantially improve lesion characterization, particularly when imaging findings are atypical or mimic malignancy. A dominant lipid peak, typically centered at approximately 1.3 ppm, together with reduced N-acetylaspartate (NAA), is highly suggestive of tuberculoma and helps distinguish these lesions from primary CNS tumors or metastatic disease [3,12]. Likewise, the absence of the metabolic patterns typically associated with pyogenic abscesses further supports the diagnosis of tuberculous infection [12].

The first case illustrates the aggressive phenotype of disseminated NTB in a young immunocompetent adult, with simultaneous meningoencephalitis, multiple cerebral microabscesses, infectious vasculitis, acute communicating hydrocephalus, and longitudinally extensive thoracic myelitis. This constellation of findings reflects widespread hematogenous dissemination and demonstrates the ability of MRI to comprehensively assess disease burden beyond the brain parenchyma. Despite prompt microbiological confirmation and early initiation of anti-tuberculous therapy, the patient required multiple neurosurgical procedures because of persistent hydrocephalus and ultimately developed permanent neurological sequelae. This case emphasizes that delayed neurological injury may occur despite appropriate antimicrobial treatment, reinforcing the importance of early diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and close radiological follow-up [2,9].

The second case highlights an uncommon presentation of NTB characterized by multiple intraventricular and subependymal enhancing nodules in a patient with a previous history of urological malignancy. The imaging appearance, together with spectroscopic findings demonstrating elevated choline and reduced NAA, initially suggested disseminated high-grade neoplasm or primary CNS lymphoma. However, the cerebrospinal fluid profile—characterized by lymphocytic pleocytosis, marked hyperproteinorrachia, elevated adenosine deaminase levels, and positive PCR for M tuberculosis—substantially shifted the diagnostic probability toward neurotuberculosis. The absence of evidence supporting recurrent systemic malignancy further strengthened this interpretation and allowed invasive brain biopsy to be avoided. This case illustrates how integrating neuroimaging with microbiological and molecular testing can substantially improve diagnostic confidence when imaging findings are nonspecific. Similar diagnostic dilemmas have been reported in other infectious diseases presenting with atypical clinical or radiological features, underscoring the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and incorporating molecular techniques early in the diagnostic work-up [13].

The complementary use of advanced neuroimaging and rapid molecular diagnostics represents one of the principal lessons from these cases. Although imaging frequently establishes the initial differential diagnosis, definitive diagnosis of NTB still relies on microbiological confirmation whenever feasible. Polymerase chain reaction has become an invaluable adjunct because of its high specificity and its ability to establish the diagnosis rapidly, thereby facilitating earlier treatment initiation and potentially reducing irreversible neurological injury [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Current treatment consists of prolonged combination anti-tuberculous therapy supplemented with adjunctive corticosteroids in patients with tuberculous meningitis or significant inflammatory disease [7,14,15]. Nevertheless, neurological disability remains common despite adequate therapy, particularly in patients presenting with hydrocephalus, vasculitis, spinal cord involvement, or delayed diagnosis. Accordingly, optimal management requires close collaboration among neuroradiologists, neurologists, neurosurgeons, infectious disease specialists, and rehabilitation teams.

Together, these 2 cases illustrate the wide neuroradiological spectrum of central nervous system tuberculosis and reinforce that atypical MRI findings should not exclude an infectious etiology, even in immunocompetent patients or in those with a previous history of malignancy. Recognition of unusual imaging patterns, prompt molecular confirmation, and multidisciplinary management are essential to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures, expedite treatment, and minimize permanent neurological disability.

Conclusion

Central nervous system tuberculosis continues to pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, especially in atypical forms, such as multiple abscesses, intraventricular involvement, cerebral vasculitis, and extensive myelitis. The 2 cases presented illustrate how the disease can mimic different oncological or infectious entities, thus delaying diagnosis if a high index of clinical suspicion is not maintained. The integration of advanced neuroimaging techniques (contrast MRI, spectroscopy, and perfusion) with molecular methods in the cerebrospinal fluid, such as PCR for M tuberculosis, is essential to confirm the etiology and guide timely treatment. Despite the early initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy and steroids, residual neurological morbidity may be significant, highlighting the need for a multidisciplinary approach and close follow-up. These findings underscore the importance of considering tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of atypical neurological lesions, even in immunocompetent patients, and strengthening access to rapid diagnostic tests to reduce complications and permanent sequelae.
