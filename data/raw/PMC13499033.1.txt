Background

To evaluate the associations of the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and related obesity-combined indexes with the incidence of major vision-threatening ocular diseases.

Methods

This prospective population-based cohort study included 400,237 participants from the UK Biobank. The TyG index and derived indexes (combined with waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, and body mass index) were calculated using baseline laboratory and anthropometric data. The outcomes were incident cases of cataract, POAG, AMD, and diabetic retinopathy (DR), ascertained using self-reported data, hospital inpatient records, and primary care records. Cox proportional hazards models were used, with restricted cubic splines fitted to characterize dose-response relationships. Secondary analyses assessed the associations with retinal thicknesses and vascular features.

Results

Over a median follow-up of 13.0 years, 46,151 cataract, 6317 POAG, 8276 AMD, and 5220 DR incident cases were identified. Distinct nonlinear associations were observed: the TyG index exhibited a reverse L-shaped relationship with cataract and DR, a J-shaped association with AMD, and a U-shaped relationship with POAG (all P total < 0.01, all P nonlinear < 0.05), suggesting an estimated optimal range from 8.6 to 9.1. Furthermore, elevated TyG-obesity indexes exhibited monotonic positive associations with the risk of cataract, AMD, and DR (all P total < 0.001), whereas moderate elevation was associated with a reduced risk of POAG (all P total < 0.01, all P nonlinear < 0.05). Elevated TyG-obesity indexes were also associated with retinal thinning and vascular abnormalities, providing supportive discrimination and risk-stratification information for incident DR.

Conclusions

The TyG index and related obesity-combined indexes show disease-specific associations with major sight-threatening conditions, accompanied by retinal structural alterations. Maintaining these parameters within optimal ranges could help mitigate the risk of vision impairment.

Cataract is the foremost cause of blindness, whereas glaucoma, AMD, and diabetic retinopathy (DR) are the three most common causes of irreversible blindness worldwide.1 Current clinical practice in these ocular diseases predominantly focuses on treatment at advanced stages, with a lack of effective early preventive strategies.2 Previous evidence has suggested a strong association between these ocular diseases and systemic metabolic disturbances.3–8 Therefore it is imperative to identify relevant risk indicators that can signal the likelihood of these diseases before the onset of pathological changes, guiding early lifestyle interventions to mitigate the burden of these vision-threatening conditions.

Insulin resistance is a central pathophysiological feature of metabolic syndrome and contributes to dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and vascular dysfunction.9,10 Conventional glycemic markers, including fasting glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), are widely used to assess glycemic status and guide diabetes management, but they are not direct surrogates for insulin sensitivity.11,12 The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp serves as the reference standard for assessing insulin resistance but is labor-intensive, costly, and impractical for routine clinical use.11,13,14 In recent years, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, which combines blood triglyceride and glucose levels, has been suggested as a simpler and reliable surrogate for insulin resistance.15–17 It demonstrates strong correlations with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp and is more feasible for both clinical practice and epidemiological studies.18 Compared with isolated cardiometabolic measures, the TyG index integrates lipid and glucose metabolic information without requiring additional insulin measurement. This practical advantage supports its potential scalability in future risk assessment settings.19,20 Recent research indicates that the TyG index has been associated with multiple cardiometabolic and systemic outcomes.21–25 However, existing ocular studies have mostly been cross-sectional, case-control, small-scale, or focused on a single disease.8,26,27 Consequently, large-scale prospective evidence linking these indexes to multiple major vision-threatening ocular diseases, particularly with respect to nonlinear risk patterns and potential thresholds, remains limited.

Because adiposity may amplify insulin-resistance-related metabolic risk,28,29 further combining the TyG index with anthropometric obesity indicators, including the triglyceride-glucose-waist circumference (TyG-WC), triglyceride-glucose-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), and triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) may more accurately reflect the body's overall metabolic risk.30–34 These indicators are often more effective than the TyG index alone for risk stratification and disease discrimination.31,33,34 Therefore the potential of these metrics to assess the risk of vision-threatening ocular diseases warrants further investigation.

To explore this further, we conducted a prospective study based on the UK Biobank cohort to examine both linear and nonlinear associations between the TyG index, its combination with obesity indexes, and the incidence of major vision-threatening ocular diseases over an extended follow-up period. This study may refine risk assessment for major vision-threatening ocular diseases and provide insight into their metabolic underpinnings.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Participants

The UK Biobank is a large-scale prospective cohort study that enrolled over 500,000 participants from across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. At recruitment, participants were aged 37 to 73 years. Data collection incorporated touchscreen questionnaires, interviews, physical examinations, biological samples, and imaging procedures, following detailed protocols available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/. The study received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided electronic informed consent at enrollment. The present analysis adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines and was conducted under UK Biobank application number 93118.

Participants lacking data on blood triglycerides, glucose, or obesity-related indicators were excluded (N = 75,048), as were those with a history of any of the four major vision-threatening ocular diseases at baseline (N = 32,640) or a history of eye injury or trauma (N = 3033). After applying these exclusion criteria, 400,237 participants were eligible and included in the present study.

Assessment of TyG, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI

Biochemical indicators, including glucose and blood triglycerides, were measured from peripheral blood samples collected at baseline. These samples were obtained with informed consent and subsequently processed by separation into individual components before storage at −80°C. All biochemical analyses were conducted in the UK Biobank central laboratory using a Beckman Coulter AU5800 automated clinical chemistry analyzer. Glucose and blood triglyceride concentrations were determined using enzymatic methods. The corresponding field IDs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Comprehensive protocols detailing sample collection, processing, and quality control procedures are publicly accessible in previous publications.35 Based on established formulas from prior studies,16,17,30 four TyG-related indexes were calculated as follows:1. TyG = ln [triglycerides (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2]

2. Body mass index (BMI) = body mass (kg) /height2 (m2)

3. Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) = waist circumference (WC) (cm)/height (cm)

4. TyG-WC = TyG × WC (in cm); TyG-WHtR = TyG × WHtR; TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI

Assessment of Vision-Threatening Ocular Diseases

In this study, we investigated four major vision-threatening ocular diseases: cataract, POAG, AMD, and DR. Consistent with the methods used in previous research,36–38 incidence dates for these conditions were ascertained using self-reported data, hospital inpatient records, and primary care data. Hospital diagnoses were identified via International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes for both ICD-10 and ICD-9 (Category ID 2002). Primary care data were recorded by healthcare professionals including general practitioners and nurse practitioners. Self-reported data was collected through verbal interviews (Field ID 20002) and touchscreen questionnaires, such as the query “Has a doctor told you that you have any of the following problems with your eyes?” (Field ID 6148). Detailed diagnostic coding criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Follow-up data were available through October 31, 2022, for England, August 31, 2022, for Scotland, and May 31, 2022, for Wales. The duration of follow-up was calculated from the date of recruitment to the date of the first diagnosis of the ocular disease of interest or censoring. The primary endpoint was defined as the first recorded diagnosis of each ocular disease. Participants were right-censored at the earliest occurrence of: (1) death from any cause, (2) loss to follow-up, or (3) the country-specific administrative censoring date.

Covariates

A broad range of potential confounders across multiple domains, including sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, and health-related status, were accounted for in the analyses. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Retinal Layer Thicknesses and Vascular Features

To provide complementary structural context and a refined anatomical basis for the observed disease associations, we quantified the associations of TyG-related indexes with retinal layer thicknesses and vascular features available in the UK Biobank. Specifically, we used the thicknesses of seven retinal layers, including retinal nerve fiber layer, ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer, inner nuclear layer, outer plexiform plus outer nuclear layer, photoreceptor segment (comprising both the inner and outer segments of photoreceptors), retinal pigment epithelium-Bruch's membrane complex, and overall macular thickness, as well as eight quantitative retinal vascular features that comprehensively quantify the fractal dimension, tortuosity, and caliber of the retinal vasculature. Details can be found in Supplementary Methods section.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means with standard deviations (SDs) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Associations between TyG-related indexes and vision-threatening ocular diseases were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. To ensure robustness and transparency, confounding factors were stepwise adjusted for across three models:36,37 Model 1 included age and sex; Model 2 added ethnicity, Townsend Deprivation Index, alcohol intake frequency, smoking status, and physical activity; Model 3 further included history of hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Missing covariate data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (five imputations), with all other covariates as predictors, as implemented in the “mice” R package.

To assess dose-response relationships, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots within the Cox regression framework were applied, with knot selection and model fit assessment based on the minimum Akaike Information Criterion. Thresholds for variables with nonlinear associations were determined using piecewise regression, with the optimal cutpoint selected based on the maximum log-likelihood. All spline models were adjusted according to model 3 covariates.

Cross-sectional associations of the TyG index, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI with retinal layer thicknesses and retinal vascular features were assessed using linear regression models. These analyses were adjusted for model 3 covariates, and multiple testing was controlled using the false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted using the “pROC” package, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate model discrimination. To assess the supportive discriminative information of TyG-related indexes for incident DR, we compared baseline models with extended models. The baseline models incorporated either demographic variables alone (age and sex) or in combination with the corresponding anthropometric measure (WC, WHtR, or BMI). The extended models were constructed by additionally including the respective TyG-related index (TyG index, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, or TyG-BMI). Differences in AUCs between models were evaluated using DeLong's test. Furthermore, additional ROC analyses compared the TyG-related indexes with models incorporating their corresponding individual components, including triglycerides, glucose, and the relevant anthropometric measure (Supplementary Fig. S1). The component-variable models showed higher AUCs than the corresponding TyG-related indexes, which is statistically expected because models containing the original variables are more flexible than a single formula-derived index.39–43 Importantly, this finding does not negate the supportive discrimination and risk-stratification value of these composite indexes, as our study aimed to evaluate them as accessible and parsimonious metabolic risk markers rather than to develop a high-dimensional clinical prediction model.

Participants were classified into quintiles for each TyG-related index, including TyG index, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI. For incident DR, Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for Q1, Q3, and Q5 to visualize risk stratification. Differences across curves in the unweighted analysis were evaluated using the log-rank test. To assess the robustness of these patterns after covariate balancing, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was performed using multinomial propensity scores, with quintile group as the exposure and age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index, smoking status, drinking frequency, physical activity, hypertension, and diabetes as covariates. Stabilized IPTW weights were then applied to generate weighted Kaplan–Meier curves.

Interaction and stratified analyses were undertaken according to age at baseline (≤ 60 years vs. > 60 years) and sex (male vs. female). Potential interaction effects were assessed using likelihood ratio tests, comparing models with and without the interaction term between the stratification factor and exposure. Five sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of results. These included the following: (a) excluding participants with less than two years of follow-up to reduce potential reverse causality; (b) excluding participants with any missing covariate data; (c) further adjusting for age squared to account for possible nonlinear age effects on outcomes; (d) restricting analyses to cases confirmed by hospital-based clinical diagnoses, thereby excluding those identified solely via self-report or primary care records44; and (e) performing exploratory cross-sectional analyses using UK Biobank Reading Centre-graded image-derived ocular phenotypes. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Because these image-derived phenotypes were assessed in a selected retinal imaging subset and did not represent longitudinal incident outcomes, they were interpreted as exploratory sensitivity analyses rather than confirmatory evidence.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

Among 502,132 participants at baseline in the UK Biobank, 400,237 participants were eligible for analysis. An overview flow chart of the study design is presented in Figure 1A. During a median follow-up of 13.0 years, 46,151 cataract cases (11.54%), 6317 POAG cases (1.58%), 8276 AMD cases (2.07%), and 5220 DR cases (1.30%) were documented. A Venn diagram (Fig. 1B) visualizes the case overlaps, whereas Figure 1C shows the proportion of incident cases during follow-up for each ocular disease across baseline age groups. Table 1 summarized the baseline characteristics of study participants stratified by the occurrence of the four diseases during follow-up. Compared to participants who did not develop these diseases, those who did were more likely to have a history of hypertension or diabetes, and exhibited higher baseline levels of the four TyG-related indexes (all P < 0.001).

Associations Between the TyG Index and Ocular Diseases

Table 2 shows the relationships between the TyG index and cataract, POAG, AMD and DR in different models. Compared with the lowest quartile, the fully adjusted HRs for participants in the highest TyG quartile were 1.04 (95% CI 1.01–1.07; P = 0.013; model 3) for cataract, 2.27 (95% CI, 2.04–2.51; P < 0.001; model 3) for DR, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.84–0.97; P = 0.003; model 3) for POAG, and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.86–0.98; P = 0.003; model 3) for AMD. To explore potential nonlinear associations between the TyG index and the four diseases, we performed RCS regression. A reverse L-shaped association was observed between the TyG index and both cataract (P nonlinear < 0.001, Fig. 2A) and DR (P nonlinear = 0.027, Fig. 2D), with inflection points at 8.876 and 8.602, respectively. In contrast, a U-shaped association was found for POAG (P nonlinear = 0.004, Fig. 2B), with an inflection point at 9.032, whereas a J-shaped relationship was identified for AMD (P nonlinear = 0.002, Fig. 2C), with an inflection point at 9.110. These findings suggest that the optimal range for the TyG index is approximately between 8.6 and 9.1.

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analysis

To further assess the robustness of the observed associations, we conducted several sensitivity analyses by excluding participants with a follow-up time of less than two years, excluding those with missing covariate data, adjusting for age-squared, restricting the analysis to cases confirmed through clinical diagnoses, and performing exploratory cross-sectional analyses using image-derived ocular phenotypes. The first four sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary longitudinal findings (Supplementary Figs. S2–S5). The image-derived ocular phenotype analyses, which were based on a selected retinal imaging subset, did not fully reproduce statistical significance but showed broadly similar trends, thereby providing cross-sectional supportive evidence (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The results of the interaction analyses stratified by age and sex are shown in Supplementary Table S3. Age was found to significantly modify the association between the TyG index and four diseases (all P for interaction < 0.001). In addition, sex significantly influences the relationships of the TyG index with both POAG (P for interaction = 0.002) and DR (P for interaction < 0.001). The specific subgroup RCS results are shown in Supplementary Figure S7. Notably, the effects of an increased TyG index were consistently more pronounced in the younger subpopulation across all four diseases. Regarding sex, males were more susceptible to the risk of POAG associated with a decrease in the TyG index, whereas females were more likely to develop DR in response to an elevated TyG index.

Relationships Between the TyG-Obesity Indexes and Ocular Diseases

The associations between TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI with the four diseases are depicted in Figure 3. For cataract (Fig. 3A) and DR (Fig. 3D), higher levels of all three indexes were associated with a greater risk. Specifically, for cataract, compared with the third quintile, the fully adjusted HRs of the highest quintile were 1.09 for TyG-WC, 1.10 for TyG-WHtR, and 1.07 for TyG-BMI (all P < 0.001). For DR, the fully adjusted HRs for the highest quintile reached 1.78 for TyG-WC, 1.84 for TyG-WHtR, and 1.69 for TyG-BMI (all P < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for POAG across quintiles (Fig. 3B), although TyG-BMI showed borderline positive associations in Q1 and Q2 (both P = 0.050) and a suggestive protective association in Q4 (P = 0.053). Moreover, for AMD, the highest quintiles of TyG-WHtR and TyG-BMI were significantly associated with an increased risk (Fig. 3C), with corresponding HRs of 1.10 (P = 0.007) and 1.10 (P = 0.004), respectively. We further assessed the nonlinear associations of the four diseases with TyG-WC (Fig. 4A), TyG-WHtR (Fig. 4B), and TyG-BMI (Fig. 4C), all of which demonstrated significant nonlinear associations (all P for total < 0.01, all P for nonlinear < 0.05). Most associations showed monotonic patterns, with risk remaining relatively stable at lower index levels before increasing gradually. TyG-BMI and POAG were the main exception, showing a J-shaped relationship. Across outcomes, the steepest risk gradient was observed for DR, whereas associations with cataract, POAG, and AMD were comparatively more gradual.

Correlations Between the TyG-Obesity Indexes and Retinal Structural Parameters

To provide structural evidence for the aforementioned associations, we further examined the relationships between TyG-derived indexes and retinal layer thickness as well as vascular features. Figure 5A shows a schematic representation of retinal layers together with the correlation results. The three TyG-obesity indexes (TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI) were associated with thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer (β ranging from −0.131 to −0.200; all FDR < 0.001). All four indexes were associated with thinning of the outer plexiform plus outer nuclear layer (β from −0.104 to −0.213; all FDR < 0.05), photoreceptor segment (β from −0.114 to −0.343; all FDR < 0.001), and overall macular thickness (β from −0.320 to −0.784; all FDR < 0.001). Figure 5B illustrates the workflow for retinal vascular feature extraction and the corresponding results. All four indexes were negatively correlated with the fractal dimension of arteries (β from −0.011 to −0.041; all FDR < 0.05) and the central retinal arteriolar caliber (β from −0.011 to −0.018; all FDR < 0.05), while showing positive correlations with the central retinal venular caliber (β from 0.040 to 0.085; all FDR < 0.001). In addition, the three TyG-obesity indexes were associated with increased tortuosity density of the combined vasculature (β from 0.020 to 0.024; all FDR < 0.01) and veins (β from 0.016 to 0.022; all FDR < 0.05). These findings may reflect underlying retinal neurodegeneration and vascular dysfunction.

Discriminative and Risk-Stratification Value of TyG-Related Indexes for Incident DR

Given the strong correlation between the TyG-related indexes and DR, we performed ROC analysis to further assess their additional discriminative information for incident DR beyond demographic and anthropometric factors (Fig. 5C). Adding the TyG index to the baseline model including age and sex improved discrimination for incident DR, increasing the AUC from 0.664 to 0.776 (DeLong's test, P < 0.001). Similar improvements were observed for the TyG-obesity indexes when added to models that included age, sex, and the corresponding anthropometric measure (all DeLong's test P < 0.001): the AUC increased from 0.775 to 0.806 after adding TyG-WC, from 0.783 to 0.813 after adding TyG-WHtR, and from 0.762 to 0.795 after adding TyG-BMI. These results support the discrimination and risk-stratification potential of these parsimonious composite indexes but do not imply that they constitute superior prediction models.

To illustrate the risk-stratification performance of TyG-related indexes, Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted across representative quintiles (Fig. 5D). Across the TyG index, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI, cumulative incidence showed a clear stepwise increase from Q1 to Q3 to Q5, with Q5 consistently exhibiting the highest risk. The divergence between quintiles became more pronounced with longer follow-up, and the differences across Q1, Q3, and Q5 were statistically significant for all four indexes (all log-rank P < 0.001). As a sensitivity analysis, IPTW-weighted Kaplan–Meier curves showed highly consistent patterns, supporting the robustness of the observed risk gradient after balancing baseline covariates (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Discussion

The present study leveraged data from 400,237 participants to comprehensively investigate the prospective associations between the TyG index and the incidence of vision-threatening ocular diseases. We observed distinct dose-response relationships: an inverse L-shaped association for cataract and DR, a J-shaped association for AMD, and a U-shaped association for POAG. Similar associations between TyG-obesity metrics and ocular diseases further reinforce the robustness of the observed patterns.

The TyG index and its obesity-related derivatives exhibited a generally consistent pattern for cataract, AMD and DR, with risk remaining stable at lower levels and gradually increasing beyond a certain threshold. This nonlinear pattern aligns with findings from previous epidemiological studies. For instance, a study based on National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey reported a suggestive association between higher TyG index and the presence of cataract (odds ratio = 1.28; 95% CI, 0.99–1.67).26 Similarly, an investigation conducted in Singapore reported a significant association between the TyG index and DR prevalence (odds ratio = 1.4; 95% CI, 1.1–1.7).27 Moreover, a small-scale case-control study involving 71 participants found that the TyG index was significantly higher in individuals with exudative AMD compared to controls.8 However, these cross-sectional or case-control studies were limited by small sample sizes and the susceptibility to reverse causality. To address these limitations, the present study provides novel population-based prospective evidence, thereby consolidating TyG as an independent metabolic risk factor of these outcomes, and identifying potential threshold effects. For AMD and POAG, the associations with the TyG index became more apparent after further adjustment for socioeconomic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic factors, suggesting that more comprehensive covariate control may better isolate its independent metabolic association in these etiologically complex diseases.

The relationships between TyG-related indexes and retinal structural alterations further validate the above associations, meanwhile providing clues to the underlying mechanisms. Specifically, elevated indexes co-occurred with thinning of multiple retinal layers, reduced vascular fractal dimension, increased vessel tortuosity, narrower arterioles, and dilated venules—changes that may indicate neurodegeneration and compromised vascular function.45–49 Mechanistically, elevated TyG index levels are indicative of increased insulin resistance and dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism.18,50 These metabolic disturbances may accelerate lens damage and promote cataract development by triggering chronic inflammation and oxidative stress pathways.51,52 In the retina, persistent hyperglycemia and lipid metabolism disorders could compromise vascular endothelial cells and retinal pigment epithelial cells, leading to apoptosis, increased vascular permeability, and pathological neovascularization—key processes in the pathogenesis of both AMD and DR.4,6,53–55 Furthermore, obesity may synergize with or exacerbate these pathological mechanisms. Visceral adipose tissue in obese individuals secretes proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha, and up regulates Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor and Intercellular Adhesion Molecule-1.56–58 These molecules activate monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, fueling an inflammatory cascade that further impairs endothelial function and accelerates ocular vascular damage.7,30,54,59,60

Our ROC analyses further showed that TyG-related indexes provided additional discriminative information for incident DR beyond demographic and anthropometric factors. Notably, among the extended models, the model incorporating TyG-WHtR achieved the highest AUC (0.813), suggesting that the combination of glucolipid metabolism and central adiposity may capture clinically relevant metabolic risk information associated with DR. By incorporating a direct measure of central adiposity, such composite indexes serve as surrogate markers of adipose tissue-driven inflammatory burden.58 Consistent with the ROC findings, the Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated clear separation in cumulative DR incidence across representative quintiles of TyG-related indexes, which remained evident after IPTW-based covariate balancing. These results further support the utility of TyG-related indexes for preliminary DR risk stratification. The discriminative utility of TyG-based composite indexes has also been validated in other disease contexts.17,31–33,61–63 From a practical perspective, waist circumference and height are routinely collected in electronic health records, allowing for low-cost, scalable preliminary risk stratification, a potential complement to fundus photography-based screening programs, particularly in resource-limited settings. Together, these findings support the potential of TyG-related indexes for preliminary discrimination and risk stratification, while emphasizing their role as accessible and parsimonious composite risk markers rather than as superior prediction models.43,64

Notably, the spline analysis suggested a U-shaped association between the TyG index and the risk of POAG. This counter-intuitive pattern suggests a more complex underlying pathophysiology. Specifically, extremely low TyG levels may reflect hypoglycemia or hypotriglyceridemia. Hypoglycemia can trigger counterregulatory hormonal responses such as increased epinephrine release, which promotes vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation, potentially impairing microvascular perfusion of the optic nerve.65 And several metabolomics studies have identified elevated triglyceride levels as a protective factor against POAG.66–68 Additionally, lower TyG levels may co-occur with malnutrition and inadequate energy supply, particularly in individuals with chronic debilitating conditions, further compromising optic nerve perfusion.69 More importantly, body composition may also affect cerebrospinal fluid pressure (CSFP), a key factor in optic nerve head biomechanics. Previous studies have shown that individuals with lower BMI tend to have reduced intracranial pressure and CSFP.70–72 A UK Biobank study reported that lower BMI was associated with a higher cup-to-disc ratio and a 10% increased likelihood of glaucoma.72 In the Canadian Longitudinal Study of Ageing, lower BMI was associated with greater vertical cup-to-disc ratio changes.72 Mechanistically, reduced adipose tissue may lead to epidural fat pad atrophy and diminished spinal CSF reserves, as well as lower circulating leptin levels, which can impair CSF production by the choroid plexus. This may reduce CSFP, disrupting the translaminar pressure gradient across the lamina cribrosa.3,71–73 As CSFP represents the counterpressure opposing intraocular pressure (IOP), an abnormally low CSFP—even in the presence of normal IOP—can increase the translaminar pressure difference (IOP − CSFP), potentially leading to posterior bowing of the lamina, axoplasmic flow stasis, and subsequent optic nerve damage characteristic of POAG.74–78 In addition to these potential biological mechanisms, survivorship bias may also contribute to this pattern, as older individuals with very low BMI may have higher mortality before reaching the typical age for POAG detection.73

Of note, younger individuals exhibited a more pronounced association between the TyG index and the four ocular diseases. This pattern may reflect age-related increases in baseline TyG levels, or selective survival bias, whereby individuals with severe insulin resistance may have succumbed to competing risks (e.g., cardiovascular events) before developing advanced ocular pathologies.79,80 Subgroup analyses also revealed that elevated TyG index levels were more strongly associated with DR risk in female participants. This may relate to sex-specific inflammatory responses, as women with metabolic syndrome tend to exhibit higher levels of C-reactive protein and IL-6.81,82 Postmenopausal estrogen decline may also exacerbate oxidative stress in the retina, increasing susceptibility to DR.83,84 In contrast, male participants showed greater vulnerability to POAG at very low TyG levels. This may be due to estrogen's vasoregulatory effects and sex differences in fat distribution pattern, which may impact venous return and alter CSFP dynamics.85–87

Our study has several notable strengths. Leveraging a large population-based cohort, we provide novel prospective evidence that TyG-related indexes are associated with the incidence of vision-threatening ocular diseases, supported by corresponding retinal structural alterations. The integration of TyG with anthropometric measures provided measurable discriminative information for DR risk, highlighting the potential of TyG-related indexes as simple, accessible, and clinically informative markers for metabolic risk assessment. Furthermore, the identification of nonlinear associations underscores the importance of maintaining TyG levels and body composition within optimal ranges to preserve ocular health, providing actionable targets for metabolic risk modification.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relies on single-timepoint measurements of exposures. Future research should incorporate multiple measurements to capture longitudinal variations. Second, the study population consisted predominantly of British Caucasians, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Third, the biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations remain speculative and require further experimental validation. Fourth, the cross-sectional image-derived ocular phenotype analyses did not fully reproduce the statistical significance observed in the main longitudinal analyses, likely reflecting differences in outcome definition, limited case numbers, and selection into the UK Biobank retinal imaging subset; larger longitudinal ophthalmic imaging cohorts are warranted for further validation.88–91 Fifth, because risk-factor studies and prediction-model studies serve distinct purposes, our ROC analyses should be interpreted as supportive assessments of discrimination and risk stratification rather than as formal prediction-model development.39–41,64 Future prediction-model studies integrating clinical, anthropometric, metabolic, proteomic, and ocular imaging features may facilitate the development of optimized risk-prediction models for these vision-threatening ocular diseases.92,93

Conclusions

This study identified distinct, disease-specific associations between the TyG index and major vision-threatening ocular diseases, demonstrating a reverse L-shaped pattern for cataract and DR, a J-shaped curve for AMD, and a U-shaped association for POAG, suggesting an estimated optimal range from 8.6 to 9.1. Our findings underscore the potential harm associated with elevated TyG-obesity indexes, particularly in relation to DR risk. As accessible and parsimonious markers of metabolic dysfunction and adiposity, TyG-related indexes may support preliminary risk stratification and inform targeted metabolic interventions aimed at preventing vision loss.
