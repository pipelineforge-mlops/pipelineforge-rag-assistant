Summary

Background

While surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) is a cornerstone of preventing surgical site infections, adherence to clinical guidelines determines its effectiveness. In Spain, nationwide data on SAP appropriateness are scarce. This study evaluated prescribing practices across Spanish hospitals to assess alignment with national standards and identify factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic use.

Methods

We conducted a multicentre, cross-sectional, point-prevalence survey (PPS) in hospitals nationwide in Spain. Adult and paediatric patients undergoing elective or urgent surgery in 11 surgical areas were included. SAP appropriateness was assessed based on the SEIMC/AEC national consensus and local clinical guidelines, if available. The primary outcome was overall appropriateness, which was defined as adherence to the recommended indication, antimicrobial agent, dose, route of administration, infusion duration, timing of administration and total duration of SAP. Agreement between guidelines was assessed using Gwet's AC1 coefficient. A mixed-effects logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with SAP inappropriateness, accounting for institutional clustering and ensuring robust variable selection.

Findings

Among 2198 patients from 91 hospitals, overall SAP appropriateness was 31·8% (n = 699) according to national consensus, increasing to 41·3% (n = 764) when compared with local protocols. According to the national consensus, the primary cause of inappropriateness was SAP duration. Agreement between national and local guidelines was moderate (Gwet's AC1 0·68), with notable discrepancies in cardiac and thoracic surgery. Independent risk factors identified by the mixed-effects logistic regression model included the number of antimicrobial agents prescribed (OR 25·44, 95% CI 17·05–37·27; p < 0·001), and the use of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (OR 2·64, 1·77–3·90; p < 0·001), or antibiotics classified as “other” (OR 15·03, 4·95–45·60; p < 0·001).

Interpretation

SAP appropriateness in Spanish hospitals is suboptimal, with a significant gap between national evidence-based guidelines and clinical practice. These findings underscore opportunities for targeted antimicrobial stewardship interventions.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) is an effective procedure for preventing surgical site infections (SSIs). Its efficacy depends not only on the selection of an appropriate antimicrobial agent, but also on correct dosing, route, timing of administration and duration. We searched PubMed for those articles evaluating the appropriateness of SAP published from database inception up to 31st January 2026, with no language restrictions. The search strategy was (“Anti-Bacterial Agents” [MeSH Terms] OR “Antibiotic Prophylaxis” [MeSH Terms] OR “surgical prophylaxis” [Title] OR “surgical antibiotic prophylaxis” [Title] OR “surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis” [Title] OR “perioperative antibiotic∗” [Title] OR “prophylactic antibiotic∗” [Title] OR “preoperative antibiotic∗” [Title]) AND (“Guideline Adherence” [MeSH Terms] OR “compliance” [Title] OR “adherence” [Title] OR “appropriate∗” [Title] OR “inappropriate∗” [Title]). Despite existing international and national guidelines, significant variability and inappropriate use of SAP persist across hospitals and surgical specialities, as have been identified in previous studies in Spain and other countries. However, these studies have often been limited to single-centre or regional studies with heterogeneous methodologies and lacked integration with national consensus guidelines.

Added value of this study

This study is the first nationwide, multicentre, observational study in Spain that has been specifically designed to assess the appropriateness of SAP across a broad spectrum of surgical procedures and populations, including adults and children. By integrating standardised assessment criteria based on a national consensus guideline and local protocols, and by collecting detailed clinical data from 2198 patients, this study provides a robust and comprehensive evaluation of prophylaxis practices. The use of REDCap electronic data capture, multidisciplinary teams, and harmonised methodology across participating centres ensures data consistency and quality, setting a new benchmark for antimicrobial stewardship studies in surgical settings.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings from this study will help identify critical gaps and variability in SAP use across Spanish hospitals, highlighting areas for targeted interventions and optimisation strategies. The evidence generated will support local and national antimicrobial stewardship programmes in refining their protocols, improving compliance with clinical guidelines, and reducing unnecessary antimicrobial exposure. This study underscores the need for continuous monitoring and improvement efforts using standardised and evidence-based SAP.

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are among the most common healthcare-associated infections, with rates varying significantly depending on the procedure from 0·6% to 9·5%. They are associated with longer hospital stays after surgery, the need for additional surgical procedures and intensive care treatment, and higher mortality rates. To mitigate this burden, surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) before incision is recommended as a preventive measure.1 However, its effectiveness is strictly dependent on appropriateness; for instance, suboptimal timing alone has been associated with higher SSI rates.2 Inappropriate SAP, due to incorrect antibiotic selection, dosage, administration route, timing or duration, can also disrupt the patient's microbiota, increasing susceptibility to infections such as candidiasis and Clostridioides difficile infection, as well as other complications, including gastrointestinal adverse effects and antimicrobial resistance.3,4 These findings highlight the urgent need to improve SAP prescribing practices.

Recent international proposals, such as the surgical prophylaxis “global bundle”, aim to address this by promoting a structured set of five core, evidence-based interventions to standardise SAP practices worldwide and ensure responsible antibiotic use. Essential strategies include the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs), continuous professional education and monitoring, and the provision of evidence-based guidelines in operating rooms.5 Optimal adherence to SAP requires multidisciplinary collaboration among anaesthesiologists, surgeons, hospital pharmacists, microbiologists, infectious disease physicians and epidemiologists to ensure success.6

Previous studies have revealed suboptimal compliance with internationally recognised guidelines worldwide.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 A national audit in Australia carried out from 2016 to 2018 revealed high rates of non-compliance across all surgical specialities, particularly with regard to timing (44·9% of cases were administered incorrectly) and excessive duration beyond 24 h (54·3%).7 Similarly, inappropriate hospital prescribing was estimated at 30·4% in England,8 and 41% of SAP prescriptions in United States hospitals in 2019–2020 were not aligned with national guidelines.9 These results have also been observed in studies across Europe, as has been the case with France, Greece and Italy.10, 11, 12

In Spain, the National Plan against Antibiotic Resistance (PRAN), led and coordinated by the Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS), has been in operation since 2014 with the aim of reducing the risk of the selection and spread of antibiotic resistance, and consequently mitigating its impact on human and animal health, while preserving the long-term effectiveness of existing antibiotics.13 The updated PRAN 2025–2027 strategic framework emphasises strengthening infection prevention and control programmes, reinforcing ASPs across all healthcare levels, and enhancing both resistance surveillance and antibiotic consumption monitoring systems.14 However, there is a lack of evidence regarding compliance with international and national standards for SAP, with few local or regional studies investigating variation in hospital practices, particularly about prophylaxis duration and antimicrobial choice.15

In this context, the present study aims to evaluate the appropriateness of SAP prescriptions in Spanish hospitals nationwide, according to national and local guidelines, through a national point-prevalence survey (PPS). The secondary objectives were to assess the agreement between local protocols and national guidelines across different centres, and to identify factors associated with inappropriate SAP prescribing.

Methods

Study design and setting

The current research was a multicentre, cross-sectional, observational study based on patient treatment records. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies.16 The study protocol has been published elsewhere.17

Hospitals were invited to participate through the Spanish Society of Hospital Pharmacy (SEFH) and were included if they attended patients in the surgical areas described below. Investigators from the participating hospitals received the study protocol and were informed about all aspects of data collection and the relevant details of the study via teleconference. Any remaining questions were resolved via teleconference or email by the study coordinators (DJ-R and ABG-G).

The study comprised two periods of PPS data collection. The first PPS period ran from 23rd October to 3rd November 2023, and the second from 16th to 26th May 2024. The progress of this process was monitored daily via email with the principal investigators in each centre until data collection was finished.

Patients

Adult and paediatric inpatients and outpatients undergoing elective or urgent surgery were included. Inclusion criteria were patients who had surgery in the following areas: angiology and vascular surgery, cardiac surgery, general and digestive surgery, maxillofacial surgery, paediatric surgery, thoracic surgery, gynaecology and obstetrics, neurosurgery, otorhinolaryngology, traumatology (including orthopaedic surgery), and urology. Patients requiring ophthalmological, dermatological or plastic surgery, or surgery for the implantation of central vascular catheters, for which the administration of SAP by the systemic route is very rare, were excluded.

Procedures

Sample size and recruitment

In 2019, more than 2·4 million surgical procedures were performed in Spanish public hospitals, excluding ophthalmological, dermatological, and plastic surgeries. Although a sample size of 107 patients would have been sufficient to estimate an appropriateness rate of 70% with 95% confidence and ±10% precision, a larger sample size of 2102 patients was set to accommodate the multicentre, multi-level design and variability across surgical areas. This sample size provided 95% confidence and 80% power, across a minimum of 50 participating centres. Taking into account a potential data loss of 10%, a final sample size of 2335 was suggested.

The final sample size was assigned per centre to ensure the surgical areas included were representative and according to the bed capacity of the participating hospitals. In accordance with a previous nationwide study,18 hospitals were categorised into four groups according to bed capacity: fewer than 200 beds (group 1); 200–499 beds (group 2); 500–999 beds (group 3); 1000 beds or more (group 4). The proposed sample sizes were 12–15 patients for group 1, 22–25 patients for group 2, 32–35 patients for group 3, and 42–45 patients for group 4. The distribution of participating hospitals across different groups is shown in Table S1.

Before starting the PPS, the research team emailed each centre the assigned sample size and surgical area distribution. Recruitment was then based on the following conditions:• Condition 1: If the number of patients undergoing surgery on the day of the time point exceeded the number assigned to the centre, investigators should select patients chronologically based on their entry time to the operating room to avoid selection bias.

• Condition 2: If investigators did not reach the assigned number of patients on the first day of data collection, recruitment should continue for a maximum of four consecutive days from the first day of data collection onwards, following Condition 1.

Data collection and outcomes

Information was collected from medical records and entered into a pre-designed, detailed data collection sheet using REDCap software for a case report form (eCRF). The data were anonymised by assigning a specific code. The retrieved information included: age; sex; weight; height; renal function (estimated creatinine clearance (CrCl) ≥45 mL/min or <45 mL/min); history of allergy to antimicrobials; history of infection or colonisation by multidrug-resistant bacteria (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria) in the last year; surgical area/service; type of procedure (scheduled or urgent); type of surgical regimen (inpatient or outpatient); duration of surgical intervention; prophylactic antimicrobial; and adverse drug reactions documented in patient medical records and potentially related to SAP.

SAP compliance assessment

The appropriateness of SAP was evaluated according to the consensus document on antibiotic prophylaxis in surgery by the Spanish Society of Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology (SEIMC) and the Spanish Society of Surgeons (AEC).19 Additionally, those centres with local antibiotic prophylaxis guidelines performed the evaluation based on these guidelines.

Overall appropriateness was considered only if SAP complied with all recommendations regarding indication, antimicrobial choice, dose, route of administration, duration of infusion, timing of the first dose administration, second intraoperative doses (if required), and duration of antibiotic prophylaxis. For each of these items, the investigators indicated “appropriate” if the medical prescription matched the recommendation; “inappropriate” if they did not; and “not assessable” if insufficient information was available (see Table S2).

Statistical analysis

Data completeness was assessed for all variables prior to analysis. The study variables were analysed descriptively. Quantitative variables were summarised using mean, median, standard deviation (SD) and range, while qualitative variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies.

Subsequently, agreement on overall appropriateness between the SEIMC/AEC consensus and local guidelines was assessed using Gwet's AC1 coefficient, a statistic based on the probability of observed agreement corrected by the chance-expected agreement. Coefficient values close to 1 indicated a higher degree of concordance. The coefficient was defined as:AC1=Po−Pe1−Pe

Where Po represents the proportion of observed agreement and Pe the probability of chance-expected agreement. The proportion of observed agreement was calculated as:

Po=1N∑i=1knii In this formula, nii represents the number of cases where both raters assigned the same category i, N is the total number of observations, and k is the total number of categories.

The probability of chance-expected agreement was calculated as:

Pe=1k−1∑i=1kπi(1−πi) Here, πi is the average probability of assigning category i, defined as πi=pi++p+i2, where pi+ and p+i are the proportions of assignment to category i by rater 1 and rater 2, respectively.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with inappropriateness in SAP, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). To ensure the validity and robustness of the estimates, a complete case analysis approach was adopted. Cases categorised as “not assessable” due to missing core information were excluded from the multivariable analysis. Candidate variables for the multivariable model included all patient and surgical characteristics described in “Data collection and outcomes”. Conversely, any covariates with more than 5% missing data were omitted from the models, provided they were not considered clinically vital to the analysis, to prevent potential bias and ensure stable statistical adjustment. Likewise, adverse drug reactions were omitted as they represent a clinical outcome rather than a baseline predictor of SAP appropriateness. Variables with a p-value <0·1 in the univariable analysis were included in an initial multivariable model, where statistical significance was set at p-value <0·05. Due to the multicentre nature of the study, a mixed-effects logistic regression model with a random intercept at the hospital level was implemented. A variable selection procedure was first performed using a penalised mixed-effects logistic regression with Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO). For this procedure, continuous variables were centred and scaled to ensure numerical stability and facilitate model convergence. Since cross-validation is not directly implemented in this context, the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined by evaluating a sequence of values ranging from 0 to 500 (in 0·1 increments). The final λ was selected by identifying the value that minimised the Bayesian Information Criterion (λ = 123·5). Predictors with non-zero coefficients in this penalised model were subsequently included in a non-penalised mixed-effects logistic regression model (refitting) to obtain the final unbiased estimates (OR and 95% CI). Model performance was evaluated through numerical and graphical calibration. Numerical validation included the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio and Calibration-In-The-Large (CITL). CITL was used to reflect overall bias, where a value of 0 on the logit scale indicates perfect calibration. Graphical calibration was assessed by plotting predicted probabilities against observed proportions, including 95% CI and prediction intervals to account for the uncertainty derived from the hospital-level random effects. From this final adjusted model, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to institutional clustering. In both the standard multivariable and the mixed-effects models, multicollinearity among the covariates was assessed using the Generalised Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF), which allowed for comparability across the variables. This was calculated as GVIF1/(2·Df), reducing to √GVIF when Df = 1.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4·5·1), specifically utilising the glmmLasso (1·6·4) and CalibrationCurves (3·1·0) packages.

Results

Demographic and clinical data summary

A total of 2198 patients from 91 hospitals (70 public centres and 21 private or mixed-management hospitals) across 16 autonomous communities in Spain participated in the study. The geographical distribution of the participating hospitals is shown in Figure S1. Of these, 58 centres were included in the first PPS and 33 in the second. Overall data completeness was high; however, missingness was identified in: weight (n = 258, 11·7%), height (n = 374, 17·0%), duration of surgery (n = 152, 6·9%), and renal function (n = 19, 0·9%).

The characteristics of the centres, surgeries and patients are shown in Table S3. Included patients had a mean (SD) age of 57 (20) years; the youngest patient was 0 years old and the oldest was 105 years old. Mean (SD) weight and height were 74 (20) kg and 164 (13) cm, respectively. Renal function was preserved (estimated CrCl ≥45 mL/min) in most patients (94·2%), while impaired renal function (<45 mL/min) was observed in 5·8%.

The most common surgical area was traumatology (27·6%), followed by general and digestive surgery (27·4%). The results are shown in Fig. 1. The mean (SD) duration of interventions was 2 (1·4) hours, ranging from 0 to 11 h. Regarding antibiotic prophylaxis regimens, cefazolin was the most frequently administered agent (51·1%), followed by amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (21·0%). A full list of prescribed antibiotics and their frequencies can be found in Table S4. The incidence of documented adverse drug reactions potentially related to SAP was remarkably low (0·14%; n = 3). In two cases, these reactions were related to teicoplanin (nausea and vomiting), and in one case, to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (diarrhoea).Fig. 1 Distribution of the surgical areas included in the study.

Factors associated with inappropriateness

Of the 2198 patients whose prescriptions were evaluated according to the consensus document, those with “not assessable” prophylaxis were excluded, leaving 1898 patients for the final analysis. To ensure model robustness and mitigate potential bias from incomplete records, variables with missing data exceeding 5% (weight, height, and surgery duration) were excluded as covariates. For renal function, which had a minimal missingness rate of 0·9%, a conservative approach was adopted by assuming normal creatinine clearance for those 19 patients. Additionally, ampicillin, aztreonam, and tobramycin were excluded from the model due to low frequency and a lack of appropriate assessments according to the consensus document, which prevented logistic adjustment.

Univariable and multivariable analyses of factors associated with inappropriateness in SAP are detailed in Table 4. In the initial multivariable analysis, cardiac surgery (OR 12·02, 95% CI 2·09–237·27; p = 0·025), urology (OR 1·71, 1·11–2·64; p = 0·015), and the use of clindamycin (OR 17·66, 2·01–394·22; p = 0·020) or antibiotics classified as “other” (OR 11·88, 2·36–61·32; p = 0·002) were identified as independent risk factors; even though the wide CI for certain factors suggest imprecision due to smaller sample sizes in these subgroups. Estimates for continuous variables (number of antimicrobial agents) were back-transformed to their original scale to provide clinically interpretable OR per unit increase.

Given the presence of multicollinearity and to account for potential institutional clustering, a robust statistical approach was implemented using a mixed-effects logistic regression model. After penalisation and subsequent refitting, the factors selected by the model and their association with SAP inappropriateness were (Table 4): inpatient surgery (OR 1·40, 95% CI 0·98–2·01; p = 0·06), the number of antimicrobial agents prescribed (OR 25·44, 17·05–37·27; p < 0·001), and the use of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (OR 2·64, 1·77–3·90; p < 0·001), or antibiotics classified as “other” (OR 15·03, 4·95–45·60; p < 0·001). The GVIF for all selected predictors was close to 1·0, confirming the absence of multicollinearity. Numerical calibration showed an O/E ratio of 0·99, indicating a good overall calibration. The CITL was −0·01, verifying the model's robust calibration and indicating a slight overestimation of risk that is, in practical terms, negligible. These findings were visually supported by the calibration plot (Figure S4), which showed a high level of agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed proportions.

The ICC calculated from this mixed-effects model was 0·397, which indicated that approximately 39·7% of the total variance in SAP inappropriateness was attributable to differences between hospitals. Finally, the model was used to estimate the risk of SAP inappropriateness for a standard reference patient, defined as a patient with reference categories for all the included categorical variables and the mean value for the total number of antimicrobials (0·83). The analysis (Figure S5) confirmed the substantial impact of institutional practices on inappropriateness; even for this identical clinical profile, the predicted probabilities and 95% CI fluctuated markedly between centres.

Discussion

This national PPS provides a comprehensive overview of SAP practices in Spanish hospitals. Our primary finding is a low rate of overall appropriateness with the national SEIMC/AEC consensus (31·8%), which increases in the cases where the evaluation was made according to adherence to local protocols (41·3%). The primary drivers of inappropriateness were prolonged SAP duration and incorrect antimicrobial choice. Furthermore, our mixed-effects logistic regression model identified the antimicrobial choice and the number of agents used in SAP as independent risk factors for inappropriateness, highlighting a pronounced inter-hospital variability.

The overall appropriateness rate in Spain is remarkably consistent with findings from other Mediterranean and European institutions, suggesting a regional trend in SAP non-compliance. In Italy, a university hospital performing over 40,000 surgeries annually reported a baseline adherence to guidelines of 36·6% before the implementation of stewardship interventions.12 Similarly, active surveillance in seven surgical departments from three Greek hospitals documented appropriateness rates as low as 28·2% before the implementation of a stewardship intervention based on education, audit, and feedback.11 Results from Italian and Greek hospitals suggest that, without active ASPs, the “natural” rate of appropriateness in high-resource healthcare environments tends to fluctuate between 30% and 40%, and that the current Spanish baseline is not an immutable standard but rather a starting point for systematic improvement. This contrasts with the 64% overall appropriateness of SAP compliance identified in a recent multicentre study conducted in France. However, the results of this study could not be fully compared because only seven surgical procedures involving five surgical areas were audited.10

Over the years, the development and implementation of hospital guidelines and protocols outlining recommendations to improve the quality of antimicrobial prescriptions has been established as a cornerstone of ASPs,13,14 to achieve the primary goal of SAP: the prevention of SSIs and their severe consequences.1 In our study, 91·2% of participating hospitals had local protocols to guide SAP prescriptions, reflecting a high level of adoption of these recommendations and consistent with global surveys in which the majority of hospitals worldwide report having specific SAP guidance.20 Specifically, our results showed an increase of almost 10% in appropriateness when the SAP evaluation switched from the national SEIMC/AEC consensus (31·8%) to local protocols (41·3%). This finding mirrors data from a multicentre study exhibiting modest concordance between antibiotic prescription guidance documents and regional standards.21 This lack of alignment often reflects the necessity of tailoring national recommendations to local epidemiology, such as high rates of antimicrobial resistance or specific patient factors like complex surgical histories or prior infection or colonisation by multidrug-resistant bacteria,19 even though the latter was below 1·5% in our study. Nevertheless, it is also possible that, given the near dates of publication of the national consensus and this cross-sectional study, some local protocols have not yet been updated to reflect the latest evidence-based practices.

Prolonged duration of SAP was identified as the primary driver of inappropriateness. This persistence of multi-day regimens contradicts current international recommendations, which advocate for a single pre-operative dose in the vast majority of procedures.22 Compared to the results of the second European PPS 2016–2017, where 54% of SAP was prolonged beyond one day,23 and the French INCISO Surveillance Network findings, where SAP duration was considered too long in 45·2% of procedures,24 our findings suggest a more favourable trend in Spanish hospitals. In low-and middle-income countries, the situation is even more pronounced, with 75%–88·9% of patients receiving SAP for durations far exceeding the 24-h window.25 The prolongation of SAP is a major determinant of antimicrobial resistance, and evidence confirms that extending prophylaxis does not further reduce the risk of SSIs but significantly increases the risk of C. difficile infection and acute kidney injury.3 In a recent Spanish study among 723 patients, inadequate SAP, mainly inappropriate redosing and duration, was associated with an increased risk of SSIs, prolonged length of stay, and greater crude mortality.26

Inappropriate antimicrobial choice was also a reason for concern. Our results show that amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was the second most prescribed agent (21·0%), a practice that significantly deviates from most international guidelines and the SEIMC/AEC consensus, which prioritises cephalosporins without anaerobic agents in most surgeries.19 The amoxicillin-clavulanic acid selection is often justified by clinicians to cover anaerobic pathogens in abdominal surgery; however, evidence suggests that this broader spectrum does not translate into lower SSI rates in studies that compared amoxicillin-clavulanic acid versus a cephalosporin-based regimen combined with metronidazole.27 The high OR for inappropriateness associated with amoxicillin-clavulanate in our study (OR 2·64) reinforces the need for ASPs to promote cefazolin, plus metronidazole when anaerobes coverage is needed, as the primary agent for procedures to avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum use and preserve the efficacy of “Watch” and “Reserve” antibiotics.

Similarly, clindamycin was frequently used as a second-line agent in patients with reported antimicrobial allergies, primarily beta-lactam allergies. The appropriateness of antimicrobial choice in this cohort was considerably lower than in non-allergic patients (63·8% versus 78·1%). This disparity is particularly concerning because beta-lactam allergy is widely overdiagnosed, meaning that many patients labelled as allergic could safely receive first-line antibiotics.28 Recent evidence has shown that non-beta-lactam alternatives, including clindamycin and vancomycin, are significantly less effective than beta-lactam-based SAP in preventing SSIs.29 Therefore, relying on clindamycin due to unverified allergy labels not only increases the risk of non-compliance with guidelines but may also compromise clinical outcomes by denying patients appropriate SAP. These findings reinforce the need to integrate allergy de-labelling programmes into surgical ASPs as a core strategy to reduce the use of suboptimal second-line agents and enhance patient safety.

A recent systematic review concludes that antimicrobial stewardship interventions, such as audit, feedback, education, and computer-assisted decision support methodologies, appear to be effective in promoting adherence to SAP protocols, reducing SSIs with a positive economic impact.30 Due to the limited resources of ASP teams, efforts should focus on the main drivers of inappropriateness. In this sense, the strong association between inappropriateness and the number of antimicrobial agents (OR 25·44) and the prolonged duration suggest that real-time electronic prescription audits or the implementation of computer-assisted automatic stop orders (requiring active clinical justification to extend prophylaxis beyond 24 h) could be useful strategies to reduce SAP duration and optimise drug selection.31 Moreover, the substantial inter-hospital variability observed (ICC 0·397) likely reflects differences in the implementation of local protocols, the maturity of institutional ASPs, and the quality of information systems. This heterogeneity underscores the need for standardisation and benchmarking between centres. Addressing this variability requires institutional strategies that promote surgical ASP teams and the digital transformation of clinical environments. Currently, the high proportion of undocumented information (20·3% “not assessable”) indicates a systemic failure in the digitisation of surgical workflows. Improving the accuracy of surgical records is a prerequisite for any reliable surveillance system and a key pillar for future success.

The main strength of this research is its multicentre, national design involving 91 hospitals and over 2000 patients. According to the Annual Report of the National Health System published in 2024, there are 604 acute care hospitals in Spain (including public and private institutions). Our study represents approximately 15% of the total national hospital infrastructure, providing a high-resolution snapshot of real-world practice in Spain. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. The voluntary participation of centres might introduce a selection bias towards hospitals with more established ASPs, which could mean the national reality is even more challenging than reported. Additionally, the lack of data on some items (i.e., duration of infusion and the timing of the first dose) prevented a full assessment of overall appropriateness in a notable percentage of patients, highlighting a significant documentation gap in Spanish surgical services. Our methodology required the simultaneous fulfilment of all items for a prescription to be deemed “overall appropriate”. The adoption of an all-or-nothing metric is supported by healthcare quality experts as a means to raise the bar on clinical performance, moving beyond the potentially misleading high compliance rates often seen in individual indicators.32 Therefore, our results should be viewed as an audit that exposes the complexity of achieving perfect SAP prescriptions in real-world clinical practice. Regarding missing data management, variables with significant missingness (weight, height, and duration of surgery) were excluded from the multivariable adjustments. Although this decision was made to ensure that the models relied strictly on high-quality observed data, we acknowledge that a complete-case approach can introduce statistical bias and restrict statistical power compared to multiple imputation, which remains the preferred and most robust standard for handling missingness in large epidemiological datasets.33 Finally, specific clinical risk factors such as the American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) physical status or wound contamination class were not recorded, as our study was focused on evaluating the quality of the SAP prescription process rather than assessing individual SSI risk.

In conclusion, SAP prescribing practices in Spain remain largely suboptimal, characterised by excessive duration and inappropriate drug selection. Our results suggest the urgent need to implement antimicrobial stewardship interventions, improve surgical documentation, and foster a multidisciplinary culture that prioritises evidence-based prophylaxis over traditional, broad-spectrum practices.
