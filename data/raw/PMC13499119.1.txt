Background and Aims

Though topical corticosteroids have long been used for the treatment of eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), real-world data for the recently Food and Drug Administration–approved budesonide oral suspension (BOS) are lacking. We sought to determine the efficacy of BOS in a real-world cohort of EoE patients.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with EoE who were treated with BOS. Baseline characteristics, history, and procedural data were extracted from the medical record. We assessed endoscopic, histologic, and symptomatic responses for the endoscopy immediately preceding and following 12 weeks of BOS.

Results

We identified 145 EoE patients prescribed BOS at our center since 2024 (mean age 35.4 years; 60% male), of whom 111 (77%) ultimately obtained insurance approval and 94 (65%) were able to fill their prescription. Post-treatment data were available for 48 patients. Compared to before BOS treatment, post-BOS patients were more likely to achieve a histologic response at all thresholds (eg, 73% vs 19% for <15 eos/hpf; P < .001) and endoscopic response (64% vs 22% for EoE Endoscopic Reference Score ≤2; P < .001), with associated significant reductions in peak eosinophil count (15.0 ± 30.6 vs 66.7 ± 71.8; P < .001) and total EoE Endoscopic Reference Score (2.0 ± 1.9 vs 3.9 ± 2.2; P < .001). Global symptom improvement was reported in 78%.

Conclusion

In this initial real-world cohort treated with the Food and Drug Administration–approved BOS, histologic response rates reflected those seen in BOS clinical trials. Symptoms and endoscopic findings improved in parallel. Because the majority of prescriptions could be filled and significant improvement was achieved compared to prior treatments, BOS should be considered when topical steroids are selected for EoE.

Introduction

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a type 2 chronic inflammatory condition that is progressive in nature.1 It is diagnosed with typical clinical symptoms, such as dysphagia and food impaction in adolescents and adults, and with at least 15 eosinophils per high-power field (eos/hpf) on esophageal biopsy, in the absence of competing causes of eosinophilia.2,3 It has a rapidly increasing prevalence with a 5-fold increase since 2009, and approximately 1 in 700 people in the United States now have EoE.4 There is an associated high burden of disease as well, with a median annual cost per patient of $2302 with contributions from office visits, endoscopy, and pharmacy claims and more than $1 billion in overall total costs annually.4,5

Treatment strategies for EoE include pharmacologic agents, diet elimination, and esophageal dilation when strictures are present. Among medications, topical corticosteroids (tCS) are a recommended first-line therapy.3,6 For more than 2 decades, tCS have been used off-label for EoE, with asthma formulations adapted to attempt to coat the esophagus.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 While effective in some patients, this off-label use is suboptimal and has multiple limitations.13 These limitations include adherence barriers from poor palatability and difficulty properly mixing viscous budesonide, as well as non-standardized formulations leading to variable dosing and administration methods.14,15 Because of this, efforts have been undertaken to develop esophageal-specific formulations of tCS,16 resulting in a variety of applications and formulations.17, 18, 19 In 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved budesonide oral suspension (BOS; brand name “Eohilia”) for patients 11 and older with EoE, the first topical steroid approved in the United States.20,21

In its clinical trial program, BOS demonstrated meaningful improvement across symptom and histologic endpoints, in addition to endoscopic findings, both in the short term (which led to the 12-week FDA approval) and with emerging longer-term data.20,22, 23, 24, 25, 26 However, data on BOS are currently limited to the clinical trial setting, and real-world data are lacking. As such, we aimed to determine the efficacy of BOS in a real-world cohort of EoE patients.

Methods

Patient Cohort and Data Sources

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with EoE who were prescribed BOS at the University of North Carolina (UNC) from 2024 (the year in which BOS was approved) through 2025 using the UNC EoE Clinicopathologic database. The details of this database have been described previously.27, 28, 29, 30, 31 All cases were diagnosed according to consensus guidelines,2 either as incident cases at UNC or as prevalent cases referred to our esophageal center. In order to determine the subset of patients with EoE who had a prescription for BOS, we performed both a manual search in the medical record and used a natural language processing keyword search that is embedded in the data warehouse for our medical record. For this, we used the search terms “budesonide oral suspension” and “Eohilia,” and received a list of any EoE patients who had any mention of these terms. For all positive results, chart review was performed to confirm that BOS was actually prescribed, rather than the medication just being mentioned as a therapeutic consideration in a note. We assessed all EoE patients with any BOS prescription over the study time frame with no exclusions.

After cohort confirmation, demographics, presenting symptoms at the time of diagnosis, prior treatments, endoscopic findings, and disease severity (Index of Severity for EoE [I-SEE]30,32,33) were extracted from the electronic medical record using standardized case report forms. Among patients with a BOS prescription, we also evaluated insurance approval, prescription fill rates, dosing, and indications for BOS initiation. Patients were either prescribed twice-daily dosing (the FDA-approved dose) or once-daily dosing (a dose based on prior clinical experience and published data29,34) based on provider discretion. As this study reflects real-world data, we reported the observed prescribing patterns and note that a subset of patients starting on BOS were already receiving 2 mg of budesonide once daily and therefore were continued on the same dose regimen. For this real-world cohort, we directed our attention to the endoscopy that immediately preceded BOS treatment as well as the post-BOS follow-up endoscopy (after 12 weeks of treatment) and extracted pertinent endoscopic findings, including the EoE Endoscopic Reference Score (EREFS) when available,35 and histologic findings. No patients were on concomitant diet elimination therapy, systemic steroids, or biologics at the time of either the baseline or follow-up endoscopy.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

Outcomes were assessed in 3 domains: histologic response, endoscopic response, and global symptom response.36 Histologic response was defined as <15 eosinophils per high-powered field (eos/hpf),37,38 with additional thresholds of ≤6 and <1 eos/hpf assessed, and the peak eosinophil count (eos/hpf) was recorded. The peak eosinophil count (eos/hpf; hpf size = 0.24 mm2) was defined as the highest value from any esophageal level, with standard biopsy practice at our center consisting of at least 6 total biopsies obtained from a minimum of 2 esophageal levels, as per guideline recommendation.3,39 Endoscopic severity was evaluated using EREFS, with a scale of 0 being normal and 9 being most severe (edema scored as 0/1, rings as 0/1/2/3, exudates as 0/1/2, furrows scored as 0/1/2, and stricture as 0/1).40,41 An EREFS score of 2 or less was defined as an endoscopic response to treatment.42,43 The need for dilation was recorded, as was the presence of any esophageal candidiasis as documented by endoscopy or histologic exam. Additionally, a binary yes/no was obtained from the endoscopist with regard to global endoscopic improvement. We also collected global symptomatic response, defined as patient-reported overall improvement, as documented from the patient’s medical record during subsequent clinical encounters. This is a metric we have previously used given that validated patient-reported outcomes are not used due to feasibility issues in routine practice at our center.12,44 Finally, I-SEE was assessed as an outcome.30,32,33

For analysis, we used descriptive statistics to summarize characteristics of the overall cohort. We compared pre- and post-BOS treatment with paired t-tests for continuous variables and with McNemar’s test for categorical variables. Analysis was performed with Stata version 12 (College Station, TX). This study was approved by the UNC Institutional Review Board (#13–2623).

Compared to pre-BOS treatment, post-BOS patients were more likely to achieve a histologic response at all thresholds (eg, 73% vs 19% for <15 eos/hpf, P < .001; and 67% vs 15% for ≤6 eos/hpf, P < .001) with associated significant reductions in peak eosinophil count (15.0 ± 30.6 vs 66.7 ± 71.8; P < .001) (Table 3). When stratified by reason for initiation of BOS, histologic response (<15 eos/hpf) was observed in 77% of patients newly starting a tCS, 79% of those transitioning from a prior tCS therapy for convenience, and 43% of those with prior tCS treatment failure (P = .15). When stratified by dosing frequency (2 mg twice daily vs 2 mg daily), there were no significant differences between the dosing groups in terms of baseline fibrostenotic features, endoscopic severity, eosinophil counts, or disease severity (I-SEE). Similarly, there were no significant differences across the range of outcomes we assessed for this study (Table 4). Additionally, after the cohort was stratified by histologic response to BOS, there were similarly no differences detected aside from dilation at baseline being associated with histologic non-response (Table 5).

Endoscopic response improved in tandem with BOS treatment (64% vs 22% achieved EREFS ≤2; P < .001) as well as decreased total EREFS (2.0 ± 1.9 vs 3.9 ± 2.2; P < .001). There was no statistically significant difference in the rates of stricture or need for dilation with BOS treatment. When further subdivided, both the inflammatory (0.8 ± 1.3 vs 2.4 ± 1.5; P < .001) and fibrostenotic (1.1 ± 0.9 vs 1.5 ± 1.2; P = .006) subscores of the EREFS improved with treatment. Global symptom response was reported by 78% of patients, and endoscopist-assessed response was noted in 75% of procedures. No esophageal candidiasis was observed with BOS use (Table 3), and no other side effects were reported during this 12-week period.

Discussion

Off-label tCS have been a mainstay of EoE treatment, but the first FDA-approved topical steroid BOS has only recently become available for use in the United States based on clinical trial data.20 The purpose of this study was to evaluate the accessibility and efficacy of BOS in a real-world cohort outside the controlled clinical trial setting. Although randomized clinical trials remain the gold standard for drug approval, they have some limitations, particularly with respect to external validity, and real-world evidence can provide complementary insights into broader patient populations and access-related barriers that are common in routine clinical practice.45 Our data demonstrate that the efficacy of BOS in a real-world cohort of EoE patients reflects Phase 3 clinical trial data,20 particularly for histologic and endoscopic outcomes. Notably, the response rates were observed despite the majority of patients receiving a 2 mg daily dose, which was a clinician-driven choice and is lower than the twice-daily FDA-approved dose, potentially informing future dosing strategies.29 Furthermore, despite insurance-related barriers, most patients obtained medication approval after prior authorization, and among those approved, the majority were able to fill the prescription.

BOS was FDA-approved based on data from a phase 3, multicenter, randomized clinical trial.20 It enrolled patients ages 11–55 and randomized them to either BOS 2 mg twice daily vs placebo for 12 weeks with coprimary endpoints being stringent histologic response (≤6 eos/hpf) and symptomatic response (30% reduction or more in the Dysphagia Symptom Questionnaire score46). On intention-to-treat analysis, 53% of patients achieved stringent histologic response with BOS vs 1% with placebo, 53% also achieved the symptom response threshold with BOS vs 39% with placebo (a significant and meaningful decrease). These were also associated with a marked reduction in endoscopic severity measured by EREFS. These data also were consistent with the results of the smaller phase 2 study of BOS.24 Comparatively, our results demonstrate similar efficacy across histologic outcomes, with 67% reaching a stringent histologic response alongside significant reductions in peak eosinophil counts and EREFS. Although validated patient-reported outcomes were unavailable in this retrospective study, we observed a global symptom improvement, which should be interpreted with caution in this real-world cohort, as these findings are not directly comparable to Dysphagia Symptom Questionnaire trial data.

Prior to the FDA approval of BOS in 2024, off-label use of tCS was the only option for this medication class in the United States for EoE,6 as the budesonide orodispersible tablet was not available.17 Both swallowed fluticasone (from an asthma inhaler) and a mixed slurry of oral viscous budesonide had been shown to be effective.9,10,47,48 A comparative efficacy study of these medications found both to significantly improve histological response (<15 eos/hpf; 71% in oral viscous budesonide vs 64% in fluticasone), symptoms, and endoscopic severity.49 These are similar response rates to what we determined with BOS in a real-world setting. Although direct evidence is not yet available, BOS may theoretically reduce barriers to medication adherence because it is a premixed and standardized dose formulation that is palatable and portable—factors previously cited as limitations with prior off-label formulations.14,15 It is possible that for these reasons we observed improvement in histologic response with BOS in groups previously treated unsuccessfully with off-label tCS.

Strengths of our study include this being the first real-world clinical data describing BOS, a comprehensive search of prescriptions and medical records yielded a reasonable sample size, paired pre/post endoscopies, and use of selected validated outcomes. Furthermore, multiple assessments were evaluated for each patient (histologic thresholds, EREFS, and I-SEE), all of which are externally validated and widely accepted. Although this study provides valuable insights, there are inherent limitations that warrant consideration. First, this is a retrospective cohort study conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit generalizability to other populations or clinical settings, particularly given that tertiary centers tend to manage patients with more severe or refractory disease. The retrospective design limits control of confounders, may not capture side effects not assessed in our health system, and relies on data collected for routine clinical purposes rather than research. However, the cohort design, granular patient data, and standardized data extraction, including a rigorous manual review process and confirmation of prescriptions, help to mitigate these biases. Additionally, to specifically address possible confounders in dosing heterogeneity and disease responsiveness, we stratified the cohort by dosing frequency and histologic response and found no difference between baseline disease features, endoscopic severity, eosinophil counts, or disease severity aside from dilation at baseline, which is a known predictor of histologic non-response.50 Another limitation is the short duration of follow-up, as the recent FDA approval of BOS restricted assessment to initial treatment response and resulted in limited availability of BOS post-treatment data. Because not all approved or filled prescriptions had follow-up endoscopy, it is important to acknowledge potential attrition and whether or not this may contribute to selection bias, as patients without follow-up may differ from those with complete data. However, from our analysis, we observed no significant differences in disease characteristics, insurance access, or demographics between those with and without follow-up endoscopy, which suggests that selection bias may not be prominent in this cohort. Separately, given the multiple endpoints evaluated without adjustment for multiple comparisons, findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in future studies prioritizing real-world, long-term effectiveness and safety of BOS. Lastly, as noted above, non-standardization of symptom reporting is an important limitation of our study. However, the high symptom response rate reported by patients may be useful to be able to convey from a real-world setting.12,44

Conclusion

In this real-world cohort treated with the FDA-approved BOS, histologic response rates generally reflected those seen in BOS clinical trials, despite variability in prescribing and dosing patterns. Symptoms and endoscopic findings improved in parallel to the histologic response. Because the majority of prescriptions could be filled and significant improvement was achieved compared to prior treatments, BOS should be considered when topical steroids are selected for EoE.
