Background

Optimizing chemotherapy for elderly patients with extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) represents a significant clinical challenge, requiring a balance between effective relative dose intensity (RDI) and treatment tolerance. The prognostic impact of RDI in this population remains unclear.

Objectives

To investigate the relationship between RDI and survival outcomes and to establish a clinically meaningful RDI threshold for elderly ENKTL patients.

Design

A retrospective observational single-center cohort study.

Methods

The study analyzed treatment-naïve ENKTL patients aged ≥60 years who received DDGP-like regimens or P-GEMOX regimen between May 2016 and December 2023. The optimal RDI cutoff was determined by receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Variable selection for multivariable Cox regression was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to mitigate overfitting and adjust for potential confounders. The primary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).

Results

A total of 79 patients were enrolled. The median number of chemotherapy cycles was 3 (IQR: 3-5), with a median treatment interval of 26 days (IQR: 23–31). Treatment delays exceeding one week occurred in 55.7% of patients (n = 44), and only 2.5% (n = 2) received full dose intensity (RDI = 1). An average RDI ≥ 0.62 was significantly associated with prolonged PFS (p = 0.010) and OS (p = 0.012). Multivariable analysis confirmed higher RDI as an independent predictor of superior PFS (HR = 0.385, 95% CI: 0.186-0.801, p = 0.011) and OS (HR = 0.433, 95% CI: 0.194-0.968, p = 0.041). Furthermore, age≥70 years (vs.60-70 years; p = 0.007), diabetes (p = 0.039), and adverse events including infection (p = 0.033), rash (p = 0.043), and diarrhea (p = 0.017) were significant predictors of reduced RDI.

Conclusion

An ARDI ≥ 0.62 may represent a critical threshold associated with improved prognosis in elderly ENKTL. However, this observation is preliminary and warrants further validation in prospective studies.

Introduction

Extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) is a subtype of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated non-Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for less than 2% of all T-cell lymphomas. 1 This disease exhibits distinct epidemiological characteristics with marked geographical heterogeneity, being predominantly prevalent in East Asia and Latin America, while remaining rare in Western countries. 2 ENKTL is characterized by highly invasive and locally destructive behavior. It is categorized based on the site of primary tumor as nasal (∼80%) and non-nasal (∼20%) clinical subtypes. 3 The nasal type primarily involves the nasal cavity and nasopharynx, often extending to adjacent structures of the upper aerodigestive tract (UADT), such as the oropharynx and Waldeyer’s ring. The non-nasal type presents outside the nasal area, with common sites including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and testes. 4 For patients diagnosed at an early stage, combined chemoradiotherapy can achieve a five-year overall survival rate of approximately 70%; however, once the disease progresses to advanced stages or relapses, the survival rate dramatically declines to below 40%.1,5 In recent years, the incidence of ENKTL has shown an upward trend, particularly among the elderly population.6,7 Notably, older patients exhibit significantly poorer outcomes, highlighting a critical unmet need in the clinical management of ENKTL. 8

The clinical management of elderly patients presents dual challenges. On one hand, aging is associated with progressive physiological deterioration, including impaired gastrointestinal mucosal barrier function, reduced hepatic and renal metabolic capacity, and diminished bone marrow hematopoietic reserve, all of which significantly compromise the metabolism and tolerance of chemotherapeutic agents.7,9,10 On the other hand, common comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and coronary artery disease further complicate treatment decision-making. 11 Studies have demonstrated that elderly ENKTL patients experience significantly higher risks of fatal infections and acute toxicities when treated with standard-intensity chemotherapy compared to their younger counterparts.12,13 Consequently, treatment strategies in clinical practice often become more conservative for this population, with physicians frequently resorting to empirical dose adjustments or reductions. However, inadequate chemotherapy dosing or a decrease in relative dose intensity (RDI) may substantially increase the risk of disease progression and relapse. 14

Against this background, RDI, a key parameter reflecting chemotherapy delivery intensity, has been recognized as closely associated with long-term outcomes.15–17 Previous research has indicated that an optimal RDI threshold of approximately 70% is associated with favorable prognosis in patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma. 18 However, current investigations on RDI have predominantly focused on diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL).19–21 In ENKTL, the independent prognostic significance of RDI remains controversial. This study aims to systematically evaluate the association between chemotherapy RDI and survival outcomes in elderly ENKTL patients by integrating multidimensional real-world clinical data.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective observational single-center cohort study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. We reviewed the medical records of patients diagnosed with ENKTL between May 2016 and December 2023. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 22

Patients

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) newly diagnosed patients; (2) age ≥ 60 years; (3) diagnosis of ENKTL confirmed through pathological tissue biopsy and/or immunohistochemical staining, in accordance with the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lymphoid neoplasms; (4) receipt of at least two cycles of either a DDGP (pegaspargase, gemcitabine, cisplatin and dexamethasone)-like regimen or the P-GEMOX (pegaspargase, gemcitabine and oxaliplatin) regimen.23,24 All consecutive patients who met these criteria within the specified study period were included. Follow-up was conducted through chart reviews, outpatient visits, and telephone visits, with the final follow-up completed in December 2024.

Clinical variables

Collected clinical parameters included gender, age, height, weight, B symptoms, hemophagocytic syndrome (HLH), primary site, immunohistochemical markers, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), 25 Chinese Southwest Oncology Group and Asia Lymphoma Study Group (CA) staging, 26 prognostic index of natural killer lymphoma (PINK) score, 27 lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, β2-microglobulin (β2-MG), EBV DNA copy number, peritumoral invasion (PTI), regional lymph node involvement (ie, invasion of a lymph-node region corresponding to primary extranodal sites), distant lymph node involvement (ie, the presence of enlarged lymph nodes beyond regional lymph nodes), comorbidities, number of chemotherapy cycles, treatment intervals, and adverse events.

Treatment and formulas

The prescription of the standard dose was described as follows, which was repeated every 3 weeks for a total of 6 cycles: pegaspargase (PEG-Asp) 2500 IU/m2, intravenously (i.v.) on day 1; cisplatin 20 mg/m2, i.v. on days 1–4 (or oxaliplatin 130 mg/m2, i.v. on day 1); and gemcitabine (GEM) 800 mg/m2, i.v. on days 1 and 8. The initial doses of the first cycle were determined by clinicians based on a comprehensive assessment of each patient. The subsequent actual doses and the number of chemotherapy cycles were adjusted according to adverse drug events and clinical responses. The RDI of the chemotherapy regimens was calculated as follows: 28 DI=Planned dose per cycle (mg·m‐2)planned weeks per cycle

DI: dose intensity; ADI: actual dose intensity; RDI: relative dose intensity; RDI drug: the RDI of each individual chemotherapy drug; ARDI (average RDI): the average RDI for the chemotherapy regimen.

The final chemotherapy cycle was excluded from the RDI calculation for the following reasons: 1) The endpoint of the last chemotherapy cycle, such as hematologic recovery, could not be accurately determined; 2) No significant correlation was observed between RDI and the number of chemotherapy cycles. In this study, we considered Average Relative Dose Intensity (ARDI) for three chemotherapeutic agents: PEG-Asp, platinum-based agents (cisplatin or oxaliplatin), and GEM. Dexamethasone was not included in the calculation because there was no significant outcome difference between the DDGP and P-GEMOX regimens. Moreover, chemotherapy-related toxicity was primarily attributed to cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, while dexamethasone served a supportive anti-inflammatory and anti-edematous effects, with its administration being highly variable in clinical practice.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Secondary endpoints included objective remission rate (ORR) and adverse events (AEs). PFS was defined as the time from the diagnosis date to the first documented disease progression, death from any cause, or the last follow-up. OS was defined as the time from the diagnosis date to death from any cause or the last follow-up. AEs were evaluated using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5). Response assessment was evaluated by the Lugano criteria with 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) position emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) or CT.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) based on their distribution, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Correlations were analyzed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients. The cut-off value for RDI was estimated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Missing numerical and categorical data were imputed using the median and mode, respectively. Potential multiplicative interactions between ARDI and CA staging or radiotherapy were evaluated using the Wald test. For multivariable analysis using the Cox proportional hazards regression model, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was employed for variable selection to mitigate overfitting, and clinically key variables (ARDI, CA staging, and radiotherapy) were forced into the final multivariable Cox model. To evaluate the potential impact of baseline characteristics and adverse events on ARDI, the Chi-square test was performed to determine statistically significant influencing factors. Analyses were performed using R (v4.4.2) and SPSS (v27.0), with a two-sided P-value <0.05 considered significant.

Survival analysis

The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 45.267 months (95%CI: 41.775-48.759). Stratified analysis based on ARDI quartiles showed no statistically significant difference in survival between dose groups; however, a clinically relevant trend toward worse PFS (p = 0.067) and OS (p = 0.061) was observed in the low RDI group (Figure 3). Based on the ROC curve analysis for PFS, the cut-off value for ARDI was defined as 0.62, with an AUC of 0.634 (95% CI: 0.510–0.759). Kaplan-Meier analysis using the predefined ARDI cutoff (0.62) revealed statistically significant differences between the high and low ARDI groups in both PFS (p = 0.010) and OS (p = 0.012) (Figure 3). The median PFS was 19.367 months (95% CI: 6.211–32.522) in the low-ARDI group but was not reached in the high-ARDI group. Similarly, the median OS was 43.633 months (95% CI: 13.051–74.216) in the low-ARDI group, compared with not reached in the high-ARDI group. No significant interaction effects on PFS were observed between ARDI and CA staging (p = 0.835) or radiotherapy (p = 0.657). Discussion

L-Asparaginase-based combination chemotherapy is the cornerstone of ENKTL treatment. 29 Distinct from B-cell lymphoma subtypes such as DLBCL, management of ENKTL more frequently involves concurrent chemoradiotherapy, complicating the independent prognostic evaluation of RDI in this disease. 30 Current chemotherapeutic regimens commonly used for ENKTL include non-CHOP-based protocols such as DDGP regimen and P-GEMOX regimen, which employ mechanistically distinct drug combinations to counter the multidrug resistance characteristic of ENKTL.23,24,31 As an antimetabolic agent, PEG-Asp exerts its antitumor effect by depleting extracellular asparagine and inhibiting glutamine-dependent tumor cell growth.32,33 GEM, a nucleoside analog, disrupts DNA synthesis and induces apoptosis, demonstrating favorable antitumor activity in NK/T-cell lymphoma cells. 34 Platinum agents exert cytotoxic effects primarily by forming DNA cross-links that interfere with cellular replication. 35 Although mechanistically similar to cisplatin, oxaliplatin does not exhibit cross-resistance with cisplatin or carboplatin. 36 Moreover, oxaliplatin exhibits a milder toxicity profile, making it a preferable option for elderly patients and those with hepatic or renal impairment. 37

Our study confirmed that elderly patients with ENKTL generally receive insufficient chemotherapy dose intensity. It is noteworthy that RDI is not necessarily indicative of initial treatment response, but rather serves as a robust indicator of long-term prognosis. After adjusting for confounding variables, RDI remained an independent prognostic factor for both PFS and OS. These results highlight the critical importance of adhering to scheduled chemotherapy administration to maintain adequate relative dose intensity—a factor strongly associated with improved survival outcomes. Although active treatment significantly reduces mortality in elderly ENKTL patients and age should not be an absolute contraindication, 38 caution remains warranted in specific populations. Previous studies reported no significant survival benefit with higher RDI in very elderly patients (≥80 years). 39 In these cases, aggressive attempts to maintain dose intensity may lead to premature treatment discontinuation without yielding meaningful clinical benefits. Thus, for this specific population, treatment optimization should be guided by individualized patient characteristics rather than a uniform emphasis on maintaining high dose intensity.

Deviations from the optimal RDI are primarily attributed to dose reductions, treatment delays, or premature discontinuation due to adverse events. 15 Performance status and comorbidities play a pivotal role in determining chemotherapy tolerance and prognosis in elderly patients.40,41 Previous studies have identified ECOG PS as a contributing factor to reduced RDI. 42 In elderly patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma along with multiple comorbidities, chemotherapy doses were typically reduced by 10–15% compared to standard regimens. 40 However, our study did not observe a significant reduction in RDI among patients with ECOG PS ≥2 or multiple comorbidities, possibly due to sample size limitations or insufficient statistical power. Chemotherapy may not be an appropriate option for frail patients with severely impaired functional status. 43 Bone marrow suppression remains a major dose-limiting toxicity of chemotherapy.44,45 Nonetheless, our findings did not establish a significant association between bone marrow suppression and RDI, which may be attributed to the widespread use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), potentially mitigating the impact of bone marrow suppression on RDI.43,46 The study identified age ≥70 years, diabetes, and AEs (including infections, rash, and diarrhea) as independent risk factors associated with ARDI.

Although a study conducted by the Southwest Oncology Group demonstrated that elderly patients receiving full-dose chemotherapy achieved CR rates and experienced treatment-related adverse events comparable to those of younger patients, 47 implementing high-dose intensity regimens in this population remains challenging in real-world clinical practice. As demonstrated in our study, most elderly patients developed severe adverse events. If dose intensity consistently remains below this threshold during the early stages of treatment, it may serve as a clinically significant signal that standard first-line chemotherapy is no longer tolerable or beneficial. In such cases, a timely re-evaluation of the treatment strategy is necessary. In recent years, chemotherapy-free regimens have emerged as a promising alternative. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, have shown remarkable efficacy in relapsed or refractory NK/T-cell lymphoma, with a relatively favorable safety profile.48,49 Moreover, targeted therapies, including anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugates and histone deacetylase inhibitors, have expanded the treatment landscape for these patients.50,51 These non-chemotherapy approaches not only reduce chemotherapy-related toxicity but also offer greater flexibility in dose adjustment and treatment planning, representing a viable treatment option for elderly or frail patients.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size and the retrospective nature of analysis may limit the reliability and generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the stringent inclusion criteria further restricted the number of eligible participants, which may have introduced selection bias. As a result, larger, prospective studies are warranted to validate these observations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, maintaining chemotherapy RDI ≥ 62% may be associated with improved survival outcomes in elderly patients with ENKTL. Further prospective, multi-center studies are warranted to validate the RDI threshold and facilitate its integration into clinical guidance.
