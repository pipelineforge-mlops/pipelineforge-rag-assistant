Introduction

Inconsistent wound assessment and inappropriate dressing selection remain major barriers in chronic diabetic wound management. Digital decision-support systems offer a practical approach for standardizing clinical processes and supporting evidence-based care.

Objective

The aims of this study were to develop and evaluate a mobile application that integrates wound severity detection with modern dressing selection to support standardized chronic diabetic wound management.

Methods

A research and development design was used with the Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation (ADDIE) model. The application integrated the Perdana Kusuma II wound severity scoring system with a moisture-based dressing recommendation algorithm. Content validity was evaluated by 10 multidisciplinary experts using a four-point Likert scale. Usability was assessed by 12 wound care nurses with the System Usability Scale (SUS). A descriptive pre-test and post-test evaluation examined the performance in wound color identification, inflammation assessment, exudate evaluation, and dressing selection among new graduate and independent wound care nurses.

Results

All expert validation items achieved the maximum score (4.00), with item-content validity and scale-content validity indexes of 1.00. The application exhibited good usability based on the SUS interpretation. Among the new graduate nurses, the mean pre-test scores increased from 0.92 to 1.42 to 1.92–2.00 across all domains. Independent wound care nurses had consistently high baseline scores (1.58–2.00) and near-maximum post-test values. Automated wound severity outputs were comparable to the manual assessments, and dressing recommendations were aligned with modern wound care standards.

Conclusion

The mobile application developed in this study is a feasible digital clinical decision-support tool with the potential to support standardized wound assessment and evidence-informed dressing selection, particularly for early-career nurses, while maintaining expert-level clinical accuracy.

Introduction

Chronic diabetic wounds remain a major clinical challenge due to the impaired wound healing mechanisms associated with hyperglycemia, neuropathy, and vascular insufficiency. These conditions increase the risk of infection, delayed healing, and lower-extremity amputation, thereby contributing significantly to morbidity, reduced quality of life, and healthcare costs.1, 2, 3 Effective wound management in patients with diabetes requires accurate wound severity assessment and appropriate dressing selection based on wound characteristics, such as tissue condition, exudate level, and signs of inflammation or infection.4,5

In clinical practice, wound assessment and dressing selection are highly dependent on the clinician's experience and subjective judgment.6,7 Variations in knowledge, skill level, and clinical interpretation among practitioners may lead to inconsistent wound classification and suboptimal dressing choices, possibly compromising the standardization of wound care and delaying the healing process. Validated wound assessment tools are available but their application in daily practice is often limited by time constraints, lack of structured guidance, and the absence of practical decision-support mechanisms that can be used efficiently at the point of care.7, 8, 9

Advances in mobile health technology provide opportunities to support clinical practice through digital applications that facilitate systematic assessment and evidence-informed decision-making.10 Mobile applications enable real-time data input, structured evaluation, and immediate feedback, making them particularly suitable for wound care settings where timely and accurate clinical decisions are required.11,12 However, many existing wound care applications are primarily focused on documentation or image-based measurement, and do not integrate structured wound severity assessment with practical guidance for dressing selection. Consequently, practitioners may still rely on manual interpretation and fragmented information when determining appropriate wound management strategies.13,14

To address these issues, in the present study, a mobile application was developed that integrates wound severity detection with modern dressing selection within a structured clinical workflow. In contrast to existing applications that primarily support documentation or isolated assessment, the proposed system combines a validated wound severity scoring system with an algorithm-based dressing recommendation, enabling users to move directly from assessment to evidence-informed intervention. This integrated approach reduces subjectivity, improves consistency in clinical decision-making, and provides a more comprehensive form of digital clinical decision support for wound care.

Objectives

The aims of this study were to develop and evaluate a mobile application that integrates wound severity detection with modern dressing selection to support standardized chronic diabetic wound management.

Materials and Methods

Study design

A research and development design was employed using the Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation (ADDIE) model15 to develop and evaluate a mobile application for wound severity assessment and modern dressing selection. The focus was on producing a functional clinical decision support tool and assessing its feasibility, usability, and preliminary effectiveness among nursing users with different levels of clinical experience to determine whether the application could support consistent clinical decision-making across varying levels of expertise. This study was conducted in Jawa Barat Province, Indonesia, from January to August 2025.

Analysis phase

The analysis phase was conducted to identify clinical, instructional, and technical requirements for application development. This phase comprised a literature review, instructional needs analysis, and field study.16 The literature review was conducted to identify standardized wound assessment methods and evidence-based dressing selection principles. Relevant articles were retrieved from databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using keywords including “wound assessment,” “chronic wound management,” “modern dressing,” and “mobile health application.” The review focused on publications from the past 5–10 years, supplemented with key foundational studies, to ensure the inclusion of current clinical practices and technological developments.

Instructional needs analysis was conducted using purposive sampling of wound care practitioners, including nurses involved in routine wound management. Participants were selected based on their experience in clinical wound care to ensure relevance to real-world practice. Data were collected through direct observation and informal discussions supported by structured observation checklists and field notes. The analysis focused on identifying workflow challenges, decision-making processes, and difficulties encountered during wound assessment and dressing selection.

The field study was conducted to examine real-world clinical conditions and the feasibility of the application. Observations were made in clinical settings to assess device availability, Internet accessibility, and environmental constraints. Data collection methods included non-participant observation, structured checklists, and documentation of field notes. The collected data were analyzed descriptively to inform the design of the application, workflow structure, and technical specifications.

Design phase

The design phase focused on translating the findings obtained from the analysis into a comprehensive application blueprint,17,18 including designing the application workflow, user interface structure, decision algorithms, and database architecture. The workflow was structured to support systematic wound assessment, beginning with patient data entry, wound image capture, wound severity assessment using the Perdanakusuma II scoring system, automated score calculation, and moisture-based dressing recommendations, followed by longitudinal wound monitoring. The user interface was designed to prioritize simplicity and intuitive navigation, while the decision algorithms integrated wound severity scoring with moisture-based dressing selection principles. A secure database structure was designed to store patient demographics, wound images, assessment results, and wound progression data.

Development phase

During the development phase, a functional mobile application prototype was constructed in accordance with the predefined system design, workflow, and decision algorithms.19,20 The application incorporated structured wound assessment input fields and an automated logic system to generate wound severity classifications using the Perdanakusuma II score and provide corresponding modern dressing recommendations. The algorithms operate using a rule-based, deterministic approach, where predefined clinical criteria are translated into structured decision rules to generate consistent and reproducible outputs. The term “moisture-based dressing recommendation” refers to a clinical decision approach based on the principle of maintaining an optimal moist wound environment to support wound healing. In this study, the role of the recommendation algorithm was to match wound characteristics, particularly the exudate level and tissue condition, with appropriate dressing types that either retain moisture in dry wounds or absorb excess exudate in highly exudative wounds, thereby maintaining moisture balance in accordance with modern wound care principles.21

The Perdanakusuma II score, developed in 2024 by Prof. David S. Perdanakusuma, is a standardized instrument designed to assess chronic wounds objectively across three core dimensions: exudate, wound tissue characteristics, and signs of infection, each rated using predefined categorical scales.22 The instrument has been validated within its original clinical context but broader international validation is still required to establish its generalizability across diverse healthcare settings. The Perdanakusuma II scoring module was embedded within the application as a rule-based algorithm, where user-inputted wound parameters were processed locally to generate a severity score and classification.

The application included key features such as structured wound data input, wound image capture, automated wound severity scoring, and evidence-based dressing recommendations. The image capture function utilized the device's built-in camera, supporting standard smartphone imaging capabilities for adequate wound visualization in clinical settings. The dressing recommendations were predefined within the application and not dynamically adjusted based on the availability of dressings in specific healthcare settings. The workflow consisted of data entry, algorithm-based processing using the Perdanakusuma II scoring system, and real-time output in the form of wound classification and dressing suggestions. The application was designed to function primarily offline, allowing core features to operate without continuous Internet connectivity. The system was developed using the Flutter framework, enabling cross-platform mobile development and efficient performance with a user-friendly interface suitable for clinical use.

Patient data were stored locally within the device using a structured internal database. The storage capacity depended on the device's available memory and was sufficient for routine clinical use, including storing wound images and patient records. Patient data could be stored for as long as the application remained installed and sufficient device storage was available because automatic deletion was not implemented. The number of patient records that could be stored depended on the device's memory capacity and was not limited by a predefined maximum within the application.

To evaluate the content validity and overall application quality, expert validation was conducted using a structured validation questionnaire.23,24 The validation instrument was developed based on previously published frameworks for mobile health application evaluation and content validity assessment, and was adapted to the context of wound care. The instrument assessed five key domains: content feasibility, interface and visual quality, usability, clinical relevance, and overall applicability. In total, 10 statements were rated using a four-point Likert scale. The evaluation results were used to determine the application's level of feasibility and identify necessary revisions based on the mean scores obtained in each assessment domain.

Content validation involved a panel of 10 experts comprising certified wound care nurse specialists, registered nurses, nurse practitioners, and nursing academics at the senior lecturer and associate professor levels. Experts were selected using purposive sampling based on predefined criteria, including professional certification in wound care, clinical experience, and academic expertise. All experts were independent of the application development process and not involved in system design or programming. Several experts contributed extensive clinical experience of independent wound care practices, whereas others provided academic perspectives on nursing education and research. This multidisciplinary expertise facilitated comprehensive assessment of the content accuracy, clinical relevance, usability, and innovation, and informed targeted revisions to enhance the application's readiness for implementation.

Implementation phase

In the implementation phase, the application's functionality, usability, and clinical applicability were assessed by the target users.20 In total, 12 wound care nurses were recruited because this phase represented a formative usability evaluation within the ADDIE development process rather than a hypothesis-testing study. Previous usability research has shown that relatively small samples are appropriate for identifying major usability issues and evaluating system functionality during early-stage application development.25 Usability was evaluated using the System Usability Scale (SUS), which is a standardized instrument developed by John Brooke to measure perceived system usability based on 10 statements rated on a five-point Likert scale. SUS captures user experience, ease of use, and overall satisfaction, and generates a composite usability score that can be interpreted within established usability categories.15,24 In the present study, automated wound severity outputs were also compared with manual assessments. This phase was followed by a limited field trial across different clinical settings to examine the stability of the application and its integration into routine practice.

The application was designed by attending to data security and patient privacy. All patient data, including wound images, were handled in accordance with ethical standards and institutional guidelines. Personal identifiers were not stored within the application, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. Access to the application was restricted to authorized users, and basic security measures such as user authentication were implemented. In addition, data management procedures followed institutional policies to ensure confidentiality and responsible data handling.

Evaluation phase

A pre-test and post-test design was used to evaluate differences before and after use of the application. The evaluation phase involved 12 participants consisting of six new graduate nurses and six independent wound care nurses. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling based on their level of clinical experience. New graduate nurses represented novice clinicians with limited clinical experience, whereas independent wound care nurses were selected because of their established experience in providing wound care in independent clinical practice. These two groups were intentionally included because the application was designed as a clinical decision-support tool for users with varying levels of expertise. New graduate nurses represented novice users who were expected to benefit from structured guidance during wound assessment and dressing selection, whereas independent wound care nurses represented experienced practitioners whose assessments provided a practical reference for evaluating the consistency and clinical applicability of the application's recommendations across different levels of clinical experience.

Participants completed an initial assessment to measure their baseline clinical reasoning or wound assessment ability. After using the application, a post-test was conducted using the same or equivalent instrument to assess changes in performance. Performance was assessed across four clinical domains (wound color identification, inflammation assessment, exudate assessment, and dressing selection) using a structured observation sheet. A scoring system ranging from 0 to 2 was applied for each domain, where 0 indicated “not performed,” 1 indicated “performed but incomplete,” and 2 indicated “performed correctly,” reflecting the level of accuracy and completeness of clinical assessment and decision-making. Pre-test and post-test mean scores were compared to explore changes in assessment accuracy and consistency. Observations were conducted by trained observers using a non-interventional approach and predefined clinical skill indicators. All observers followed the standardized scoring criteria to ensure consistency between pre-test and post-test assessments. This phase was intended to assess improvements in performance and clinical decision-making support attributable to use of the application, rather than to determine definitive clinical effectiveness, thereby providing preliminary evidence of the potential role of the application in supporting consistent wound assessment and dressing selection. Given the relatively small sample size and exploratory nature of the study, inferential statistical analysis was not performed.

The usability and clinical applicability of the application were good during implementation testing (Figure 4). Automated wound severity classifications were comparable with manual assessments, and the usability evaluation yielded a mean SUS score of 79.8 ± 9.62, indicating good usability according to standard SUS interpretation guidelines. Participants also reported that the dressing recommendations were consistent with current modern wound care practices. The limited field trial further demonstrated that the performance of the application was stable across different clinical settings. The application consistently supported wound severity assessment and dressing selection throughout routine clinical use, indicating its feasibility for practical implementation. Discussion

Digital standardization of wound severity assessment

The findings obtained in this study indicate that integrating a standardized wound severity scoring logic into a mobile application enabled consistent and automated wound classification by users, highlighting the importance of translating manual assessment frameworks into structured digital workflows that promote uniform and reproducible clinical reasoning. This digital operationalization redefines wound assessment from a descriptive, experience-dependent activity into an executable clinical pathway. In conventional practice, even standardized tools remain susceptible to inter-practitioner variability due to their reliance on individual interpretation and recall of scoring criteria.26, 27, 28 By embedding the scoring logic directly into the system architecture, each assessment step was systematically standardized, and score calculation and interpretation were performed automatically, thereby enhancing the reliability and clinical consistency.29

This digital standardization functioned as a form of cognitive support by reducing the mental workload associated with complex clinical judgment. The enforced sequential structure and automated outputs minimized inconsistencies and strengthened adherence to evidence-based wound care principles.29 From a clinical informatics perspective, workflow-integrated decision support is more effective than standalone reference tools because it aligns directly with real-time clinical tasks. Moreover, the structured data generated by the application enabled the longitudinal monitoring of wound progression.30 This capability can support outcome evaluation, quality improvement, and future data-driven refinement of the assessment and recommendation algorithms, thereby promoting more reproducible, transparent, and accountable wound care practices.

Algorithm-guided dressing selection as clinical decision support

The application extended beyond assessment by linking wound severity outputs to moisture-based modern dressing recommendations, representing a transition from observational documentation toward prescriptive clinical decision support. In many care settings, dressing selection is still shaped by subjective experience and limited product knowledge, contributing to inconsistent and suboptimal wound management.31 The expanding diversity of dressing products and the biological complexity of wound healing exacerbate this challenge, making it difficult for clinicians to consistently select the most appropriate dressing for each wound.32 Economic considerations also influence decision-making because wound care costs are substantially higher for non-healing wounds, reinforcing the need for accurate and efficient product selection.33

To address these challenges, the application made the rationale behind dressing selection explicit and reproducible by encoding moisture-balance principles into a transparent algorithm, enabling clinicians to systematically link wound characteristics with appropriate interventions.34 This approach reduces reliance on tacit knowledge and promotes alignment with evidence-informed wound care standards, particularly when supported by high levels of expert agreement and positive usability feedback in algorithm-based decision tools.12 Instead of replacing clinical judgment, the system augmented it by offering structured guidance that clinicians can adapt to individual patient contexts, as found in digital platforms that significantly improve wound documentation quality and support remote monitoring without compromising care standards.35 In addition, mobile decision-support applications have been shown to facilitate wound assessment and access to evidence-based information, reinforcing their role in clinical decision-making across care settings.36

Algorithmic support is particularly valuable in environments with heterogeneous practitioner experience, where standardization of care processes is essential for patient safety and quality improvement. Decision-support algorithms are highly acceptable for early-career clinicians, with strong usability ratings and high levels of expert agreement, indicating their effectiveness in addressing variability in clinical experience.12 In addition, standardized care pathways are known to promote adherence to best practices, reduce treatment variability, and strengthen interprofessional collaboration, although wound care services still lack consistent standardization in many healthcare systems.37

Application as a learning-oriented clinical tool

In addition to its role as a clinical decision-support system, the application can function as a learning-oriented platform embedded within routine clinical workflows. The structured assessment pathways, visual reference examples, and immediate system feedback transform everyday wound care activities into a continuous learning process that reinforces theoretical concepts through repeated, context-based clinical use. Evidence from multiple health domains demonstrates that mobile health applications consistently improve user knowledge, where a meta-analysis of 52 studies involving 4057 participants found significantly higher knowledge scores among app users compared with control groups,38 as well as positive effects of applications for hypertension self-care,39 breast cancer awareness,40 coronary heart disease education,41 and asthma management.42 These studies did not specifically examine wound care applications but the consistent knowledge gains observed across diverse clinical contexts suggest that mobile platforms can serve a dual role by providing clinical functionality while simultaneously enhancing user knowledge and clinical competence.43

By externalizing expert clinical reasoning into a visible and systematic framework, the application can potentially facilitate knowledge transfer and narrow performance gaps between novice and experienced practitioners. Differences in clinical reasoning processes between experts and novices have been identified, particularly in differential diagnosis formulation, diagnostic planning, and clinical data reassessment, which can inform the design of structured knowledge-transfer frameworks.44 The feasibility of eliciting and formalizing tacit expert knowledge has also been demonstrated through cognitive task analysis methods applied across training contexts.45 Improvements in clinical reasoning documentation across training levels and the positive acceptance of digital reasoning support tools suggest that structured systems can strengthen professional learning and performance.46,47

Over time, repeated interaction with standardized assessment and dressing logic may promote the internalization of evidence-based wound care principles, thereby strengthening clinical confidence and competence. From a health systems perspective, these types of learning-oriented digital tools can potentially contribute to workforce capacity development, support knowledge standardization, and enhance the consistency of care across clinical settings. Empirical evidence from digital wound care interventions substantiates these mechanisms at the level of clinician performance and care processes, demonstrating significant improvements in clinical confidence and competence, and reduced variations in practice following the implementation of decision-support applications.48 Furthermore, digital wound tracking systems have been associated with clinically meaningful gains in efficiency, including significantly shorter healing durations and fewer nursing visits compared with conventional monitoring.49 Improvements in the quality and completeness of documentation have also been observed, with substantially higher recording rates in intervention groups than standard care, highlighting the potentially positive contribution of learning-oriented digital tools to more standardized and consistent wound care practices.35

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study was the integration of a validated wound assessment instrument with an automated dressing recommendation algorithm to create a unified clinical decision support system rather than a standalone documentation tool. The multidisciplinary expert validation and iterative development process further enhance the credibility and clinical relevance of the application.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The application relied on manual input for wound characteristics, and thus its accuracy was dependent on user interpretation. Moreover, this study was designed as an early-stage development and usability evaluation; therefore, its primary focus was assessing the feasibility, usability, and preliminary performance rather than clinical effectiveness. The relatively small sample size and this preliminary focus may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical settings. In addition, the application was developed based on the Perdanakusuma II scoring system, which has been validated and widely applied in Indonesia. The application framework was informed by both Indonesian and international evidence on wound assessment and modern dressing selection, but its current implementation may have limited applicability in healthcare settings where other internationally recognized wound assessment systems are routinely used. All of the validation items achieved the maximum scores, resulting in I-CVI and S-CVI values of 1.00, which may reflect a high level of agreement among experts regarding the relevance and appropriateness of the application. However, the lack of variability in responses is acknowledged as a limitation because it may have reduced the sensitivity at detecting differences in expert judgment. In addition, long-term clinical outcomes such as healing time, infection rates, and cost-effectiveness were not assessed. These limitations indicate that there is a need for larger-scale and outcome-focused studies.

Clinical implications and future directions

Clinically, the application has the potential to support standardized, evidence-informed wound care by reducing variability in assessment and dressing selection. The roles of the application as both a decision-support and educational tool make it particularly suitable for settings with limited access to specialized wound care training. Future development should focus on integrating artificial intelligence for automated wound image analysis, expanding patient data modules, and enabling interoperability with electronic medical record systems. Future studies should also evaluate the impact of the application on clinical outcomes, including wound healing time, infection rates, and cost-effectiveness, to establish its effectiveness in real-world clinical practice. Multi-center trials are also necessary based on evaluations of patient outcomes and economic impact to establish the application's broader clinical value.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of a digitally integrated wound care application for supporting clinical reasoning, standardization, and continuity of care in chronic diabetic wound management. By embedding a validated severity scoring system and evidence-based dressing logic into a structured clinical workflow, the application can potentially transform tacit wound care knowledge into an executable decision framework to support more consistent, rational, and accountable practice. In addition to its technical function, the system is a model for how mobile health technologies can bridge gaps between clinical guidelines and everyday practice, particularly in resource-variable settings. Following further development and outcome-based validation, this type of digital decision-support platform may contribute meaningfully to improving the quality, safety, and sustainability of chronic wound care.
