Abstract

Monitoring treatment response of tuberculosis (TB) remains challenging due to the low sensitivity and delayed culture results. Host-derived proteomic biomarker offer a promising alternative by reflecting systemic immune and inflammatory response during therapy. This review aims to systematically evaluate protein-based host biomarkers for monitoring TB treatment response. A systematic search of PubMed and Scopus was conducted for studies published up to September 2025. Eligible studies included human research evaluating protein biomarkers associated with treatment response in adult pulmonary TB. A total of 54 studies comprising 105 distinct biomarkers were included. Biomarkers were grouped into seven categories: acute-phase proteins, inflammatory cytokines, innate immune markers, matrix remodeling enzymes, immune checkpoint molecules, angiogenic factors, and metabolic recovery proteins. Successful treatment was associated with a rapid decline in inflammatory markers followed by modulation of immune and tissue remodeling pathways, while markers of metabolic recovery increased over time. Multi-protein biosignatures demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to individual biomarkers. Host-derived proteomic biomarkers provide a dynamic and multidimensional approach to monitoring TB treatment response. While multi-marker signatures show strong potential for clinical application, further standardization, validation, and large-scale studies are required to facilitate their integration into routine TB management.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading causes of death from infectious diseases worldwide. In 2024, an estimated 10.7 million people developed TB globally, resulting in approximately 1.23 million deaths among HIV-negative individuals. The global TB incidence rate was estimated at 131 cases per 100,000 population per year, with a case fatality rate of 11.5%.1 Although WHO-recommended anti-TB treatment regimens administered for 4–6 months can cure approximately 90% of patients with TB, the disease remains associated with high mortality when left untreated, with the death rate approaching 50%.1 Despite the efficacy of therapeutic interventions, a considerable proportion of patients still experience tuberculosis recurrence. Post-treatment recurrence remains a persistent challenge, with relapse rates typically ranging from 2% to 8% in clinical trials, but reaching 10% to 15% in high-burden, real world programmatic setting.2 Effective monitoring of treatment response is critical to ensure therapeutic success, detect treatment failure early, and guide clinical decision-making.3 Treatment monitoring enables clinicians to evaluate therapeutic response, ensure adherence, and detect early treatment failure or adverse drug reactions.4 The development of novel treatment monitoring techniques with high sensitivity and efficacy is critical for improving patient outcomes and limiting the spread of TB.

Conventional methods such as sputum smear microscopy and sputum culture remain the primary standards for monitoring TB treatment. However, their utility is constrained by highly variable diagnostic yields and the prolonged turnaround times inherent to mycobacterial cultures, impede timely clinical decision-making, such as detecting treatment failure or drug resistance.5,6 Moreover, both smear microscopy and culture rely heavily on the viability of bacilli within the sputum sample. Non-viable or dormant M. tuberculosis cells often fail to grow in conventional culture media, leading to false-negative results, even though patients may still harbor viable bacilli and face a high risk of relapse.7,8

In addition, the sensitivity of these methods is low in paucibacillary TB conditions, such as in children, HIV-infected individuals, or extrapulmonary TB cases. Smear microscopy requires at least 5000–10,000 bacilli per mL of sputum to be detected microscopically.9 Consequently, patients with low bacterial loads are often misclassified as negative despite ongoing infection, with smear sensitivity ranging from 20% to 80%, and often below 30% in pediatric or extrapulmonary TB.10,11

In response to the limitations of pathogen-based tests, there is growing interest in host-directed biomarkers, molecular signals generated by the human immune system or metabolic pathways in response to infection and therapy.12,13 Unlike sputum-based tests, host biomarkers can be measured in more accessible and less hazardous specimens, such as blood (serum, plasma, or whole blood), urine, or saliva.13 These biomarkers provide a window into the systemic physiological state of the patient, reflecting the degree of pulmonary and systemic inflammation and its resolution during successful antibiotic treatment.4,14

The rationale for using proteomic and immune-directed markers stems from the dynamic host–pathogen interaction that defines TB pathogenesis. Successful treatment leads to a rapid decline in mycobacterial burden, which is mirrored by the “cooling” of host inflammatory and immune activation signatures.15–17 These biomarkers show potential for meeting the WHO Target Product Profile (TPP) criteria for TB monitoring, which recommends a minimum sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 70% for screening tools.4,18 In addition, advances in high-throughput proteomic platforms have enabled the identification of multi-marker protein signatures that can be measured simultaneously for disease monitoring because multi-marker protein analysis can improve accuracy by capturing and representing the diverse pathways, biological processes, and cell types involved in complex disease.15,19 Addressing the gap necessitates both innovative diagnostic methods and socioeconomic feasibility. Emerging host proteomic signatures have the potential to be translated into low-cost, point of care multiplex platforms, such as paper-based lateral flow assay or targeted ELISAs. These platforms offer a highly scalable alternative to infrastructure-intensive, time consuming MGIT culture systems currently relied on ini many resource-limited settings.4,13,20

This narrative review with systematic searching aims to investigate the clinical utility of host-derived proteomic signatures for monitoring anti-tuberculosis therapy. The objective of reviewing the existing body of evidence are as follows: First, the clinical scope should be restricted to adult pulmonary tuberculosis to minimize confounding heterogeneity. Second, the focus should be on non-sputum biological matrices, including serum, plasma, and urine. Third, the performance of biomarkers should be characterized across core therapeutic outcomes, including early treatment response, two-month culture conversion, and long-term relapse. Finally, the comparative performance of single-protein markers versus multi-marker signatures should be analytically dissected alongside their dynamic temporal kinetics. This review will therefore cover the identification and validation of protein-based biomarkers, evaluate their potential impact on TB therapy outcomes, and clarify how protein biomarkers from sputum and non-sputum samples can strengthen TB treatment monitoring and contribute to global TB control and eradication efforts. In addition, this review addresses the uncertainty regarding the clinical utility of multi-protein signatures compared to single-protein approaches, particularly in contexts involving readily available samples such as serum, plasma, and urine.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Search Strategy

A comprehensive systematic search strategy was conducted to identify relevant studies on Clinical Biomarker on TB treatment response. Electronic databases, including Pubmed and Scopus were searched utilizing a combination of keyword terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as (tuberculosis OR “pulmonary TB” OR “mycobacterium tuberculosis infection”) AND (“protein biomarker*” OR “biological marker*” OR “clinical biomarker*” OR “protein marker*” OR “biologic marker*”) AND (“treatment outcome*” OR “treatment response” OR “treatment efficacy” OR “treatment monitoring” OR “therapy effectiveness”). The search was limited to articles published in English up to September 2025.

Eligibility Criteria

Four independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were considered for further assessment if they were deemed to be potentially relevant or if there was uncertainty based on the abstract alone. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a fifth reviewer. Our objective was to emphasize a comprehensive approach to evaluate clinical protein biomarkers on TB treatment responses. To ensure a uniform and high-quality evidence base, primary research study were required to meet following specific inclusion criteria:

(1) Population: the study will include human subject aged 18 years or older, bacteriologically confirmed active pulmonary tuberculosis, (2) Intervention: patients undergoing standard, WHO-recommended first line anti-tuberculosis treatment regimens; (3) Outcome: the longitudinal tracking of host-derived protein biomarkers, measured at predetermined intervals during the therapeutic regimen, with clear documentation of correlations to clinical outcomes; (4) Language: the following articles have been published in English-language peer-reviewed journals exclusively up to September 2025.

Exclusion criteria included pediatric population, tuberculosis with comorbid conditions, nonprotein-based biomarker, and studies related to review articles, letters, protocols, and editorials. This framework focused on capturing evidence regarding protein biomarker efficacy, biological plausibility, and potential utility in monitoring therapeutic progress. The study selection process was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Records identified from the selected databases were screened for duplicates, followed by title and abstract screening. Potentially eligible studies were then assessed through full-text review based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data Extraction

The data collected for this narrative review were author, study publication year, study title, study location, study design, study population, sample size, type of protein biomarker, and key findings related to key biomarker expression and treatment outcomes. The extracted data were analyzed to provide a narrative review of the different protein biomarkers and each of its mechanism on TB treatment (Table 1). The results of the included studies were summarized based on the mechanism of action of each protein biomarker, the correlation between the biomarker and specific stages of tuberculosis treatment, and the pathophysiology of TB leading to regulatory changes. A total of 105 identified biomarkers were grouped into seven categories based on their function to facilitate a comprehensive analysis of the multidimensional host response: Acute Phase & Systemic Inflammatory Proteins, Inflammatory Cytokines and Chemokines, Innate Immune and Complement Activation, Matrix Remodelling and Tissue Damage, Immune Checkpoint and T-cell Activation, Growth Factors & Angiogenesis, Metabolic and Immunometabolic Recovery (Table 2). To account for methodological variations across the included literature, “specificity” was operationally defined and categorized based on the comparison frameworks utilized in the primary studies. Diagnostic specificity was evaluated relative to latent tuberculosis infection or healthy control cohorts, whereas therapeutic monitoring accuracy was benchmarked against either pathogen-based clearance (mycobacterial growth or sputum culture conversion) or secondary clinical surrogate endpoints, including radiological resolution and standardized symptom scores.

Results

Study Characteristics and Geographic Distribution

A total of 54 studies met the inclusion criteria for this review (Table 1). Methodologically, prospective cohorts with longitudinal designs predominated (n=35), followed by case-control and nested case-control studies (n=14), while retrospective, experimental, and cross-sectional designs accounted for the remaining 5 studies. The geographic distribution of the evidence spanned five continents but was unevenly distributed. Asia contributed the largest body of evidence (26 studies),23,25–30,32,39,40,42–44,47,48,54,56,59,60,63–66,68,72,74 followed by Africa (16 studies),22,24,26,36,38,44,45,51,58,61,62,67,69–71,73 Europe (9 studies),21,34,36,37,45,46,49,50,57 South America (6 studies),31,33,35,53,55,70 and North America (3 studies).36,41,45 One study was conducted across different continents.44

A total of 105 distinct biomarkers were reported across the included studies (Table 3). C-reactive protein (CRP) was the most frequently investigated biomarker, reported in 15 articles, followed by interferon gamma–induced protein 10 (IP-10/CXCL10) in 12 articles and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in five articles. Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP-8) were each examined in four studies. In terms of biological matrices, the literature showed a marked shift toward non-sputum samples; serum was utilized in 31 studies and plasma in 22 studies. Less frequent matrices included sputum (3 studies), while urine and whole blood were each utilized in only 2 studies (Table 4). Functional Categorization of Host Proteomic Biomarkers

In the Acute Phase & Systemic Inflammatory Proteins category, five protein biomarkers were identified across the included evidence: C-reactive protein (CRP), Serum Amyloid A/SAA1 (SAA/SAA1), ferritin, transthyretin (TTHY), and afamin (AFAM) (Table 2). With successful TB treatment, CRP, SAA/SAA1, ferritin, and AFAM consistently decreased (↓), whereas TTHY increased (↑), consistent with its role as a negative acute-phase protein. CRP, an acute-phase reactant, demonstrated a rapid early decline within the first ~2 weeks, and high baseline levels or persistent elevation at 2 months were associated with slower treatment response, potential treatment extension, or risk of failure.31,36,45,70 SAA/SAA1, also an acute-phase protein, was strongly linked to baseline disease severity and treatment response, including correlation with week-8 culture status.36,45 Ferritin (an iron-storage/inflammation marker) showed a decline by day 60, while higher day-60 ferritin was reported among patients who remained culture-positive, supporting its monitoring value.31 Notably, Alpha-2-macroglobulin (A2M) was reported to discriminate treatment failure with high nominal accuracy (reported AUC up to 1.0 in initial cohort evaluations), though such estimates reflect exploratory findings subject to potential overfitting.

Biomarkers within the inflammatory cytokines and chemokines category (Table 2) demonstrated dynamic changes during anti-tuberculosis treatment, with most pro-inflammatory mediators showing declining trends in parallel with therapeutic response. IP-10 (CXCL10) was the most reproducible marker, with longitudinal reductions documented across multiple cohorts using serum, plasma, and urine samples.28,29,37,38,44,51,69–72,74 Other key cytokines, including IL-638,69 TNF-α and its soluble receptor sTNF-R1,26,70 and IL-1β,70 consistently declined during therapy. Chemokines involved in monocyte recruitment, such as MCP-1 (CCL2) and MIP-1α (CCL3), also exhibited coordinated downward trends in successful responders.29,69,71 Overall, across the included studies, inflammatory cytokines and chemokines including IP-10 (CXCL10), IL-6, TNF-related markers, IFN-γ–associated chemokines, IL-1β, IL-5, IL-18, MCP-1 (CCL2), MIP-1α (CCL3), MIF, CXCL9 (MIG), and CXCL11 (I-TAC) demonstrated modulation during anti-TB treatment, with the predominant pattern being a decline over time in patients responding successfully to therapy.

Innate immune and complement activation proteins demonstrated consistent treatment-associated modulation during anti-tuberculosis therapy (Table 2). Russomando et al (2024) reported that C1q and C4 were elevated at baseline and that a C1q-related signature differentiated active TB from cured profiles.35 Wang et al (2015) documented that Complement C3 and FCN2 (Ficolin-2) decreased in cured patients and changed significantly after two months of treatment, with FCN2 levels associated with smear status.39 Kedia (2018) identified a serum protein signature including RET4 and PGRP2 that changed with therapy and predicted culture conversion.45 Ndiaye (2022) reported that SELL (L-selectin), within a multi-protein panel, supported treatment monitoring and prediction of sputum culture conversion.51 Musteikienė (2021) showed that suPAR declined after one month of therapy and differed according to conversion status.50 Adzic-Vukicevic et al (2024) and Chen et al (2015) reported significant declines in chitotriosidase (CHIT1) during treatment, correlating with clinical improvement.21,23 Andrade (2013) further demonstrated that HO-1 levels decreased following successful therapy but remained elevated in treatment failure.59 Collectively, these findings indicate coordinated modulation of complement components (C1q, C3, C4), innate recognition molecules (FCN2, PGRP2), trafficking markers (SELL), and immune activation markers (suPAR, CHIT1, HO-1) during effective TB treatment.

Biomarkers involved in Matrix Remodeling & Tissue Damage showed significant modulation during TB therapy (Table 2). In general, positive treatment responses are characterized by a decrease in the levels of biomarkers such as MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-7, MMP-8, Neuron-Specific Enolase (NSE), and ARHGDIB (RhoGDI2).

Kumar et al (2020) reported that reductions in plasma levels of MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-7, and MMP-8 are associated with recovery, while elevated baseline levels strongly predict poor treatment outcomes (failure, relapse, or death).29 This finding was supported by Lee et al (2015), who emphasized that a decrease in MMP-8 is a critical indicator, with consistently high levels (>3000 pg/mL) at the second month of treatment being significantly correlated with persistent positive cultures.30 A similar decreasing trend was observed for NSE. Nam et al (2016) showed that high NSE levels, initially caused by macrophage activity at granuloma lesions, significantly decreased after the completion of anti-TB treatment.32 Additionally, Wang et al (2015) identified through proteomic analysis that ARHGDIB was downregulated in patients who had been declared cured compared to those who had not yet received treatment.39 In contrast, an exception was observed for MMP-9, which showed an increasing pattern. This finding is consistent with data from Kumar et al (2020), who found that MMP-9 levels were significantly higher in cases with adverse outcomes compared to cured controls, suggesting that elevated levels of MMP-9 are associated with severe tissue damage pathology and an increased risk of treatment failure.29

Analysis of biomarkers in the Immune Checkpoint & T-cell Activation category indicates that therapeutic success was further characterized by a decline in soluble immune checkpoints, including sPD-1, sPD-L1, and sBTLA, reflecting the reversal of immune dysregulation (Table 2). Specifically, significant reductions were observed in soluble immune checkpoints (sPD-1, sPD-L1, sPD-L2, sBTLA, sCTLA-4, sGITR), IgG antibody titers for sMTL-13, and cytokines IL-2 and IL-4. Chen et al (2022) revealed that patients with active TB exhibit elevated levels of soluble immune checkpoints (sICs) in circulation, reflecting immune dysregulation. However, effective anti-TB treatment triggers a downward trend in these molecules.42 Specifically, sPD-L1 shows a rapid early decline, while sBTLA decreases progressively and significantly after six months of therapy. A similar decreasing pattern was observed in response to mycobacterial antigens. Nogueira et al (2010) reported IgG antibody titers against the mycobacterial lectin sMTL-13 declined sharply within the first 1–2 months of therapy, making it a specific biomarker to monitor treatment efficacy.33 Furthermore, T-cell activation markers like IL-2 and IL-4 also showed significant reductions, correlating with faster radiological recovery and tissue repair. Iqbal et al (2016) found that elevated levels of IL-2 and IL-4 at the time of diagnosis were associated with slower radiological recovery (slow responders), whereas lower or decreasing levels correlated with faster pulmonary lesion healing (fast responders), suggesting that downregulation of excessive T-cell activation supports more optimal tissue repair.25

Growth Factors and Angiogenesis biomarker classes exhibit a decline in expression in patients following TB treatment (Table 2). Active TB was associated with elevated systemic levels of angiogenic factors, which subsided following treatment. Vascular Endothelial Growth Factors (VEGF-A, VEGF-C, VEGF-D) and their receptors (VEGF-R2) showed substantial reductions post-treatment, with VEGF-R2 achieving a diagnostic AUC of 0.999 for active disease.47 Similar declines were observed in Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF) and Platelet-Derived Growth Factor-BB (PDGF-BB) within the first four months of intervention.61,74 The reduction within this biomarker class correlates with a diminished rate of neovascularization and the mitigation of fibrotic tissue formation, both of which are pathological hallmarks of chronic infections such as TB.

In contrast to inflammatory markers, proteins associated with Metabolic and Immunometabolic Recovery exhibited an upward trajectory (Table 2). This elevation is functionally linked to post-inflammatory systemic restoration. This includes tissue repair, the resolution of inflammation characterized by pathogen clearance, and the reconstitution of metabolic homeostasis. Albumin and Apolipoprotein levels increased significantly after the second month of treatment, signaling the restoration of nutritional status.39 Additionally, Phosphoseryl-tRNA kinase (PSTK) levels rose progressively throughout the treatment duration, which is hypothesized to reflect enhanced bacterial clearance and protection against oxidative stress.27

Discussion

Principal Findings: The Multidimensional Trajectory

The synthesis of 54 studies indicates that monitoring tuberculosis (TB) treatment is most effectively conceptualized as a multidimensional host-response trajectory rather than a single biomarker event. Successful anti-TB therapy (ATT) triggers coordinated shifts across several biological pathways, shifting from an inflammatory state to structural repair and metabolic recovery. Various biomarkers identified in tuberculosis in this review were grouped based on their biological functions and roles in the immune response to Mycobacterium tuberculosis, facilitating the interpretation of disease mechanisms and their clinical relevance (Table 2). A discernible transition was identified through the observation of distinct biological phases. A rapid “cooling” of acute-phase and systemic inflammatory proteins was evident during the initial two weeks (eg, CRP, SAA1). These biomarkers are primary indicators of systemic disease burden and reflect the immediate “cooling” of the host response. The cooling event is followed by the modulation of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (eg, IP‑10 (CXCL10), IL‑6, TNF‑α). These biomarker class of proteins serve as critical mediators for immune cell recruitment, granuloma formation, and cellular immune polarization in TB. Furthermore, components of the innate immune response, including C1q, C3, C4, and pattern recognition proteins demonstrate significant treatment-associated modulation due to their roles in complement activation and pathogen detection. Simultaneously, the downregulation of matrix remodelling enzymes (MMP-1, 2, 7, 8) indicates the termination of lung tissue degradation since this class of biomarker provide a window into pulmonary lesion dynamics and structural organ recovery. Therapeutic success is further marked by the resolution of immune dysregulation and excessive T-cell activation (eg, sPD‑1, sPD‑L1/‑L2, sCTLA‑4, sBTLA), indicate a restoration of immune tolerance and more optimal tissue repair. The process ultimately leads to metabolic and immunometabolic recovery, as evidenced by elevated albumin levels and apolipoproteins. This recovery is indicative of a restoration of nutritional homeostasis. Biomarkers associated with angiogenesis, such as VEGF-A, -C, -D and their receptors (VEGFR), as well as FGF and PDGF-BB, are significantly elevated in active TB but undergo substantial attenuation within 2 to 4 months of successful intervention. This reduction correlates with a diminished rate of neovascularization and the mitigation of fibrotic tissue formation in pulmonary lesions. Finally, the process culminates in metabolic and immunometabolic recovery, characterized by rising levels of albumin and apolipoproteins, reflecting the restoration of nutritional status and lipid metabolism.

Performance Comparison: Single vs Multi-Marker Signatures

Overall, the included studies indicate that single protein biomarkers have significant potential for TB diagnosis and treatment outcome prediction, although their performance is not uniformly consistent across clinical settings. Individual markers such as Alpha-2-Macroglobulin (A2M) exhibited high single-cohort discrimination (AUC 1.0) in identifying treatment failure.48 Similarly, VEGF-R2 and VEGF-A show very high accuracy in distinguishing active disease from latent infection, with VEGF-R2 achieving 100% sensitivity.47 Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1) has also proven to be highly accurate in differentiating active TB, with a higher specificity (94.9%) than traditional acute-phase markers like CRP.59 However, other single markers such as CCL3, fibronectin, CRP, and CXCL9 show more moderate or context-dependent performance. For instance, fibronectin shows high specificity (100%) but lower sensitivity (84.21%) when predicting severity based on chest X-rays, while CXCL9 shows 83% sensitivity in evaluating early treatment response.60,75 This indicates that while single protein biomarkers are highly informative, their clinical utility remains dependent on the biological pathways represented and their intended application.

The evidence strongly suggests that combined biomarker signatures offer superior stability and robustness across diverse clinical settings. Jacobs et al (2016) reported a six-marker biosignature demonstrated high nominal discrimination (with reported AUCs approaching 1.0) within their specific discovery cohorts, while another six-marker panel in the same study achieved also with relatively high discrimination (AUC of 1.0) in HIV-negative individuals.76 Similarly, Similarly, Kumar et al (2021) discovered that the combination of CRP, A2M, and SAP, and Pandiarajan et al (2024) found that the combination of IL-10, IL-17A, and TNF-α, was reported for predicting unfavourable treatment outcomes with high nominal accuracy (AUC of 1.0).48,52 Even when perfect discrimination was not reached, other biosignatures generally maintained a more balanced sensitivity–specificity profile than most single markers, supporting the view that multibiomarker models better capture the biological complexity of TB.

This advantage is further supported by studies directly comparing single biomarkers with multi-biomarker models. In Kumar et al (2021), individual markers such as CRP and SAP performed well, but the combination CRP + A2M + SAP improved discrimination to 100% sensitivity, 100% specificity, and an AUC of 1.0.48 In Chung et al (2016), CXCL9 and CXCL10 showed good standalone performance, yet combinations of CXCR3 ligands increased sensitivity to 88–93% while maintaining high specificity (92–96%).60 Likewise, Pandiarajan et al (2024) showed that although IL-10 alone already performed very well, the addition of IL-17A and TNF-α further optimized discrimination.52 This pattern is logically extended by Jacobs et al (2016), which showed that a six-marker biosignature outperformed single markers such as NCAM and CRP.76 Taken together, these findings suggest that although selected single biomarkers may perform exceptionally well, multibiomarker approaches are more promising for clinical application because they provide greater robustness, more balanced accuracy, and better alignment with the biological heterogeneity of TB.

Biomarkers within the acute-phase and systemic inflammatory protein classes play a pivotal role in monitoring both acute and chronic inflammatory conditions, including those induced by infectious agents such as Mtb. These conditions are characterized by the elevation of protein levels within these classes, notably CRP and SAA1.77–79 CRP is a primary acute-phase protein frequently utilized to monitor responses to chronic infection, functioning as a pattern recognition receptor within the innate immune response. It is synthesized by hepatocytes, and its regulation is modulated by IL-6.77,78 SAA1 serves as a critical mediator of immune activation and metabolic regulation, bridging the acute-phase response with long-term disease dynamics. SAA proteins exhibit dual roles in inflammation: promoting tissue repair and resolution under sterile conditions, while potentially exacerbating damage in chronic inflammatory diseases and infections.79

Biomarkers categorized as inflammatory cytokines and chemokines also tend to exhibit increased levels during TB infection. IP-10 (CXCL10) functions as a chemokine secreted by monocytes and endothelial cells in response to IFN-gamma stimulation, serving to recruit activated T-cells to the site of infection. Consequently, its levels reflect Th1 activity and the efficacy of TB treatment.80,81 Furthermore, IL-6 acts as a cytokine that activates the JAK/STAT signaling pathway to trigger the acute-phase response and B-cell differentiation, thereby serving as a key indicator of inflammation.82 TNF-alpha functions as an effector of apoptosis and continuous granuloma formation, operating in conjunction with its soluble form, sTNF-R1. This relationship signifies the level of immune system activity and regulates the bioavailability of TNF-alpha throughout the inflammatory process.83,84

The monitoring process also involves additional protein classes. Within the complement system, components C3 and C4 facilitate the opsonization of TB bacilli by macrophages, where a decline in these protein levels reflects the resolution of the infection.85,86 Regarding matrix remodeling, a downregulation in the expression of MMPs indicates reduced extracellular matrix degradation and lung tissue destruction, signaling structural organ recovery from cavitation-induced damage.87,88 Elevated serum albumin levels serve as an indicator of metabolic recovery, reflecting the restoration of nutritional status following TB infection.39 Lastly, PSTK is an intermediate enzyme involved in the synthesis of selenoproteins and is associated with selenium levels. PSTK exerts a protective role against oxidative stress during early lung injury. Antioxidant activity increases during the TB recovery phase as a physiological response to neutralize oxidative stress and enhance bacterial clearance.27

Comparison with Existing Monitoring Strategies: Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

Current TB treatment monitoring primarily relies on pathogen-based approaches, including sputum smear microscopy and sputum culture.1,5 While these methods remain the conventional standard, they present several limitations that restrict their clinical utility. Sputum microscopy has relatively low sensitivity, particularly in patients with paucibacillary disease, HIV co-infection, or extrapulmonary involvement.5 Culture methods, although more sensitive, require prolonged incubation periods and depend on bacterial viability, which can delay the detection of treatment failure or relapse. These constraints highlight the need for complementary monitoring strategies capable of reflecting treatment response earlier and more dynamically.1,5

Host-derived proteomic biomarkers provide an alternative perspective by capturing systemic immune and inflammatory responses associated with the decline of Mycobacterium tuberculosis burden during therapy. Host-derived proteomic biomarkers measured in serum, plasma, or urine provide a systemic perspective that circumvents the challenges of sputum collection and bacterial viability.16,89 These biomarkers can reflect ongoing host pathology even when bacilli are dormant or present in low loads, potentially meeting the WHO Target Product Profile (TPP) criteria for screening tools (90% sensitivity and 70% specificity).90 For instance, while a sputum culture requires 4–8 weeks, a high MMP-8 level (>3000 pg/mL) at the second month was identified as an independent predictor of persistent culture positivity (Lee et al (2015)). Furthermore, the rapid decline of IP-10 and CRP within the first week of treatment can serve as an early indicator of an appropriate bactericidal effect, potentially identifying misdiagnosed or drug-resistant cases long before culture results are available.91

The act of predicting post-treatment relapse remains quantitatively distinct from the process of tracking on-treatment bacterial clearance. Longitudinal studies have indicated that end-of-treatment host biomarker signatures have the capacity to stratify post-cure relapse risk with moderate-to-high accuracy (AUCs ranging from 0.75 to 0.86; adjusted hazard ratios of 3.0 to 4.8). This offers a quantitative framework that can be used to identify the 5–10% of individuals who have been clinically cured and who face disease reactivation.

Across the 54 studies included in this review, more than one hundred protein biomarkers were reported, supported by a predominance of longitudinal cohort studies that provide vital temporal context. This diversity reflects the complex host–pathogen interaction underlying TB pathogenesis and highlights the potential value of host proteomic signatures in capturing multidimensional aspects of treatment response. The use of diverse biological sample types, including serum, plasma, urine, and whole blood, also highlights the feasibility of non-sputum-based monitoring approaches.

Despite these strengths, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the evidence base demonstrates substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, and may lead to variability in biomarker quantification and limit comparability across studies. In addition, sampling time points varied considerably, with some studies evaluating early treatment responses within the first weeks, while others focused on later stages such as two-month culture conversion or treatment completion. These differences complicate efforts to define standardized biomarker thresholds or temporal trajectories that could be translated into clinical practice. Second, heterogeneity also exists in the clinical outcome definitions used across studies. Treatment success, culture conversion, relapse, and radiological improvement were variably used as outcome measures, which may influence the observed associations between biomarker levels and treatment response, reduces the ability to perform direct comparisons or meta-analytic synthesis of biomarker performance. Additionally, cohort characteristics varied widely across studies, including differences in geographic settings, disease severity, and patient populations. These factors may influence host immune responses and contribute to inconsistencies in reported biomarker performance. It is also imperative to acknowledge that host-derived biomarkers offer an objective, subclinical perspective on the resolution of localized lung inflammation, thereby enhancing the accuracy of subjective clinical wellness scores. However, existing diagnostic evaluations remain limited in their ability to differentiate active disease from latent infection or healthy cohorts, particularly when driven by broadly shared inflammatory pathways. This limitation underscores an urgent necessity for cross-disease validation against conditions such as lung cancer and pneumonia, with the aim of ruling out generalized, non-specific systemic inflammatory corrections.

Methodologically, the exclusion of the EMBASE database introduces potential publication bias, and pronounced study heterogeneity precluded a formal risk-of-bias assessment. From a statistical perspective, panels that report near-perfect accuracy (AUC ~1.0) must be interpreted with caution. This is due to the fact that the majority of these panels lack independent external validation and are susceptible to overfitting due to their small sample sizes.

Despite promising results, translation into point-of-care diagnostics remains limited due to cost, assay complexity, and lack of standardized thresholds. A significant translational obstacle emphasized by this review is the substantial platform heterogeneity present within the compiled database, encompassing a range of targeted Multiplex Luminex immunoassays, high-throughput LC-MS/MS, and SOMAscan aptamer arrays. This heterogeneity poses a substantial challenge to the attainment of robust inter-study reproducibility and assay standardization. Furthermore, the diagnostic accuracy of these host signatures in the real world is susceptible to significant metabolic and clinical confounders, including HIV co-infection, diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, and tobacco smoking. These factors can modulate baseline systemic inflammation, which may compromise the generalizability of biomarkers in diverse endemic settings.

To advance these biomarkers toward clinical implementation, future research should prioritize methodological standardization and external validation. Standardized sampling schedules, harmonized treatment outcome definitions, and consistent analytical platforms are essential to improve comparability across studies. Of note, the achievement of the World Health Organization’s Target Product Profile (TPP) benchmarks of 90% sensitivity and 70% specificity persists as an active research objective rather than a realized standard due to the predominance of host proteomic signatures remaining in the exploratory discovery stage. In addition, the clinical generalizability of these candidate signatures is inherently constrained by modest sample sizes and significant geographic clustering. The study cohorts are predominantly situated in East Asia and South Africa. Large-scale, multicenter validation of multi-protein signatures using standardized sampling schedules is essential to determine their generalizability across diverse global epidemiological settings. Particular emphasis should also be placed on evaluating multi-protein signatures rather than individual biomarkers, as combined biomarker panels are more likely to capture the multidimensional host response to therapy and achieve the sensitivity and specificity thresholds required for clinical TB monitoring. A transition from costly multiplex platforms to economical paper-based lateral flow assays or targeted ELISAs is imperative to avert the “financial ruin” that households encounter during prolonged TB therapy.

Conclusions

This comprehensive review of 54 studies confirms that TB treatment monitoring is most effectively conceptualized as a multidimensional host-response trajectory rather than a single biomarker event. The evidence demonstrates a clear biological transition: successful anti-TB therapy triggers a rapid “cooling” of acute-phase and systemic inflammatory proteins and cytokines within the first two weeks, followed by the modulation of innate immune components and the reduction of matrix remodeling enzymes that drive tissue destruction. The process culminates in metabolic and immunometabolic recovery, which signal the restoration of nutritional homeostasis and the resolution of oxidative stress. A pivotal finding is the superiority of multi-marker signatures over single-protein approaches. These host-derived markers, measurable in non-sputum samples such as serum and urine, circumvent the limitations of traditional pathogen-based tests, offering a potential path toward early detection of treatment failure and more personalized therapeutic management. Future implementation depends on large-scale validation, assay standardization, and integration into accessible diagnostic platforms. The successful clinical translation of these candidate biosignatures is contingent upon the validation of their specificity against non-TB pulmonary infections and malignant conditions. This is imperative to mitigate the risk of false-positive therapeutic monitoring results driven by overlapping pathways of generalized tissue healing and systemic inflammation resolution.
