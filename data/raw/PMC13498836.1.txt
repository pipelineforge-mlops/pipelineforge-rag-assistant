Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis affects millions of people worldwide each year and requires timely interventions because it causes life-threatening, preventable complications. Nurses play a pivotal role in the prevention of this health problem.

Objectives

This study aimed to assess nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, practices, and associated factors regarding deep vein thrombosis prevention at comprehensive specialized hospitals in northeastern Ethiopia, 2022.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to August 30, 2022, employing a structured, self-administered questionnaire among 404 nurses. Study participants were selected through a simple random sampling technique. The data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and exported to SPSS 25 for analysis. A binary logistic regression model was utilized to determine outcome variables and associated factors, and multivariable logistic regression was applied to control for potential confounders.

Results

A total of 404 nurses participated in the study, giving a response rate of 97.8%. In this study, 56.7% (95% CI [51.8%, 61.5%]), 59.0% (95% CI [54.0%, 63.7%]), and 59.4% (95% CI [54.6%, 64.2%]) of nurses had adequate knowledge, favorable attitudes, and satisfactory practices, respectively. Age ≥ 36 years (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI [1.18, 6.31], p = .02), using standard guidelines (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI [1.25, 3.33], p = .001), deep vein thrombosis education (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI [1.15, 3.38], p = .01), and training (AOR = 2.85, 95% CI [1.69, 4.8], p = .001) were significantly associated with knowledge. Monitoring system (AOR = 4.06, 95% CI [2.50, 6.59], p = .001), deep vein thrombosis education (AOR = 1.88, 95% CI [1.14, 3.10], p = .01) and adequate knowledge (AOR = 1.68, 95% CI [1.03, 2.72], p = .04) were associated with practice. Educational status (AOR = 1.79, 95% CI [1.16, 2.77], p < .001) and work experience (AOR = 1.85, 95% CI [1.02, 3.42], p = .04) were factors independently associated with attitude.

Conclusion

Slightly more than half of the nurses demonstrated adequate knowledge, favorable attitudes, and satisfactory practices. Deep vein thrombosis education, training, availability of guidelines, and educational status were significantly associated with nurses’ knowledge of deep vein thrombosis prevention. Work experience, workload and educational status were associated with attitudes, while monitoring, deep vein thrombosis education and adequate knowledge had a significant association with practices.

Background

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is the formation of a blood clot within the deep veins, and it remains a significant cause of mortality and morbidity globally (Walke & Pohekar, 2021). It is typically linked to a multitude of factors, including increased blood clotting, reduced blood flow, and blood vessel injury, explained by Virchow’s triad (Stone et al., 2017). The development of DVT can be attributed to prolonged immobilization, stroke, cardiac conditions, obesity, varicose veins, anesthesia, and advanced age (> 65 years) (R Mousa et al., 2022). Importantly, DVT frequently occurs alongside other comorbidities, which complicates patient management and increases healthcare costs (Mulatu et al., 2020).

Hospitalized patients are highly vulnerable to developing DVT due to various health conditions like obesity, heart failure, or cancer (Goel et al., 2018), as well as prolonged hospital stays, all of which predispose them to the development of this condition (Abuzied et al., 2021).

Deep vein thrombosis is a major public health concern worldwide, affecting millions of people per year (Njonnou et al., 2019). In Europe, about 10% of general medical and surgical patients develop DVT, with the risk rising to nearly 50% after major orthopedic surgeries (Shargall & Litle, 2018). It causes about 60,000 to 100,000 deaths in the USA annually (Alyousef et al., 2022). In Africa, postoperative DVT rates vary from 2.4% to 9.6% (Danwang et al., 2017). Three-fourths of DVT cases occur with other medical disorders, complicating patient management and outcomes (Mulatu et al., 2020) in Ethiopia. This variation reflects the need for appropriate and context-specific prevention strategies.

Beyond its considerable burden, DVT can lead to life-threatening complications, such as pulmonary embolism (PE) (McLendon et al., 2017). About 2 in 5 patients with DVT are affected by post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), which reduces patients’ activity levels and quality of life (Duffett, 2022).

Despite its increased prevalence and fatal consequences, DVT is largely preventable disease (Stone et al., 2017). Thus, prevention remains the most cost-effective and feasible way (Bushra Parveen et al., 2021) through the application of different preventive methods. Non-pharmacological methods, such as intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) and graduated compression stockings (GCS) (Sachdeva et al., 2018), and pharmacological methods, which include the administration of heparin, warfarin, and low-molecular-weight heparin (Bircher & Chowdhury, 2020), are important preventive approaches for hospitalized patients.

Nurses play a key role in preventing DVT by assessing patients’ risk, administering anticoagulants, encouraging preventive exercises, teaching patients, and applying mechanical preventive techniques (Alyousef et al., 2022; Gomes et al., 2021). Therefore, their knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices are vital for reducing DVT-related complications and improving patient outcomes (Bushra Parveen et al., 2021). However, gaps in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding DVT prevention may contribute to poor patient outcomes (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018), and multiple institutional and nurse-related factors may influence their DVT prevention.

Although nurses play crucial roles in preventing DVT, there is limited information in Ethiopia about their knowledge, attitudes, practices and related factors of DVT prevention. Hence, this study was intended to evaluate nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, practices, and identify factors affecting DVT prevention. The study findings could help develop targeted and context specific interventions and improve patient outcomes.

Literature Review

Nurses’ Knowledge Regarding DVT Prevention

Evidence shows that nurses’ knowledge of DVT prevention varies considerably across different settings. Studies conducted in India (Antony et al., 2016), Turkey (Shah et al., 2020), and the Republic of Cyprus (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018) consistently reported that only about half of nurses demonstrated adequate knowledge regarding DVT prevention, requiring improved education and training to strengthen evidence-based preventive practices. In contrast, studies across various countries indicated that considerably lower levels of nurses’ knowledge regarding DVT prevention. Cross-sectional studies in Pakistan (Bushra Parveen et al., 2021), South Korea (Oh et al., 2017), and Egypt (Mohammed et al., 2018) revealed that a small proportion of nurses (20%–33%) demonstrated adequate knowledge. These findings indicate that many nurses may lack sufficient understanding of DVT risk factors, complications, and preventive strategies, which could affect the quality of patient care and implementation of preventive measures. Moreover, a cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia showed that a limited number of nurses (21.1%) had adequate knowledge of DVT prevention (Yesuf et al., 2021), indicating nurses’ knowledge gap to effectively implement appropriate preventive measures in clinical practice.

Nurses’ Attitudes Toward DVT Prevention

Studies conducted in Ethiopia (Kiflie et al., 2022), and China (Xu et al., 2019) showed most nurses generally have positive attitudes toward DVT prevention, although the level of positive attitude varies across settings. Studies from China (T. Yan et al., 2021) and Egypt (Mohammed et al., 2018) reported that most nurses expressed a favorable attitude toward prevention of DVT and its complications. This variation suggests that while several nurses recognize the importance of DVT prevention, there is a need for continued training and institutional support to enhance favorable attitudes and encourage consistent preventive practices.

Nurses’ Practices Regarding DVT Prevention

Research carried out in different countries identified considerable differences in nurses’ DVT prevention practices. Study data from China (Ting Yan et al., 2021), India (Antony et al., 2016), and Pakistan (B Parveen et al., 2021) revealed that a noticeably lower proportion of nurses (14%–31.2%) had satisfactory DVT prevention practices, indicating unsatisfactory practices in most nurses, needing appropriate and evidence-based interventions to enhance this critical nursing role. In contrast, findings from South Korea and the Republic of Cyprus showed that more than half of nurses scored satisfactory DVT prevention practices (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018; Oh et al., 2017). Similarly, in Ethiopia, less than half (42.5%) of the nurses demonstrated satisfactory practices (Yesuf et al., 2021), although relatively higher than those mentioned above, necessitating further investigation, including influencing factors. These findings indicate that although DVT prevention is a vital component of nursing care, its practical implementation remains inadequate in many healthcare settings. This also highlights the need for strengthened training, monitoring, and further research to better assess the factors influencing prevention practices.

Factors Affecting Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice of DVT Prevention

Nurse-Related Factors

Numerous nurse-related and institutional factors have been shown to influence nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices of DVT prevention. Findings from China (Ting Yan et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2020a), Turkey (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018), Iraq (Najm et al., 2020), and Ethiopia (Kiflie et al., 2022) consistently reported that nurses’ higher level of education, leadership roles, and job positions were associated with adequate knowledge and better preventive practices (Kiflie et al., 2022). Similarly, nurses with more work experience and who received DVT education are more likely to have good knowledge (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018; Ting Yan et al., 2021; Yu-Fen et al., 2018) and adhere to recommended preventive measures (Kiflie et al., 2022; Oh et al., 2017; Yesuf et al., 2021). This illustrates that experience and targeted education may help identify risk factors and prevention strategies for DVT. Similarly, availability of clinical guidelines was associated with DVT prevention practices (Mohammed et al., 2018; Silva et al., 2020a; Yesuf et al., 2021). Availability of protocols may facilitate translation of knowledge into action, ultimately improving prevention practices.

Institution-Related Factors

Some studies indicated that the working unit was significantly associated with nurses’ knowledge (Yesuf et al., 2021; Yu-Fen, Yuan, Ya-Ping Chen, et al., 2018) and practice (Kiflie et al., 2022) of DVT prevention, showing that the nature of the clinical setting may influence nurses’ exposure to and experience with preventive measures. A study conducted in China showed that the absence of an institutional monitoring system was associated with poor implementation of DVT prevention practices among nurses (T. Yan et al., 2021). Similarly, evidence has shown that training is associated with adequate knowledge (Xu et al., 2020b) and better practice (Mohammed et al., 2018; Yesuf et al., 2021) of DVT prevention. Furthermore, various studies have consistently reported a significant association between workload and DVT prevention practices (Hebeshy, 2018; Mohammed et al., 2018; Silva et al., 2020a, Silva et al., 2020), where workload is often associated with reduced compliance with recommended preventive measures.

Methods

Study Design and Settings

A multicenter institutional cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to August 30, 2022, at three comprehensive specialized hospitals in the Amhara region, northeastern Ethiopia. These hospitals are the final referral choice for other health institutions and provide high admission rates and a high level of care.

The source population of the study was all nurses working in these selected hospitals. Study participants were all nurses working at these three comprehensive specialized hospitals during the study period. Nurses with 6 months and above work experiences who were working in surgical, medical, ICU, ART clinic, orthopedic, oncology, and emergency units were included in the study. Pediatric nurses, nurses who were seriously ill, on annual vacation, and on maternity leave at the time of data collection were excluded from the study.

Sample Size and Sampling Techniques

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was determined by using a single population proportion formula with a 95% confidence level, 5% desired precision, Z α/2 = 1.96 (normal standard deviation of 95% confidence level), and proportion (p = 42.5%) taken from previous study (Yesuf et al., 2021). n=(Za/2)2*p (1–p)/d2

n=(1.962*.425(1–0.425)/.052=375 . By adding a 10% non-response rate, the total sample size was 413 nurses.

Sampling Techniques

After the study hospitals were selected, the number of nurses in each hospital was obtained from the human resource management of the respective hospitals. The sample size for each hospital was calculated proportionally to obtain the total sample size. Then, a representative sample size for each working unit within each hospital was allocated proportionally, and the sampling frame was prepared for each unit of the selected hospitals. Finally, the study participants were selected from each working unit by a simple random sampling technique.

Study Variables

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices were the outcome variables of the study. The independent variables of the study were sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, and marital status); nurses’ related-factors (educational status, work experience, previous education on DVT, reading literature related to DVT, and job position); and institutional factors (working unit, workload, training on DVT, availability of protocols/guidelines, and monitoring systems with feedback).

To measure the outcome variables, researchers used mean-based categorization to facilitate comparison of findings with previous studies that used similar methods. Before that, normality of data distribution was checked using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and the data were normally distributed with (p = .32, .24, and .31) for the knowledge, attitude, and practice scores, respectively, confirming that the data did not deviate significantly from a normal distribution. Adequate Knowledge: Nurses who scored mean and above from the knowledge questions were considered to have adequate knowledge of DVT prevention (Yesuf et al., 2021; Yohannes et al., 2022b). Satisfactory Practice: Nurses who scored mean and above from the practice questions were considered to have satisfactory practice of DVT prevention (Yesuf et al., 2021; Yohannes et al., 2022b). Attitude refers to nurses’ beliefs, perceptions, and willingness to implement DVT preventive measures such as risk assessment, early mobilization, and prophylaxis (Ting Yan et al., 2021). Nurses who scored mean and above from the attitude questions were considered to have a favorable attitude toward DVT prevention (Wang et al., 2021). Workload: Participants who work > 39 hours a week were considered to have “workload”; otherwise, they had “no workload” (Yazew et al., 2021).

In this study, the term DVT education refers to the learning acquired through formal nursing academic programs, including those at the undergraduate and postgraduate stages, whereas DVT training refers to post-qualification educational opportunities, including on-the-job training, workshops, and continuing professional or nursing education programs focused on DVT prevention and management.

Activity monitoring system refers to the formal mechanism within the hospital to monitor, supervise, or assess nurses’ performance activities aimed at preventing DVT, including routine visits by supervisors, use of standardized checklists, audits, and feedback related to DVT control and prevention. Nurses who indicated that there was at least one formal mechanism for monitoring activity related to DVT prevention in their hospital were classified as having a DVT activity monitoring system, while those who indicated otherwise were classified as not having a monitoring system (Ivers et al., 2012).

Data Collection Tools and Procedures

Data were collected through a structured and pretested, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was adapted from related studies after an intensive literature review (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018; Antony et al., 2016; Yesuf et al., 2021; Yohannes et al., 2022b). Before the data collection, researchers involved experts in the field (two cardiology professors and two cardiac nurses), who critically reviewed the instrument, and it was finalized based on their feedback. The questionnaire consisted of four sections. The first section assessed sociodemographic, institutional and nurse-related factors. The second section of the questionnaire contains knowledge assessment questions regarding DVT prevention with 34 items using “yes,” “no,” and “do not know” responses. Each correct answer was assigned 1 point, while incorrect answers or “do not know” responses were assigned a score of 0. The total knowledge score ranged from 0 to 34. The observed mean ±SD = 25.8±5.6 and nurses who scored ≥ 25.8 were considered to have adequate knowledge, whereas those who scored below the mean (25.8) were considered to have inadequate knowledge. The third section evaluates nurses’ practices of DVT prevention with 13 items on three-point Likert scale: always = 2, sometimes = 1, never = 0. The overall practice score was calculated by summing up all items, and the total score ranged from 0 to 26. The observed mean ±SD = 17.9±7.2 and nurses who scored ≥ 17.9 were classified as having satisfactory practice, while those scoring below the mean (17.9) were considered to have unsatisfactory practice.

The fourth section contains attitude assessment questions regarding DVT prevention with 6 Likert-type items on a 4–point scale, from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (4). Since there were no negative attitude items, reverse scoring was not performed. The total attitude score was calculated by adding the scores for each question and the total score ranged from 6 to 24. The observed mean ±SD = 16.8±5.4 and nurses who scored ≥ 16.8 were viewed as having a favorable attitude. Those who scored below the mean (16.8) were considered to have an unfavorable attitude.

In order to assess the results of dichotomized knowledge outcome, sensitivity analysis was carried out using multiple linear regressions with original continuous knowledge scores as the dependent variable. The results were found similar to those of the main analysis. Age (B = 0.19, 95% CI [0.03, 0.35], p = .017), educational status (B = 2.43, 95% CI [1.29, 3.57], p < .001), DVT training (B = 3.58, 95% CI [2.24, 4.92], p < .001), DVT education (B = 3.16, 95% CI [1.78, 4.53], p < .001), and availability of guidelines (B = 2.23, 95% CI [1.88, 3.53], p = .001). Similarly, a sensitivity analysis was performed for continuous attitude score as the dependent variable to test the robustness of the results in the main analysis, which used the dichotomized attitude score. Educational status (B = 1.82, 95% CI [1.18, 2.84], p = .001), workload (B = -0.13, 95% CI [-0.22, -0.04], p < .001), and years of experience (B = 1.27, 95% CI [1.02, 2.49], p = .04). Moreover, a sensitivity analysis test for continuous practice score as the outcome variable identified that receiving DVT education, the level of knowledge, and activity monitoring system significantly associated with practice. DVT education (B = 1.24, 95% CI [1.03, 2.78], p = .010), activity monitoring system (B = 3.01, 95% CI [1.89, 4.15], p < .001), and knowledge (B = 1.39, 95% CI [1.22, 2.57], p = .02).

Data Quality Control

The questionnaire and the checklist were prepared in English prior to the data collection. The questionnaire was critically reviewed by a panel of experts before data collection. One day training was given to the data collectors and supervisors on the study objectives, data collection instruments, techniques, and procedures. A pretest was conducted among 20 randomly selected nurses at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, which is located outside the study area. A reliability test was performed, and corresponding Cronbach’s alpha values for knowledge, attitude, and practice items were 0.91, 0.87, and 0.89, respectively. The consistency and completeness of the data were checked by the principal investigator and supervisors on a daily basis.

Data Processing and Analysis

The data were checked for completeness, coded, entered into EpiData version 3.1, and then exported to SPSS 25 for analysis. Multicollinearity assumption for independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and there was no multicollinearity between independent variables (VIF ≤ 1.88).

The binary logistic regression model fitness was checked with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the model adequately fitted the data (p = .101, .94, and .59 for knowledge, attitude, and practice, respectively). Bivariate regression analysis was performed to select candidate variables for multivariate analysis, and independent variables with a P-value < .25 in the bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariate logistic regression analysis to control for potential confounders (Malhotra, 2020; Mulatu et al., 2020). The results of the final model were expressed using an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence level. The significance of associations was declared at p < .05 in the multivariate logistic regression. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were used to present the study results. Finally, the results were presented using tables and text, and reported in accordance with STROBE checklist standard.

Ethical Considerations

According to the Helsinki declaration, which was developed by World Medical Association, medical studies involving human participants, must be submitted to the research ethics committee for consideration, comment, guidance and approval before the research begins. To maintain the declaration during this research, the research proposal was submitted, and the ethical approval letter was obtained from the concerned University’s Institutional Ethical Review Committee (IERC) (Redacted). The letters of authorization were obtained from the respective comprehensive specialized hospitals included in the before actual contact with participants. After the participants were provided brief information about the study’s purpose and benefits, written informed consent was obtained for data collection. Confidentiality was maintained through anonymity, participation was voluntary, and no participant identifying information was collected.

Hospital Specific Prevalence of Knowledge, Attitude and Practice of Nurses

The study results revealed minimal variation among the hospitals in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices of DVT prevention. Nurses’ adequate knowledge regarding DVT prevention in Hospital 1, Hospital 2, and Hospital 3 were 56.9% (74), 56.9% (87), and 56.2% (68), respectively. A favorable attitude toward DVT prevention was reported by 59.2% (77), 58.8% (90), and 58.7% (71) of nurses in Hospitals 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Satisfactory DVT prevention practices were observed in 59.2% (77), 59.5% (91), and 59.5% (72) of nurses among the three hospitals.

Discussion

The main aim of this study was to examine nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, practices, and identify factors associated with DVT prevention. This study revealed that 56.7% (95% CI [51.8%, 61.5%] of nurses had adequate knowledge regarding DVT prevention, which was consistent with study reports from northwest Ethiopia (55.6%) (Yohannes et al., 2022a) and India (58%) (Antony et al., 2016).

The proportion of nurses with adequate knowledge regarding DVT prevention was higher than that reported in studies conducted in northwest Ethiopia (21%) (Yesuf et al., 2021), Egypt (27.5%) (Mohammed et al., 2018), the Republic of Cyprus (50%) (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018), Pakistan (31.2%) (Bushra Parveen et al., 2021), and South Korea (20%) (Oh et al., 2017). These discrepancies may reflect differences in study sites, participants’ characteristics, selection procedures, and data collection periods. In addition, while some of the studies were conducted within specific clinical areas, such as intensive care units, or were limited to one hospital, this study included nurses from multiple wards, which may affect the exposure of nurses to DVT prevention practices, contributing to these differences in their knowledge.

However, nurses’ knowledge of DVT prevention was lower than those reported in studies undertaken in China (77.81%) (Yu-Fen, Yuan, Ya-Ping Chen, et al., 2018). The possible explanation for this finding may be disparities in country-specific nursing curricula, access to continued education and current evidence-based DVT prevention protocols, and differences in organizational support for professional development. However, these factors were not examined in this study.

Regarding practices, more than half of the nurses demonstrated satisfactory DVT prevention practices. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in China (56.19%) (T. Yan et al., 2021). However, it was higher than reports from Gondar University hospital (39.4%) (Yesuf et al., 2021) and the Amhara region in northwest Ethiopia (48.8%) (Yohannes et al., 2022b). These variations may be partially explained by differences in prioritizations for DVT prevention in routine clinical practice and service organization across facilities. In addition, methodological differences, such as the single-institution study and the purposive sampling used in one of the comparison studies, may also have contributed to these differences.

Moreover, nurses’ practice levels were higher than that reported in studies carried out in India (14%) (Antony et al., 2016), South Korea (49%) (Oh et al., 2017), and the Republic of Cyprus (28.5%) (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018). Institutional factors, such as working environment, staffing level, monitoring systems and the availability of quality improvement initiatives, may contribute to these differences, although these factors were not assessed in the current study.

In multivariable regression analysis, previous DVT education was significantly associated with nurses’ preventive practices. Nurses who received DVT education were more likely to apply DVT prevention measures than those who had not. This highlights that providing structured and evidence-based education may help strengthen the implementation of preventive care in clinical settings. This is congruent with a study conducted in South Korea (Oh et al., 2017). Similarly, nurses who had adequate knowledge were more likely to practice DVT prevention than those with inadequate knowledge, suggesting that adequate knowledge helps enhance DVT prevention practice by improving adherence to the recommended interventions. This finding aligns with the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) theoretical framework, which asserts that knowledge is an important factor in determining health-related behaviors. Individuals with greater knowledge of health issues better recognize the importance of preventive measures and implement in their practice (Kilale, 2016). Similar trends were reported in studies conducted in Ethiopia (Yohannes et al., 2022b) and China (Xu et al., 2019).

Nurses’ practices in preventing DVT were significantly associated with an activity monitoring system. Nurses with perceived activity monitoring system were more likely to implement DVT prevention measures than those without perceived monitoring system (AOR = 4.06, 95% CI [2.50, 6.59], p = .001). This observation could imply that regular supervision motivates nurses to more reliably implement DVT prevention measures. A structured monitoring system offers continuous oversight and prompt feedback, for identifying clinical practice gaps, consequently enhancing compliance with institutional procedures. It may also promote a culture of accountability and continuous quality improvement, motivating nurses to routinely assess patients for DVT risk and implement appropriate preventive measures. However, studies showing significant association of activity monitoring system with nurses’ DVT prevention practices were not found.

Nurses who had received training on DVT prevention were more likely to have adequate knowledge than those who had not. This implies that training may contribute to improving the knowledge of nurses and keep them updated with the latest evidence, as has been reported in the Republic of Cyprus (Serpici & Gursoy, 2018) and China (Li et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2019). The availability of guidelines was positively associated with nurses’ adequate knowledge of DVT prevention, which aligns with a study conducted at Gondar University Hospital in northwest Ethiopia (Yesuf et al., 2021). Thus, nurses who had access to these guidelines were more likely to have adequate knowledge than those who did not. This finding underscores the need for institutions to have current guidelines readily available to facilitate nurses’ understanding and supporting evidence-based DVT prevention alongside service integration and policy implementation (Abuzied et al., 2024).

Furthermore, educational status was significantly associated with nurses’ level of knowledge regarding DVT prevention. Nurses with a BSc degree and higher educational status were more likely to have adequate knowledge than those with lower educational qualification. Similar findings have been reported in studies in Ethiopia (Yohannes et al., 2022a), China (Yu-Fen, Yuan, Ya-Ping Chen, et al., 2018), Iraq (Najm et al., 2020), and Turkey (Al-Mugheed & Bayraktar, 2018). This reflects that as nurses advance in their educational qualification, their knowledge may also improve. Therefore, supporting nurses’ continuing education may enhance their knowledge and promote implementation of quality patient care using evidence-based practices.

This study revealed that age was significantly associated with nurses’ adequate knowledge regarding DVT prevention. Nurses in age category of 36 years and above were more likely to have adequate knowledge (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI [1.18, 6.31], p = .02) regarding DVT prevention than those below the 31 years. However, no comparable studies were found that specifically showed significant association between age and nurses’ knowledge regarding DVT prevention.

In this study, more than half of the nurses had a favorable attitude towards DVT prevention, which is consistent with study results reported in Egypt (56%) (Mohammed et al., 2018). However, this is lower than proportions reported in China (68.5%) (T. Yan et al., 2021). The observed variation may be attributed to professional and health system factors, including specific work units, differences in organizational culture and availability of supportive supervision, which may affect whether attitudes to evidence-based practices, such as DVT prevention, are encouraged.

The nurses with a BSc degree and higher were more likely to have a favorable attitude than those with lower educational qualifications. The findings indicate that education may shape attitudes about preventive care. Advanced nurses may be more familiar with the evidence-based practice, current clinical data, and disease prevention measures and thus may be more informed about DVT prevention. These findings were in line with studies conducted in China (Li et al., 2018; Yu-Fen, Yuan, Ya-Ping Chen, et al., 2018) and Iraq (Najm et al., 2020). Likewise, nurses with lesser workload were more likely to have adequate attitudes towards preventing DVT than those with workload. High workload may contribute to stress, fatigue and conflicting clinical demands that may make nurses less likely to maintain a positive attitude towards prevention, suggesting that adequate staffing and effective workload management are important to promote a positive attitude to preventing DVT. A similar trend was observed in a study carried out in China (T. Yan et al., 2021).

Moreover, nurses with 11 or more years of work experience were more likely to have a favorable attitude toward DVT prevention than those with less than 11 years of experience (AOR=1.85, 95% CI [1.02, 3.42], p = .04). This suggests that increased clinical experiences of nurses were significantly associated with more positive professional attitudes. Experienced nurses may be more aware of timely DVT prevention due to increased exposure to at-risk patients. They are also more likely to have gained practical knowledge and confidence through years of clinical practice, which may contribute to a more positive attitude toward DVT prevention. However, no comparable studies showing a similar trend were found.

Implications for Practice

The findings indicate that knowledge, attitudes, and practices of nurses regarding DVT prevention in the study area were suboptimal, highlighting the need for targeted and context-specific interventions. Nurses should pay attention to assessment and monitoring of hospitalized patients for DVT risk, prioritize high-risk patients, and implement evidence-based prevention measures in accordance with standard clinical guidelines. Hospitals should offer training opportunities, ensuring DVT prevention guidelines are available and accessible, address the workload of nurses, and establish routine monitoring and support systems. Together, these may enhance knowledge of nurses, promote positive attitudes, strengthen prevention practices, and improve patient outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study has several strengths. It employed adequate sample size, a multicenter study design, and addressed institution and nurses-related factors overlooked in previous studies in Ethiopia. On other hand, this study also has some limitations. A cross-sectional study design precludes establishing the causal association between the outcome and explanatory variables. The findings of this study may not be generalized to other study participants outside the study areas. In addition, mean-based categorization of outcome variables may risk information loss, and hospital and unit-level clustering analysis was not carried out. Self-reported practice may also have introduced social desirability bias. Future research with qualitative methods to explore barriers to DVT prevention practices among nurses should be conducted.

Conclusion

Nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding DVT prevention were suboptimal, indicating presence of gaps in DVT prevention practices. Previous DVT education, in-service training, availability of guidelines and educational level were significantly associated with nurses’ knowledge of DVT prevention, while workload and educational level were significantly associated with attitudes. Monitoring systems, previous DVT education and adequate knowledge were significantly associated with satisfactory practices of DVT prevention. Enhancing training opportunities, ensuring access to DVT prevention guidelines, strengthening routine monitoring and support systems, and managing workloads of nurses, may improve knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices regarding DVT prevention.
