Conventional therapies for colorectal cancer (CRC) are often limited by toxicity and therapeutic resistance. Alternative therapeutic strategies are therefore needed. Natural product-derived compounds have been explored as potential anticancer agents. The molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of JI-CS004, a chloroform fraction derived from the herbal formulation SH003, against CRC remain unclear. Methods: The anticancer effects of JI-CS004 were evaluated in CRC cell lines by assessing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, ER stress signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis. ROS dependency was examined using N-acetylcysteine (NAC). Antitumor activity was evaluated in an HCT116 xenograft model. Results: JI-CS004 substantially reduced CRC cell viability and induced intracellular ROS accumulation. ROS scavenging by NAC markedly attenuated JI-CS004–induced cytotoxicity, ER stress signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis, supporting an important contribution of ROS to these cellular responses. JI-CS004 activated ER stress-related signaling through the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 and IRE1α/JNK pathways, increased CHOP expression, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and OXPHOS complex expression, and induced intrinsic apoptosis accompanied by cytochrome c release and caspase-9/3 activation. In vivo, JI-CS004 substantially suppressed tumor growth and increased the expression of CHOP and cleaved caspase-3. Conclusions: JI-CS004 induces ROS-dependent intrinsic apoptosis associated with ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, supporting further investigation of its anticancer potential in CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health issue. In 2020, CRC accounted for 1.93 million new cases and 0.94 million deaths worldwide, ranking among the top three diseases in terms of incidence and mortality [1]. In 2025, CRC will rank third in estimated new cases and third to fourth in estimated deaths in the United States [2]. Demographic changes and risk factor patterns are projected to substantially increase the burden by 2040 [3]. Surgical resection is the definitive treatment for resectable CRC, whereas non-resectable cases typically receive standard systemic treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy [4]. Various anticancer drugs have been developed for patients with CRC in recent decades [5, 6]. However, limited therapeutic efficacy and the emergence of drug resistance indicate an urgent need for novel treatment strategies [7–9].

Various chemotherapy regimens, such as folinic acid (leucovorin), 5-fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX); capecitabine and oxaliplatin (CAPOX); and folinic acid (leucovorin), 5-fluorouracil, and irinotecan (FOLFIRI), have been developed for patients with CRC, including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), oxaliplatin- and irinotecan-based doublets, and triplet intensification in selected patients. Regimens containing fluoropyrimidine derivatives, with 5-FU as the key component, continue to serve as the foundational therapies for patients with CRC [10–12]. Despite its widespread use, this regimen offers limited survival benefits and is often associated with drug resistance and adverse effects [13]. Combination and targeted therapies have recently been introduced to overcome these limitations; however, sustained responses are rare [14–16]. Therefore, novel treatment strategies to improve clinical outcomes in patients with CRC are urgently required.

SH003 is a standardized 30% ethanol extract of Astragalus membranaceus Fisch. ex Bunge, Angelica gigas Nakai, and Trichosanthes kirilowii Maxim. in a 1:1:1 ratio [17]. SH003 has been reported to exert anticancer effects in various cancers by suppressing angiogenesis via blocking VEGF–VEGFR2 binding [18], inducing cancer cell death via endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress- and autophagy-linked mechanisms [19], causing apoptosis by upregulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and activating the ER stress-mediated pathway, and exhibiting synergistic effects with docetaxel in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) models [20, 21]. In addition, SH003 alleviates docetaxel-induced neuropathic pain in vitro and in vivo chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain model [22]. A Phase I clinical trial (NCT03081819) demonstrated acceptable tolerability up to 4,800 and 9,600 mg/day [23, 24], and a recent multicenter Phase I study of SH003 combined with docetaxel demonstrated favorable safety [25].

ROS play a dual role in cancer biology, sustaining oncogenic signaling at moderate levels and triggering cell death when the oxidative load exceeds the adaptive capacity [26]. Under physiological conditions, ROS participate in redox signaling, which maintains cellular homeostasis; however, excessive ROS accumulation disrupts this balance and causes the oxidative modification of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [27]. In CRC, redox dysregulation is linked to tumor progression and therapeutic vulnerability [28]. Several natural compounds have been shown to induce apoptosis in CRC cells by promoting ROS overproduction and mitochondrial dysfunction, suggesting that deliberate modulation of oxidative stress is a promising therapeutic strategy [29]. The ER is a key ROS-responsive hub, where oxidative imbalance induces ER stress and the unfolded protein response (UPR) [30]. ER stress is caused by the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the ER. The UPR restores ER homeostasis by limiting new protein entry, boosting chaperone-mediated folding, and removing misfolded proteins via ER-associated degradation (ERAD). The UPR signaling pathway is mediated by three ER transmembrane stress sensors: PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6 [31, 32]. Activation of the PERK arm leads to eIF2α phosphorylation, resulting in reduced global translation and increased ATF4 expression. Under persistent ER stress, this pathway contributes to the induction of CHOP, which promotes intrinsic apoptosis [33, 34]. Activation of the IRE1α arm splices XBP1 mRNA to produce XBP1s and activates JNK, a kinase that mediates apoptotic signaling during prolonged stress [35, 36]. Activation of the ATF6 arm promotes the translocation of ATF6 from the ER to the Golgi, where it is cleaved into an active transcription factor that induces the expression of genes involved in protein folding and ERAD. Under sustained stress, ATF6 can also contribute to CHOP induction and apoptotic signaling [37, 38]. Prolonged ER stress frequently converges in the mitochondria, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and intrinsic apoptosis. When ER stress impairs mitochondrial function, an increase in the cellular ADP/ATP ratio serves as an early and sensitive indicator of bioenergetic failure, as reduced ATP synthesis leads to elevated ADP levels [39]. This failure is typically followed by a loss of membrane potential (ΔΨm), suppression of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), which promote cytochrome c release and activation of caspase 9/3. Therapy-refractory CRC can acquire OXPHOS, rendering it particularly susceptible to agents that disrupt mitochondrial bioenergetics [40–43].

Despite these promising findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer activity of SH003 in CRC are incompletely understood, partly due to the complexity of its multi-component composition. To facilitate a more mechanistic investigation, fractionation using organic solvents can be used to reduce compositional complexity and enrich specific classes of phytochemicals based on polarity. In particular, chloroform partitioning preferentially isolates relatively non-polar, lipophilic compounds associated with redox modulation and cytotoxic activity [44]. Based on this approach, the present study focused on the chloroform fraction of SH003 (JI-CS004) to elucidate its mechanism of action in CRC cells. Whether JI-CS004 induced intracellular ROS accumulation, activated ER stress signaling, and promoted mitochondrial dysfunction leading to intrinsic apoptosis was investigated. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the cytotoxic effects of JI-CS004 and support its potential as a candidate for non-genotoxic anticancer therapy in CRC.

Materials and methods

Preparation of JI-CS004

The extract was prepared by mixing the SH003 components, A. membranaceus (wfo-0000185834), A. gigas (wfo-0000536198), and T. kirilowii (wfo-0000407970), in a 1:1:1 ratio (w/w) and adding 30% ethanol to 8–10 times the weight of the herbal ingredients. The plant names were checked with the World Flora Online database (https://www.worldfloraonline.org/) on November 12, 2025. The mixture was extracted at 80–100 °C, filtered to obtain the extract, and concentrated under reduced pressure below 60 °C to yield a 30% ethanol extract at Hanpoong Pharm & FOODS Co. (Jeonju, South Korea) under good-manufacturing-practice procedures. The batch number of SH003 was 19,002. The SH003 fractions were obtained by freeze-drying the SH003 extract, followed by repeated extractions (twice) with n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and butanol. Each fraction layer, including the aqueous layer, was concentrated under 140 torr at 60 °C and subsequently dried below 60 °C for 70 h. JI-CS004 (batch no. JRP-M1002) were dissolved using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (D8418; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a concentration of 25 mg/mL, centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min to obtain the supernatant and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Cell line culture

Human CRC cell lines DLD1, HT29, LoVo, and HCT116 were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, South Korea). CRC cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (LM011-03; Welgene, South Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (16000-044; Gibco, USA) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (LS203-01; Welgene, South Korea) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in an incubator. Cells were typically used at approximately 70% confluence for all experiments, unless otherwise indicated. The identity of the cell lines was verified by short tandem repeat profiling provided by the supplier. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination and cultured at passages below 10.

Cell viability assay

The 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (M2128; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used to investigate the effects of JI-CS004 on the viability of CRC cells. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 103 cells/well. Cells were treated with DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, or JI-CS004 at different concentrations (0.1–100 µg/mL) and incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h. After the incubation period, MTT was added to each well at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and incubated in the dark for 2 h. The formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO and absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Colony formation

For the colony formation assay, the cell lines were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells/well. Cells were treated with DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, or JI-CS004 at concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 1 µg/mL. The treatment period was extended to 7 d, allowing for adequate colony formation. Following treatment, the colonies were stained using crystal violet reagent (C0775; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and counted according to the method in the publication “Clonogenic assay of cells in vitro” [45].

Flow cytometry

CRC cells were seeded in 60-mm dishes at a density of 4.0 × 105 cells/dish. Cells were treated with 2.5 µg/mL of DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, or JI-CS004 for 48 h. After incubation, all cells including those in the culture medium were collected using trypsin-EDTA and washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). For apoptosis analysis using flow cytometry, cells were resuspended in 200 µL of 1× annexin V binding buffer (51-66121E; BD Pharmigen, USA) diluted with PBS. Cells were stained with annexin V-FITC (556420; BD Pharmingen, USA) for 30 min, followed by the addition of 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; ab66110; Abcam, UK) immediately prior to flow cytometry. Apoptotic DNA fragmentation was analyzed using the TUNEL Assay Kit (BrdU-Red; ab66110; Abcam, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and labeled with BrdU-dUTP in the presence of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT), followed by staining with an anti-BrdU-Red antibody. The 7-AAD was used for DNA counterstaining. TUNEL-positive cells, defined as cells exhibiting BrdU incorporation, were analyzed using flow cytometry. All flow cytometry analyses were conducted using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (B53000; Beckman Coulter, USA).

Western blotting

CRC cells were seeded in 60-mm dishes at a density of 4.0 × 10⁵ cells/dish. Cells were treated with 2.5 µg/mL of DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, or JI-CS004 for 24–48 h. After treatment, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, dithiothreitol, NaF, Na3VO4, and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were vortexed for 10 s and kept on ice for 10 min; this process was repeated three times. After centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant obtained from the lysed cells was quantified using the Bradford assay dye reagent (5000006; BioRad, USA). Protein samples were mixed with sample buffer and denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 5 min. Proteins were fractionated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a methanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (IPVH00010; Millipore, USA). The transferred proteins on the membrane were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies: PARP (9542, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology [CST], USA), cleaved caspase 3 (9661, 1:1000; CST, USA), cleaved caspase 9 (7237, 1:1000; CST, USA), Bak (12105, 1:1000; CST, USA), Bcl-2 (15071, 1:1000; CST, USA), PERK (5683, 1:1000; CST, USA), p-PERK(T980) (3179, 1:1000; CST, USA), eIF2α (9722, 1:1000; CST, USA), p-eIF2α(S51) (3398, 1:1000; CST, USA), ATF4 (97038, 1:1000; CST, USA), ATF6 (65880, 1:1000; CST, USA), IRE1α (3294, 1:1000; CST, USA), p-IRE1α(S724) (ab48187, 1:1000; Abcam, UK), XBP1s (40435, 1:1000; CST, USA), JNK1 (3708, 1:1000; CST, USA), p-JNK(T183/Y185) (4668, 1:1000; CST, USA), CHOP (sc-166682, 1:1000; Santa Cruz, USA), OxPhos Cocktail (45-8099, 1:500; Invitrogen, USA), and β-actin (3770, 1:1000; CST, USA). The following day, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with anti-mouse (5450-0011, 1:5000) and anti-rabbit (5450-0010, 1:5000) secondary antibodies (SeraCare, USA). Protein bands were detected using the EZ-Western Lumi Pico Kit (DG-WP100; DoGenBio, South Korea) or Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (WBKLS0500; Millipore, USA), and visualization was conducted using a developer and fixer in a dark room. Band densitometry was conducted using ImageJ v1.54p (National Institutes of Health), and the relative protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using a mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (ab113850; Abcam, UK). Briefly, 2.0 × 105 cells were seeded in 96-well black/clear bottom plates. Cells were treated with 2.5 µg/mL of DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, or JI-CS004 for 24 h or with 100 µM carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP) for 4 h. The cells were stained with 20 µM JC-1 (lyophilized) for 30 min. After washing with the buffer, fluorescence was detected using a fluorescence microplate reader at an excitation/emission wavelength (Ex/Em) = 475/590 nm (aggregated) and Ex/Em = 475/530 nm (monomer). The JC-1 ratio was presented as aggregated fluorescence/monomer fluorescence.

ADP/ATP ratio assay

The ADP/ATP ratio was determined using an ADP/ATP ratio assay kit (MAK135; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells were seeded in 96-well white/clear bottom plates at a density of 2.0 × 105 cells/well. Cells were treated with 2.5 µg/mL of either DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, or JI-CS004 for 24 h. After treatment, the culture medium was removed and ATP reagent was added to each well. The initial ATP luminescence, referred to as RLUA was recorded 1 min after adding the ATP working solution. The ADP-converting enzyme mix was then added to each well to convert ADP to ATP. The ADP-converting enzyme mix was added to convert ADP to ATP, followed by a 10 min incubation. The second luminescence, RLUB, was measured, and the stop solution was added to record the final luminescence, RLUC. The ADP/ATP ratio was calculated using the following formula:

ADP/ATP ratio = (RLUC – RLUB)/RLUA (1).

Determination of cellular ROS

Cellular ROS levels were determined using the 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA)/H2DCFDA-Cellular ROS Assay Kit (ab113851; Abcam, UK). The cells were seeded in 96-well black/clear bottom plates at a density of 2.0 × 10⁵ cells/well. Cells were treated with 2.5 µg/mL of DMSO (vehicle control), 5-FU, JI-CS004, or 200 µM tert-butyl hydrogen peroxide (TBHP, positive control) for 24 h. The cells were stained with 20 µM DCFDA for 45 min. After washing with buffer, fluorescence was detected using a fluorescence microplate reader at an Ex/Em of 485/535 nm.

In vivo studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Kyung Hee University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (KHSASP-25-080) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Six-week-old male, BALB/c, nude mice were purchased from NARA Biotech (Seoul, South Korea) and housed in a specific-pathogen-free animal facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22–23 °C at 40–60% humidity. The mice were acclimated for 1 w before the experiment. HCT116 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse in a 100 µL mixture of PBS and Matrigel (CLS356231; Corning, USA) (1:1 ratio). After tumor establishment, the mice were randomly assigned to five groups. Each group consisted of seven mice. The mice were treated with JI-CS004 or 5-FU at the specified doses. JI-CS004 was administered at three doses (30, 120, and 300 mg/kg). JI-CS004 was dissolved in a vehicle composed of PEG400:100% EtOH: Tween-80:10% glucose in saline at a ratio of 6:3:1:20 and administered orally (100 µL). The negative control group mice received the vehicle only. The 5-FU (30 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally three times per week. Both drugs were added in saline and 100 µL was administered per injection. Body weight and tumor volume were measured three times per week. The tumor volume was calculated using the following formula:

Tumor volume = (length × width²) × 1/2 (2).

After 21 d of treatment, the mice were euthanised using CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation. Tumor tissues were excised, measured, and photographed for further analysis.

Histological analysis

Harvested tumors were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for paraffin processing, embedded in paraffin blocks, and sectioned at 7 μm. For histological analysis, the sectioned tissues were stained with hematoxylin and eosin under identical conditions following deparaffinization and rehydration. For immunohistochemical staining, antigen retrieval was conducted by boiling the sections in Tris-EDTA (pH 9.0) at 90 °C for 30 min. The samples were cooled for 20 min. The slides were treated with an ABC-HRP Kit (PK-4000; Vector Laboratories, USA) for 30 min and blocked using goat serum for 1 h. Staining for Ki-67 (ab16667, 1:100; Abcam, UK), cleaved caspase 3 (9661, 1:100; CST, USA), and CHOP (sc-166682, 1:100; Santa Cruz, USA) was conducted by overnight incubation at 4 °C followed by staining with the secondary antibody for 1 h. The expression of each molecule was analyzed using a 3’-diaminobenzidine substrate kit (SK-4100; Vector Laboratories, USA) and slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. Histological analyses were conducted using a microscope (Scope. A1, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

LC-MS/MS analysis of indicator compounds

The reference compounds, bergapten, osthenol, isopimpinellin, and cucurbitacin D, were each dissolved in methanol (MeOH) at a concentration of 1,000 mg/L (10 mg in 10 mL) to prepare stock solutions and subsequently diluted to 100 mg/L with MeOH to obtain working standard solutions. SH003 extract and JI-CS004 were dissolved in MeOH (10 mg/mL), sonicated, and filtered through a 0.45-µm PTFE membrane filter prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was conducted using an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm; Agilent Technologies). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in distilled water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B), with gradient elution as follows: 0–1 min, 1% B; 1–3 min, linear increase to 90% B; 3–6 min, maintained at 90% B; 6–7 min, returned to 1% B; and 7–13 min, equilibrated at 1% B. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 10 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using an Agilent 6410 Triple Quadrupole LC-MS/MS system equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in positive ion mode. The operating parameters were: gas flow, 10 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 30 psi; gas temperature, 350 °C; and capillary voltage, 4,000 V. Quantitative analysis was conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode using protonated precursor ions ([M + H]+) and corresponding product ions. The MRM transitions and collision energies were: cucurbitacin D (m/z 517.5, product ions at m/z 499.3 and 481.3 with collision energies of 5 and 7 V, respectively), isopimpinellin (m/z 247.1, product ions at m/z 217.1 and 232.1 with collision energies of 20 and 10 V, respectively), and bergapten (m/z 217.2, product ions at m/z 174.1 and 202.1 with collision energies of 30 and 20 V, respectively). Data acquisition and processing were conducted using the manufacturer’s software.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was conducted at least twice. Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. One-way ANOVA was used to assess the differences among treatment groups at a single time point, whereas two-way ANOVA was applied to data involving two independent variables, time and treatment. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v8.0.2 (GraphPad Software). The results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of n experiments. *P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

JI-CS004 induces cytotoxic effects in CRC cell lines

To evaluate cytotoxic activity, the cell viability of JI-CS004 in the human CRC cells (DLD1, HCT116, LoVo, and HT29) treated with increasing concentrations (0–100 µg/mL) for 24, 48 and 72 h was measured. The 5-FU was used as the positive control. In all cell lines, viability declined at higher concentrations and longer exposure times (Fig. 1A). The IC50 values of JI-CS004 were lower than those of 5-FU at 24 h and 48 h, whereas at 72 h, the two treatments showed similar IC50 values in each cell line (Table 1). JI-CS004 reduced the colony size and colony count in DLD1, HCT116, and HT29 cells (Fig. 1B). To determine whether the observed loss of viability prevented apoptotic cell death, the pro-apoptotic effects of JI-CS004 in CRC cell lines were assessed using annexin V/7-AAD staining. Relative to the vehicle control, 2.5 µg/mL JI-CS004 increased the proportion of apoptotic cells (annexin V positive) in all CRC cell lines, whereas 5-FU induced comparable or higher annexin V positive populations depending on the cell line. No marked difference between JI-CS004 and 5-FU was observed in DLD1 and HCT116, whereas 5-FU induced a substantially higher apoptotic population than JI-CS004 in HT29 (Fig. 1C, D). To confirm apoptotic cell death, a TUNEL assay using BrdU-Red labeling was conducted. Relative to the vehicle control, JI-CS004 substantially increased the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells in all CRC cell lines. In DLD1 cells, JI-CS004 and 5-FU induced comparable levels of TUNEL positivity, whereas in HCT116 and HT29, 5-FU induced a substantially higher proportion of TUNEL-positive cells than JI-CS004 (Fig. 1E, F). The levels of apoptosis-related proteins were also measured in CRC cell lines treated with JI-CS004. JI-CS004 induced considerable PARP cleavage in all CRC cell lines. Treatment with 5-FU also increased PARP cleavage relative to the control (Fig. 1G, H). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S1. Taken together, these results suggest that JI-CS004 inhibited proliferation and induced apoptotic signaling in CRC cell lines.

JI-CS004 promotes ROS-dependent cytotoxicity in CRC cell lines

To assess whether JI-CS004 induced intracellular ROS production, 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCF-DA) fluorescence was measured using tert-butyl hydrogen peroxide (TBHP) as a positive control. After 24 h of exposure, JI-CS004 increased the intracellular ROS levels in the CRC cell lines, whereas 5-FU induced an increase only in DLD1 cells, with no marked changes observed in HT29 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 2A). Pretreatment with NAC alone reduced DCF-DA fluorescence and completely inhibited JI-CS004-mediated ROS induction (Fig. 2B). To determine whether ROS mediated the cytotoxic effects of JI-CS004, cells were co-treated with NAC and JI-CS004 and cell viability and apoptosis were evaluated. NAC pretreatment restored cell viability (Fig. 2C) and substantially reduced apoptotic cell populations, as determined using annexin V/7-AAD staining (Fig. 2D, E). Furthermore, a TUNEL assay using BrdU-Red labeling demonstrated that JI-CS004-induced DNA fragmentation was substantially suppressed by NAC treatment (Fig. 2F, G). These findings indicate that JI-CS004 induces intracellular ROS in CRC cell lines, and that its cytotoxic and apoptotic effects were at least partly dependent on ROS.

JI-CS004 activates ER stress signaling in CRC cell lines

Because JI-CS004 increased the intracellular ROS levels in CRC cells (Fig. 2), the markers of ER stress and UPR activation were examined. JI-CS004 treatment increased the phosphorylation of PERK, eIF2α, IRE1α, and JNK and the protein levels of ATF4 and XBP1s, consistent with the activation of the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 and IRE1α/XBP1s/JNK arms of the UPR. Although 5-FU treatment increased the expression of some of these proteins, the extent of the increase was substantially lower than that observed for JI-CS004 (Fig. 3A, B). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S2. The ATF6 levels did not show any appreciable increase under the same conditions. The CHOP levels were substantially increased. Collectively, these results indicate that JI-CS004 induced ER stress in CRC cell lines, primarily engaging the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 and IRE1α/XBP1s/JNK signaling pathways.

JI-CS004 induces mitochondrial dysfunction mediated by ER stress in CRC cell lines

Given that prolonged ER stress is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, key indicators were evaluated to confirm this effect. Cellular energy status was assessed using the ADP/ATP ratio, the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (ΔΨm), and the integrity of respiratory complexes by quantifying representative OXPHOS subunits. The ADP/ATP ratio was increased after JI-CS004 treatment. The 5-FU treatment increased the ADP/ATP ratio, but this increase was less than that observed for JI-CS004 (Fig. 4A). The JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio decreased after JI-CS004 treatment. The 5-FU treatment decreased the JC-1 ratio but to a lesser extent than JI-CS004 (Fig. 4B). Immunoblotting indicated decreased levels of representative mitochondrial OXPHOS subunits after JI-CS004 treatment, indicating the potential impairment of respiratory chain function, whereas 5-FU treatment did not cause a marked change (Fig. 4C, D). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S3. To assess whether ER stress contributed to mitochondrial dysfunction, cells were treated with JI-CS004 and 4-PBA. Co-treatment substantially attenuated the JI-CS004-induced increase in the cellular ADP/ATP ratio and decrease in the JC-1 ratio, although both results remained substantially different from those of the control (Fig. 4E, F). Together with ER stress activation, these findings indicate that JI-CS004 induced mitochondrial dysfunction in CRC cells, and that this effect was at least partly dependent on ER stress.

JI-CS004 activates intrinsic apoptosis in CRC cell lines

ER stress with CHOP activation (Fig. 3) and mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 4) prompted an assessment of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. To this end, whether JI-CS004 induced intrinsic apoptosis was investigated. JI-CS004 treatment increased Bak and decreased Bcl-2 protein expression, indicating a shift toward apoptosis in the balance of Bcl-2 family proteins (Fig. 5A, B). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S4A. Consistent with this, analysis of the cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions indicated cytochrome c redistribution, with increased levels in the cytosol and decreased levels in the mitochondria (Fig. 5C, D). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S4B. The cleaved caspase 9 and 3 protein expression levels were elevated (Fig. 5E, F), indicating activation of the intrinsic caspase cascade. Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S4C. Although 5-FU treatment generally resulted in lower expression levels of these intrinsic apoptotic proteins than with JI-CS004, the cleaved caspase 3 protein expression levels were higher in the 5-FU than in the JI-CS004 treatment group. Collectively, these results indicate that JI-CS004 activates intrinsic apoptosis.

NAC attenuates JI-CS004-induced ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis in CRC cell lines

To confirm whether the JI-CS004-induced ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis were related to intracellular ROS generated by JI-CS004 treatment, CRC cells were pretreated with the antioxidant, NAC, to scavenge intracellular ROS. Whether JI-CS004 still induced these effects under ROS-scavenging conditions was assessed. The relationship between JI-CS004-induced ER stress and intracellular ROS levels was confirmed. JI-CS004 treatment substantially increased the phosphorylation of PERK, eIF2α, IRE1α, and JNK, as well as the protein levels of ATF4 and XBP1s. These changes were attenuated by co-treatment with NAC (Fig. 6A, B). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S5A. Whether JI-CS004 induced mitochondrial dysfunction and increased the intracellular ROS levels was determined. The increased ADP/ATP ratio and decreased JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio and OXPHOS subunits in the mitochondria after JI-CS004 treatment were found to be partially reversed by co-treatment with NAC (Fig. 6C–F). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S5B. The relationship between JI-CS004-induced intrinsic apoptosis and the intracellular ROS levels was confirmed. JI-CS004 treatment increased the protein expression levels of Bak and decreased the protein expression levels of Bcl-2. Both effects were attenuated by co-treatment with NAC (Fig. 6G, H). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S5C. The redistribution of cytochrome c from the mitochondria to the cytosol induced by JI-CS004 treatment was reversed by NAC co-treatment (Fig. 6I, J). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S5D. Consistently, the increased expression levels of cleaved caspase 9 and 3 after JI-CS004 treatment were attenuated by co-treatment with NAC (Fig. 6K, L). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Figure S5E. Taken together, these results indicate that the intracellular ROS generated by JI-CS004 contributed to ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis in CRC cell lines.

JI-CS004 suppresses xenograft tumor growth

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of JI-CS004, an HCT116 xenograft model was established using 5-FU as a positive control (Fig. 7A). Tumor growth was inhibited in the JI-CS004 treatments groups (30, 120, and 300 mg/kg) and the 5-FU group (30 mg/kg) compared to the negative control (NC) group. Tumor volumes were substantially reduced relative to the NC group in all JI-CS004 and 5-FU treatment groups (Fig. 7B, C). Within the JI-CS004 group, the tumor volumes in the high-dose (300 mg/kg) group were markedly lower than those in the low-dose (30 mg/kg) group, whereas the middle dose (120 mg/kg) group was similar to both low- or high-dose groups. Compared to the JI-CS004 group, the 5-FU group substantially exhibited reduced tumor volumes. At the endpoint, tumor weights exhibited patterns similar to those of tumor volumes, with substantially lower weights in the JI-CS004 and 5-FU groups than in the NC group; the lowest weight was observed in the 5-FU group. Unlike the tumor volumes, no statistical differences were detected among the different doses in the JI-CS004 and between the 5-FU and JI-CS004 groups (Fig. 7D). No marked differences were observed in body weight among the groups (Fig. 7E). To confirm whether JI-CS004 modulated tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis in vivo, immunohistochemical staining of the tumor sections was conducted (Fig. 7F, G). The proportion of Ki-67-positive nuclei was substantially lower in the JI-CS004 and 5-FU groups than in the NC group, with no statistical differences among the JI-CS004 doses or between the JI-CS004 and 5-FU groups. The proportion of cleaved caspase 3-positive nuclei was substantially higher in JI-CS004 and 5-FU than in the NC group and showed a dose-dependent increase in the JI-CS004 groups; the high-dose (300 mg/kg) group had a substantially higher positive ratio than the 5-FU group. CHOP-positive nuclei were substantially elevated in the middle- (120 mg/kg) and high-dose (300 mg/kg) JI-CS004 groups and in the 5-FU compared to the NC group. CHOP expression increased dose-dependently in the JI-CS004 groups and was substantially higher in the high-dose (300 mg/kg) than in the 5-FU group. Taken together, these results indicate that JI-CS004 exerted antitumor activity, characterized by suppressed growth with anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic efficacy and engagement of CHOP levels, in the HCT116 xenograft in vivo mouse model.

Cucurbitacin D reproduces JI-CS004 associated ROS accumulation, ER stress signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis in CRC cell lines

To investigate whether specific phytochemicals contributed to the anticancer effects of JI-CS004, the major constituents present in SH003 and JI-CS004 were analyzed. The presence of indicator compounds, including osthenol, bergapten, isopimpinellin, and cucurbitacin D, in SH003 and JI-CS004 was confirmed by chromatographic analysis. The retention times of these compounds in SH003 and JI-CS004 were consistent with those of the corresponding reference standards, confirming their identities (Fig. S6). Quantitative analysis indicated that all four compounds were markedly enriched in JI-CS004 compared to SH003 (Table S1). Osthenol exhibited the most pronounced increase, with its content rising from 20.45 ± 0.39 mg/kg in SH003 to 796.39 ± 17.69 mg/kg in JI-CS004, corresponding to a 38.94-fold increase. In addition, cucurbitacin D increased from 108.29 ± 4.55 mg/kg to 1,007.71 ± 55.55 mg/kg (9.30-fold), whereas isopimpinellin and bergapten increased by 8.20-fold and 4.87-fold, respectively.

The cytotoxic effects of JI-CS004 and the selected phytochemicals were compared in CRC cell lines. All compounds reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. However, cucurbitacin D exhibited a reduction pattern most similar to that of JI-CS004 (Fig. S7A). Consistently, annexin V/7-AAD flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that cucurbitacin D and JI-CS004 substantially increased the proportion of apoptotic cells across all tested CRC cell lines compared to the control (Fig. S7B). TUNEL assay results further confirmed that both treatments markedly induced DNA fragmentation, indicating apoptosis induction (Fig. S7C). These findings suggest that cucurbitacin D may contribute to the pro-apoptotic effects of JI-CS004. To determine whether cucurbitacin D also reproduced the underlying molecular mechanisms of JI-CS004, the intracellular ROS levels and ER stress-associated signaling pathways were evaluated. DCF-DA fluorescence analysis indicated that both JI-CS004 and cucurbitacin D substantially increased intracellular ROS levels in all CRC cell lines (Fig. S8A). Both treatments enhanced the phosphorylation of PERK, eIF2α, IRE1α, and JNK, with increased expression of ATF4 and XBP1s, indicating activation of ER stress signaling pathways (Fig. S8B). Given the close link between ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, MMP was assessed using JC-1 staining. Treatment with JI-CS004 or cucurbitacin D resulted in a marked decrease in the JC-1 aggregate-to-monomer ratio, indicating MMP loss (Fig. S8C). Immunoblot analysis indicated that both treatments reduced the expression levels of representative mitochondrial OXPHOS subunits, suggesting impairment of mitochondrial respiratory function (Fig. S8D). Apoptosis-related signaling was assessed. Both JI-CS004 and cucurbitacin D increased the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein, Bak, while decreasing the anti-apoptotic protein, Bcl-2, indicating a shift in the balance of Bcl-2 family proteins toward apoptosis. Cleaved caspase 9 and cleaved caspase 3 levels were substantially elevated, demonstrating activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway (Fig. S8E). These results suggest that cucurbitacin D may contribute, at least in part, to the anticancer effects of JI-CS004 by reproducing its ROS-associated ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptotic signaling in CRC cells.

Discussion

CRC is one of the most common malignancies worldwide [1]. Standard first-line treatment involves fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy (5-FU or capecitabine) combined with oxaliplatin or irinotecan in regimens such as FOLFOX or FOLFIRI [5]. However, these regimens are associated with severe toxicity and frequent development of drug resistance, leading to treatment failure in many patients [46]. Although targeted therapies (EGFR and VEGF inhibitors) and immunotherapies have been developed to overcome these limitations, their clinical benefits are modest, and many patients eventually relapse or cannot receive these treatments [5]. These limitations highlight the need for new treatment strategies.

SH003 is a standardized herbal formulation composed of A. membranaceus, A. gigas, and T. kirilowii. We reported that SH003 exhibits multi-target anticancer activity by modulating multiple signaling pathways in various cancer cells [18, 19, 47–49]. Also, SH003 can overcome drug resistance [50] and exhibits synergistic effects with conventional chemotherapy [20, 21, 51]. Previous study characterized the chemical composition of SH003 and identified several phytochemicals within the formulation [18]. We conducted solvent fractionation to identify the components contributing to the anticancer activity of SH003, and found that the chloroform fraction of SH003 (JI-CS004) was enriched in several phytochemicals (bergapten, isopimpinellin, cucurbitacin D, osthenol) and exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity against colorectal cancer cell lines compared to SH003. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of JI-CS004 in CRC remain unclear. Understanding these mechanisms may provide mechanistic insight into the anticancer activity of JI-CS004 and support the evaluation of its therapeutic potential. Therefore, the present study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of JI-CS004 in CRC.

This study suggests that ROS accumulation is a key mediator contributing to JI-CS004-induced ER stress signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptosis. Although these findings support a central role for ROS, the precise directionality between ROS accumulation and ER stress signaling cannot be fully resolved using NAC alone. JI-CS004 markedly activated the PERK and IRE1α arms of the UPR, whereas total ATF6 protein levels were not appreciably altered. Because ATF6 activation requires proteolytic cleavage and nuclear translocation, total ATF6 levels alone cannot indicate whether this branch was activated or suppressed [52]. Thus, the present data support engagement of the PERK and IRE1α pathways but do not permit a definitive assessment of ATF6 involvement, which would require evaluation of ATF6-dependent transcriptional activity. Furthermore, attenuation of mitochondrial dysfunction by 4-PBA further supports the involvement of ER stress in JI-CS004-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Among the four phytochemicals enriched in JI-CS004, cucurbitacin D most consistently reproduced the cellular responses induced by JI-CS004. These findings suggest that cucurbitacin D may be a candidate bioactive constituent contributing to the anticancer activity of JI-CS004. However, the isolated compounds and JI-CS004 were tested at identical nominal mass concentrations rather than at concentrations reflecting the actual abundance of each constituent within the fraction. Therefore, the quantitative contribution of cucurbitacin D to the overall activity of JI-CS004 could not be determined. In addition, because interactions among the constituents were not evaluated, whether their effects are additive, synergistic, or independent remains to be established. Taken together, these findings provide mechanistic evidence supporting the anticancer activity of JI-CS004 in CRC cells.

Although the present findings indicate that JI-CS004 can activate oxidative stress-induced apoptotic pathways, its clinical application requires further evaluation of safety, pharmacokinetic properties, and therapeutic efficacy. Future investigations should include pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiling to assess its translational potential. JI-CS004 appears to induce apoptosis through a ROS dependent mechanism, whereas 5-FU acts primarily through DNA damage [12]. This mechanistic distinction raises the possibility that JI-CS004 may retain anticancer activity in cellular contexts in which DNA damage based therapy is limited; however, this possibility was not evaluated in the present study. Because all experiments were conducted using 5-FU sensitive CRC cell lines, further studies using 5-FU resistant or patient derived models are required to evaluate the activity of JI-CS004 in chemoresistant CRC. Furthermore, bioassay-guided fractionation will be necessary to identify the specific active compounds responsible for the ROS-dependent anticancer effects of JI-CS004.

Conclusion

JI-CS004 induced ROS-dependent intrinsic apoptosis through ER stress-associated mitochondrial dysfunction in CRC model. These findings provide mechanistic evidence for the anticancer activity of JI-CS004 and support further investigation of its pharmacological and therapeutic potential in CRC.
