The mechanisms by which tumor-derived extracellular vesicles and particles (EVPs) promote vascular permeability during premetastatic niche formation remain unclear. Here, we show that tumor EVPs rapidly induce vascular leakiness within 1 h of administration in female mice, creating a permissive environment that enhances metastatic seeding. Rather than acting directly on endothelial cells, EVPs activate NF-κB and JAK–STAT signaling in interstitial macrophages, leading to IL-6 secretion and increased vascular permeability. Interstitial macrophage depletion markedly reduces EVP-induced vascular leakiness and metastasis. We identify extracellular vesicle-associated integrin α5 (ITGα5) as a major functional determinant of this process, promoting macrophage activation and IL-6 secretion without affecting EVP uptake. EVPs derived from colorectal cancer tumors with high ITGα5 similarly induce macrophage IL-6 secretion and vascular permeability. Together, these findings define an EVP–macrophage–IL-6 axis that drives vascular permeability during premetastatic niche formation and identify EVP-associated ITGα5 as a key mediator of metastatic progression and a potential therapeutic target.

Dror et al. show that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles and particles reprogram interstitial macrophages during the formation of the lung premetastatic niche, thereby increasing vascular permeability and promoting metastatic potential.

Main

Metastasis is a critical phase of tumor progression, remaining a primary challenge in treating cancer and a major cause of cancer mortality1. Therefore, elucidating the fundamental molecular and cellular processes driving discrete stages of metastasis is critical to identify novel therapeutic targets. To establish metastasis, tumor cells must first intravasate into the circulation, survive in the blood circulation, then extravasate into distal tissues and colonize them2. The extravasation of malignant cells into distant tissues can be facilitated by vascular permeability3. The lung is highly efficient at trapping circulating cancer cells in capillaries because of size restriction and, thus, is a frequent site of metastasis4. Lung vascular endothelial cells (ECs) provide both physical and immunological barriers to fluids, proteins and cells5. To extravasate from the bloodstream into distant organs, cancer cells disrupt endothelial barrier integrity, leading to increased vascular permeability5. The primary tumor facilitates metastasis through the secretion of soluble factors and extracellular vesicles and particles (EVPs), which create a supportive microenvironment in distant organs. Our previous work demonstrated that premetastatic niche (PMN) formation precedes the arrival of cancer cells6–9. Vascular permeability is a hallmark of lung PMN formation and represents an early step in the cascade leading to pulmonary metastasis10–12. Consequently, upon reaching the PMN, cancer cells exploit the increased vascular permeability to infiltrate the organ and establish metastatic lesions. Accordingly, strategies aiming at reducing vascular endothelial permeability have shown promise in diminishing the metastatic burden10,11,13.

EVPs, which contribute to PMN formation, are a diverse group of nanoscale vesicles actively released by cells14. EVPs selectively package cargo comprising proteins, RNA, DNA and lipids and act as vehicles in long-distance intercellular communication, both in normal physiology and in pathology, particularly in cancer development and metastasis15,16. Previous work from our group demonstrated that, within 24 h of tumor EVP administration, vascular leakiness is detected in both the lungs and the brains of naive animals17. However, the mechanism by which tumor-derived EVPs induce vascular permeability remains unexplored.

Previous studies have demonstrated that cancer-cell-secreted soluble factors, including VEGF, TGFβ and TNF, are potent inducers of vascular permeability11,18,19. In addition, endothelial apoptosis20 and necroptosis21 promote permeability and cancer cell extravasation. EVPs also compromise the endothelial barrier through multiple mechanisms. EVP-associated microRNAs increase vascular permeability by disrupting adherens and tight junctions or through actin remodeling13,22,23, while EVP-transferred proteins further modulate these processes24,25. These findings highlight the importance of endothelial integrity as a barrier to extravasation. However, the molecular mechanisms by which EVPs regulate vascular permeability across cancers and tumor progression remain unclear.

Here, focusing on the lung as the site of future metastasis, we set out to dissect the events that initiate vascular permeability during PMN formation, by comparing cancer models characterized by varying levels of vascular permeability. Surprisingly, we found that cancer-cell-specific EVPs modulated vascular permeability through their effect on lung interstitial macrophages (IMs), rather than a direct effect on ECs. The ability of EVPs to induce vascular leakiness depended on their integrin α5 (ITGα5) expression. Specifically, ITGα5-enriched EVPs activated NF-κB and STAT3 signaling in IMs, which triggered the secretion of IL-6, in turn inducing vascular permeability in ECs. Furthermore, we demonstrated that lung-tropic EVP-induced permeability was accompanied by an increased incidence of extravasation and metastasis. Taken together, these findings reveal that tumor EVPs promote premetastatic vascular permeability through an ITGα5-dependent macrophage–endothelial signaling axis, thereby facilitating cancer cell extravasation and metastatic seeding, and identify this pathway as a potential target to limit metastasis.

Results

Tumor orthotopic models and tumor-derived EVPs induce varying degrees of vascular leakiness in the lungs

Given the lung’s susceptibility to metastasis and vascular leakiness, a hallmark of the lung PMN, we sought to identify tumor types capable of inducing pulmonary vascular permeability. We compared metastatic models with lung tropism (B16F10 melanoma, K7M2 osteosarcoma and 4T1 breast cancer) to the nonmetastatic 67NR breast cancer line. To assess vascular leakiness, we orthotopically implanted these cancer cell lines into mice. Lung permeability was evaluated 14 days later, a time point corresponding to PMN formation. We next examined the earliest time point when permeability changes could be detected, which was as early as 2 days following B16F10 implantation. Notably, increased tumor volume at later time points did not further increase vascular leakiness (Fig. 1b,c).

Our observations also suggested a potential correlation between the preferential extravasation of high-molecular-weight dextran around larger blood vessels, rather than microvessels, within the lung. To investigate this phenomenon further, we stained lung tissue of mice bearing 14 days orthotopic tumors for von Willebrand factor (vWF), which is strongly expressed in the endothelium of veins and arteries but not of capillaries26, along with VE-cadherin, which is homogeneously expressed in all lung endothelia27 (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1a). Our findings revealed that over 80% of leakiness was observed surrounding the larger vWF-expressing blood vessels rather than the capillaries (Fig. 1e).

To investigate whether vascular permeability occurred in veins and/or arteries, we performed staining for endomucin, which is expressed in the venous and capillary endothelium but not in most arterial endothelia28. We observed similar levels of dextran leakiness around both veins and arteries in the lungs of B16F10 tumor-bearing mice 14 days following tumor implantation (Fig. 1f). This finding indicates that macrovessels are susceptible to tumor-induced vascular leakiness at very early stages of cancer progression, emphasizing the fundamental role of vascular permeabilization during the initial stages of metastasis.

We previously showed that vascular leakiness is detected in the lung 24 h after melanoma B16f10-derived EVP administration29. Thus, we examined the earliest time points when vascular leakiness could be observed and found similar permeability at 1 h and 24 h following EVP injection (Extended Data Fig. 1b). As the 1-h time interval would enable us to investigate the specific effect of EVP-induced vascular leakiness separately from other potential effects of EVPs (such as immune cell recruitment and ECM remodeling), we focused on this time point for further investigation.

To validate whether tumor EVPs could induce vascular permeability to a degree similar to that observed in tumor-bearing mice, we isolated EVPs from B16F10 and K7M2 tumor-derived explants and administered them retro-orbitally into naive mice. Within 1 h, B16F10 and K7M2 tumor-derived EVPs induced significant vascular leakiness, comparable to tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1g). To test whether tumor-cell-derived EVPs are the major driver of vascular leakiness in vivo, we used Rab27a-deficient tumors, which lack a key regulator of EVP biogenesis30,31. Rab27a-deficient tumors induced reduced leakiness despite similar tumor size (Extended Data Fig. 1c). To validate whether cancer-cell-derived EVPs are sufficient to induce vascular leakiness, we isolated EVPs from multiple cancer cell lines and assessed their uptake and effects in the lung 1 h after injection. While all EVPs were taken up to varying degrees (Extended Data Fig. 1d), only B16F10 and K7M2 EVPs increased vascular leakiness, whereas EVPs from 4T1, Melan-A and primary osteoblasts did not (Fig. 1h). Consistently, EVP-depleted conditioned medium (CM) failed to induce vascular permeability, confirming that the effect is mediated specifically by EVPs (Extended Data Fig. 1e). To further exclude the contribution of copurified soluble contaminants, tumor-derived EVPs were purified by iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation32. EVP-enriched fractions were identified by transmission electron microscopy and western blotting (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g), with fractions 3–5 selected for in vivo experiments. Density gradient-isolated EVPs exhibited comparable ability to induce vascular leakiness relative to input preparations (Extended Data Fig. 1h), confirming that the observed vascular effects are mediated by EVPs.

Acute vascular leakiness promotes cancer cell extravasation and lung metastasis

Having observed increased vascular permeability within 1 h of EVP injection, we tested whether this process promotes cancer cell extravasation and lung metastasis. EVPs inducing different levels of leakiness were injected, followed 1 h later by tail-vein injection of corresponding tumor cells, and metastasis was assessed after 14 days (Fig. 2a). We found that B16F10 EVPs (high leakiness) increased metastatic foci compared to PBS, whereas Melan-A EVPs (low leakiness) had no effect (Fig. 2b). These findings were confirmed using luciferase-expressing B16F10 cells, with bioluminescence imaging recapitulating histological quantification (Extended Data Fig. 2a–c). Similarly, K7M2 EVPs increased metastatic foci, while 4T1 EVPs did not (Fig. 2c). To test whether EVP-induced leakiness facilitates extravasation of low-leakiness models, K7M2 EVPs were administered 1 h before 4T1 cell injection. To confirm that this effect is driven by vascular permeability, histamine was used as a positive control33 (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Histamine increased metastasis to an extent similar to EVPs, with no additive effect when treatments were combined (Extended Data Fig. 2e), suggesting a shared mechanism. Anti-histamine blocked histamine-induced but not EVP-induced permeability, indicating that EVP effects are histamine independent (Extended Data Fig. 2f). Together, these findings show that increased vascular permeability is sufficient to promote metastasis and represents an early EVP-driven process.

Prior studies showed that, following tail-vein injection, most B16F10 cells extravasate into the lungs by day 4, while nonextravasated cells likely die34,35. To determine whether increased metastasis reflects enhanced extravasation, tumor cells were labeled with a cytoplasmic dye before injection. The lung endothelium was visualized by retro-orbital injection of CD31–PE antibody, followed by perfusion and whole-organ three-dimensional (3D)36. We quantified the proportion of tumor cells extravasated into the lung parenchyma and multicellular tumor foci on day 4 (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 2g). A single dose of B16F10 EVPs 1 h before tumor cell injection significantly increased extravasation (Fig. 2g), whereas 4T1 EVPs had no effect. No difference in multicellular foci was observed between PBS and EVP-treated groups in either model (Fig. 2h), indicating that vascular permeability, rather than enhanced proliferation, primarily drives metastatic seeding.

Lung vascular leakiness is mediated by IMs

To understand how EVPs increase vascular permeability, we identified the lung cell populations taking up tumor-derived EVPs. Lipophilic dye-labeled B16F10 or K7M2 EVPs were administered retro-orbitally and lung cells were assessed by flow cytometry 1 h later. CD31+ ECs accounted for ~30–40% of EVP+ cells, whereas CD45+ immune cells represented ~60–70% (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3a), as confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 3c). As the majority of EVPs were taken up by immune cells, we further characterized immune populations taking up EVPs in the lung (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 3b). Among immune populations, F4/80+ monocytes accounted for ~40% and macrophages for ~20% of EVP uptake (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 3b). To identify the IM subset taking up EVPs, we applied a refined gating strategy. We identified three IM subsets: IM1 (CD11C+MHCII+), IM2 (CD11C−MHCII+) and IM3 (CD11C−MHCII−)37. EVPs were preferentially taken up by IM1 and IM2, while IM3 contributed minimally (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 3c). Notably, IM1 and IM2 are tissue-resident macrophages localized near large vessels, consistent with sites of vascular leakiness (Fig. 1e), whereas IM3 is monocyte derived and parenchymal37,39, suggesting a role for resident IMs in mediating permeability.

As endothelial and immune cells constituted the primary populations taking up EVPs in the lung, we aimed to identify the specific cell type responsible for disrupting the endothelial barrier. To this end, we performed an in vitro permeability assay using primary lung ECs. Direct B16F10 EVP treatment did not alter endothelial permeability (Fig. 3f); therefore, EVPs do not affect ECs directly.

We next tested the requirement of immune populations by selective depletion. AMs were depleted using clodronate liposomes, IMs were depleted using anti-CSF1R, neutrophils were depleted using anti-Ly6G and monocytes were depleted using anti-Ly6C, with depletion efficiency confirmed by flow cytometry (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Depletion of AMs or neutrophils had no effect on EVP-induced permeability (Extended Data Fig. 4a). In contrast, IM depletion with anti-CSF1R significantly reduced vascular leakiness (>80% B16F10, >55% K7M2; Fig. 3g). While monocyte depletion did reduce vascular leakiness (23%), the effect was notably less profound compared to that of IM depletion (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Therefore, we concluded that although recruited monocytes contribute to the induction of vascular leakiness, IMs have a more substantial role in regulating vascular permeability. Thus, we focused our mechanistic studies on the IM population.

To assess whether IM-dependent permeability drives metastasis, we depleted IMs before EVP and tumor cell injection (Fig. 3h). IM depletion reduced metastatic burden by ~50% in both models (Fig. 3i,j). In an orthotopic tumor model, anti-CSF1R treatment every other day for 10 days reduced vascular leakiness without affecting primary tumor growth (Fig. 3k,l). IM depletion also significantly prolonged survival following EVP and tumor cell administration (Fig. 3m).

EVP uptake did not affect IM proliferation or phagocytosis (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). To determine whether EVP-responsive macrophages were tissue resident or monocyte derived, we assessed CCR2 expression on EVP+ macrophages. At steady state, CCR2 is expressed primarily on circulating monocytes and on the IM3 subset, which derives from recruited monocytes, whereas IM1 and IM2 are CCR2− long-lived tissue-resident macrophages37,40. Consistent with our uptake analysis, EVPs were predominantly internalized by CCR2− IM1 and IM2 populations (Extended Data Fig. 4d), indicating that EVP-responsive macrophages are tissue resident rather than newly recruited. These data suggest that tumor-derived EVPs directly reprogram resident IMs without inducing their expansion or recruitment.

To test whether IM-derived factors mediate permeability, IMs were isolated and treated with B16F10 EVPs (1 µg ml−1, 3 h) and their CM was applied to EC. CM from EVP-treated IMs significantly increased EC permeability compared to control CM (Fig. 4b). Similarly, CM from K7M2 EVP-treated IMs increased permeability relative to 4T1 EVP-treated IMs (Extended Data Fig. 5b), indicating tumor-specific IM reprogramming.

To identify IM functional changes occurring in response to EVP uptake, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on flow-activated cell-sorted ex vivo EVP+ IMs (Supplementary Table 1). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed enrichment of the IL-6–JAK–STAT3, inflammatory and TNF–NF-κB pathways (Fig. 4c, Extended Data Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table 2). Consistently, EVP+ IMs downregulated M2-associated genes (Cd206, Retnla, Egr1 and Cd163) and maintained or increased the expression of M1 markers (Cd40, Cd86, Gpr18 and Cd68), indicating a shift toward a proinflammatory state (Extended Data Fig. 5d).

To identify effector cytokines, we set to identify the specific cytokines induced in IMs by the uptake of EVPs, by conducting a cytokine array assay and measuring RNA expression. Compared to 4T1 EVPs, B16F10 and K7M2 EVPs increased secretion of IL-6, CXCL2, CCL3 and TNF (Fig. 4d, Extended Data Fig. 5e). Consistently, EVPs that do not induce leakiness (4T1 and Melan-A) did not alter cytokine mRNA expression, whereas B16F10 and K7M2 EVPs significantly upregulated IL-6, CXCL2, CCL3 and TNFα transcripts in IMs (Extended Data Fig. 5f). Cytokine induction was detectable within 1 h and increased by 3 h, indicating rapid transcriptional activation (Extended Data Fig. 5g).

To assess the functional relevance of cytokine upregulation, mice were treated with neutralizing antibodies before EVP administration. Blocking CCL3 or TNF had no effect, while CXCL2 neutralization reduced permeability only in the B16F10 model. In contrast, IL-6 neutralization significantly reduced vascular leakiness in both B16F10 and K7M2 models (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 5h), identifying IL-6 as a key mediator.

The ability of IL-6 to induce vascular permeability is well established in vitro and in vivo41,42. Indeed, recombinant IL-6 increased vascular permeability in vivo and in human pulmonary artery ECs (HPAECs) (Fig. 4f,g) and disrupted VE-cadherin and ZO1 organization similarly to EVP-treated IM CM (Fig. 4h). Notably, IL-6 administration restored vascular leakiness in IM-depleted mice (Extended Data Fig. 5i), indicating that IL-6 acts downstream of IM activation and upstream of endothelial dysfunction. Lastly, IL-6 neutralization significantly reduced EVP-induced metastasis in both models (Fig. 4i,j), demonstrating that IM-derived IL-6 is required for vascular leakiness and metastatic seeding.

To determine which EVP protein(s) are involved in vascular permeability, we first compared the expression of known adhesion proteins from the GSEA adhesion gene set to the adhesion proteins identified in our MS results (Supplementary Table 5). CDH2, NCAM1 and ITGα5 were highly expressed in B16F10 and K7M2 EVPs but low or absent in controls (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Western blotting confirmed their enrichment in leakiness-inducing EVPs (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 6b). To assess function, CDH2 was knocked down and NCAM1 and ITGα5 were knocked out in tumor cells, with efficient reduction confirmed in cells and EVPs (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 6c). While CDH2 or NCAM1 knockdown had no effect (Extended Data Fig. 6d), ITGα5 loss significantly reduced lung vascular leakiness induced by B16F10 and K7M2 EVPs (Fig. 5d). Importantly, ITGα5 loss did not affect EVP characteristics, including markers (Extended Data Fig. 6e), morphology (Extended Data Fig. 6f), size distribution and number (Extended Data Fig. 6g), and uptake by lung cells, including IMs (Extended Data Fig. 6h,i). To evaluate whether EVP uptake depended on integrin–fibronectin interactions, we pretreated EVPs with a synthetic RGD peptide, which blocks ligand binding by several RGD-recognizing integrins, including ITGα5 (ref. 44). RGD treatment had no effect on total lung EVP uptake or on the proportion of EVP+ IMs, indicating that EVP internalization does not rely on RGD-binding ligand interactions (Extended Data Fig. 7a). To explore which EVP-associated integrins might facilitate lung uptake, we analyzed ITGα3 and ITGα4, which were differentially enriched in EVPs derived from various tumor cell lines and bind to non-RGD ligand sequences in laminin and fibronectin, respectively45,46. ITGα3 and ITGα4 contributed to EVP uptake, as their loss reduced lung uptake without affecting permeability (Extended Data Fig. 7b–e). These data suggest that ITGα5 is required for downstream signaling rather than EVP delivery.

Furthermore, orthotopic implantation of ITGα5-deficient B16F10 tumors did not alter primary tumor growth but significantly reduced lung vascular leakiness (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 7f). EVPs lacking ITGα5 also showed reduced permeability in vitro (Fig. 5f), decreased tumor cell extravasation (Fig. 5g) and reduced metastatic burden in both models (Fig. 5h,i).

Conversely, overexpression of ITGα5 in 67NR cells (which are poorly metastatic and induce minimal vascular permeability (Fig. 1a)) increased EVP ITGα5 (Extended Data Fig. 7g), enhanced vascular permeability (Fig. 5j) and induced IL-6 mRNA expression in IMs (Fig. 5k), increasing lung metastasis (Fig. 5l). In an orthotopic education model, preconditioning with ITGα5-deficient EVPs reduced metastasis despite increased primary tumor weight (Fig. 5m,n), further supporting a role for EVP-associated ITGα5 in metastatic progression. These data suggest that ITGα5-enriched tumor EVPs are necessary and sufficient to induce lung vascular permeability, facilitate tumor cell extravasation and, thus, promote metastasis.

To assess human relevance, we used human SK-Mel-192 melanoma EVPs, which express ITGα5 (Extended Data Fig. 8e). These EVPs induced vascular leakiness in mice, which was reduced upon AV3 treatment (Fig. 6b). While control EVPs increased permeability, CM from macrophages exposed to AV3-treated EVPs reduced endothelial permeability (Fig. 6c), indicating that ITGα5 inhibition can reduce endothelial permeability induced by human tumor EVPs. Next, we aimed to define mechanisms through which ITGα5 controls vascular permeability. We conducted RNA-seq of lung IMs treated for 3 h with ITGα5-knockout (KO) or control EVPs from either B16F10 or K7M2 (Supplementary Table 6). GSEA yielded consistent findings in both models; ITGα5 is required for activation of IL-6–STAT3, TNF–NF-κB and inflammatory pathways (Fig. 6d, Extended Data Fig. 8g,h and Supplementary Table 7), consistent with in vivo data (Fig. 4c). We next examined a cohort of 25 participants with colorectal cancer (stages 2–4) (Supplementary Table 9), isolating EVPs from primary tumors and matched adjacent tissues. Both sources had canonical EVP markers (CD81 and CD9) (Extended Data Fig. 9b), with similar size and concentration profiles (Extended Data Fig. 9c). However, tumor-derived EVPs exhibited higher ITGα5 expression (Fig. 7c,d). Functionally, tumor-derived EVPs induced significantly greater IL-6 secretion from macrophages than adjacent tissue EVPs (Fig. 7e). In vivo, EVPs from colorectal tumors but not matched adjacent tissues increased vascular leakiness in immunodeficient mice (Fig. 7f). Consistently, CM from macrophages treated with tumor-derived EVPs increased endothelial permeability, whereas adjacent-tissue-derived EVPs had no effect (Fig. 7g).

Collectively, our findings indicate that, while ITGα3 and ITGα4 mediate EVP uptake, ITGα5 drives vascular permeability through NF-κB activation, leading to IL-6 secretion from IMs. This disrupts endothelial barrier integrity, promoting tumor cell extravasation and lung metastasis (Fig. 7h).

Discussion

Previous studies established that the ability of EVPs to increase lung vascular permeability is primarily mediated through the direct effects of EVPs on ECs. This in turn results in remodeling of endothelial tight junctions and adherens junctions and direct transfer of EVP microRNAs to ECs13,49,50. Other studies have shown that EVPs can transfer to EC proteins that alter vascular permeability24,51.

We unveil a mechanism in which the direct uptake of EVPs by ECs does not have a discernible impact on vascular permeability. Instead, our findings point to an alternative, indirect mechanism whereby uptake of ITGα5-enriched tumor EVPs by IMs located close to the endothelium is essential for vascular leakiness in the premetastatic lung. We showed that, upon EVP uptake by IMs, EVPs stimulate the secretion of cytokines, primarily IL-6, to promote endothelial permeability41. The depletion of IMs or blocking IL-6 in vivo significantly reduced the ability of EVPs to induce vascular leakiness and concurrently reduced the number of metastatic lesions in mice. Moreover, tumor-derived EVPs derived from participants with colorectal cancer were enriched in ITGα5, induced IL-6 secretion in macrophages and promoted endothelial leakiness. Consist with these finding, high tumoral ITGα5 expression correlated with reduced overall survival across multiple cancer types. Together, these findings suggest that evaluating ITGα5 levels specifically in circulating EVPs represents an important future direction with potential diagnostic and prognostic relevance. While B16F10, K7M2 and 4T1 tumors are all capable of metastasizing to the lung, the ability to induce vascular permeability varies widely among these cancer models. Notably, despite the high metastatic potential of 4T1 tumors, 4T1-derived EVPs induce relatively mild vascular permeability. Thus, the metastatic ability of 4T1 tumors likely relies on other mechanisms, such as the recruitment of neutrophils52.

In our study, we identified a mechanism that implicates IMs as gatekeepers of metastatic progression. IMs and AMs constitute the two primary populations of resident pulmonary macrophages. In contrast to AMs, which reside in close proximity to the epithelial alveolar cells, IMs are predominantly situated within the interstitial space between the microvascular endothelium and alveolar epithelium37,39. This distinct localization underscores the potential influence that IMs may exert on the surrounding ECs. Compared to AMs, IMs are less frequent and relatively understudied, especially in cancer. Prior research suggested that IMs are capable of secreting proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF, in both mice and humans39, consistent with our current study. While we acknowledge that IMs are unlikely to act alone and additional lung-resident or recruited cells may participate in EVP-induced vascular remodeling, our findings demonstrate the predominant and central role of IMs in inducing vascular leakiness and subsequently promoting extravasation and metastasis. Our data identify IL-6 as the principal mediator of EVP-induced vascular leakiness within the early time frame. However, our neutralization studies also indicate that other cytokines likely act alongside IL-6 to modulate endothelial permeability and PMN formation. IL-6’s role in increasing endothelial permeability in vitro53, primarily by altering VE-cadherin and tight junction proteins, is well established54. Our findings that vascular permeability is induced by the secretion of IL-6 from IMs and blocking IL-6 results in a notable reduction in both vascular leakiness and metastasis in vivo not only support established in vitro findings but also suggest a mechanistic basis for how tumor EVPs can prime and exploit cell populations within the premetastatic microenvironment to facilitate metastasis.

While some integrins, such as ITGα4 and ITGα3, mediate EVP uptake in the lungs, we identified ITGα5 as the EVP cargo necessary and sufficient to induce vascular permeability and facilitate metastasis mediated by IMs. We uncovered a link between ITGα5 expression on EVPs and the induction of IL-6 secretion from IMs through NF-κB signaling. ITGα5 loss or inhibition in EVPs reduces vascular leakiness, cancer cell extravasation and metastasis to the lung, while overexpression of ITGα5 in EVPs promotes these processes. Nevertheless, even though ITGα5 has a central role in EVP-mediated IM activation, vascular leakiness was not completely abolished upon ITGα5 loss, suggesting that additional EVP components (proteins, lipids or RNAs) may contribute, warranting future investigation. The absence of ITGα5 on EVPs does not impair the ability of IMs to uptake EVPs but significantly hinders activation of NF-kB, STAT signaling and IL-6 secretion, which consequently diminishes their effect on adjacent vasculature. Although our data demonstrate that ITGα5-containing EVPs activate NF-κB signaling in IMs, the precise receptor–ligand interactions underlying this response remain to be fully elucidated and represent an important direction for future mechanistic studies.

ITGα5 is upregulated in a spectrum of tumors and is closely associated with tumor progression and metastasis55–57. Additionally, ITGα5 has also been linked to angiogenesis and correlates with heightened immune infiltration within the tumor microenvironment58,59. Although the precise role of ITGα5 in influencing vascular permeability remains unexplored, some evidence suggests an increase in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and TNF, in tumors that express elevated levels of ITGα5 (refs. 59,60). We confirmed that this elevation of proinflammatory cytokines is associated with an increase in permeability in vitro.

Our research also details the kinetics of vascular permeability induction by both tumors and their EVPs and reveals a remarkably short time frame for the induction of lung vascular permeability. This finding provided an opportunity to delve into the specific influence of a single EVP dose on the permeability of lung ECs and the extravasation of cells, while minimizing the confounding effects associated with EVP treatment. This is in contrast to previous studies demonstrating the importance of vascular leakiness following tumor growth or multiple injections of EVPs in vivo13,23, which could introduce other variables, such as immune infiltration and modulation, angiogenesis and ECM remodeling15. Although our analyses show rapid NF-κB activation followed by IL-6 induction, we did not fully map the temporal sequence of these events and, therefore, acknowledge that the kinetic relationship between integrin signaling and cytokine release remains to be clarified. Our research highlights the potent impact of a single EVP dose, emphasizing the critical role of vascular leakiness in the processes of extravasation and metastasis.

In summary, our study offers a perspective regarding the influence of IMs in mediating the effects of tumor-derived EVPs on both vascular permeability and the progression of metastatic events. We identify interactions among EVPs, IMs, IL-6 and ITGα5 and elucidate their important roles in the regulation of vascular permeability and the advancement of metastatic processes, thus presenting several potential therapeutic opportunities.

Methods

Ethics approval

All procedures complied with relevant ethical regulations. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted in accordance with AAALAS guidelines (WCM protocol 0709–666A). Animals were monitored for health status and killed if required.

Human tumor samples were obtained from the Yonsei Cancer Center (YCC). All participants provided written informed consent allowing the publication of deidentified data, under protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine (IRB no. 4-2019-0811) and Weill Cornell Medicine (IRB no. 0604008488). All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The numbers of female and male participants were collected from the medical record and are indicated in the Supplementary Table 9. Sex was not used as a variable in data analysis and our findings do not apply to only one sex.

Cell lines and cell culture

B16F10 (CRL-6475), K7M2 (CRL-2836) and 4T1 (CRL-2539) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection, while SK-MEL-192 cells were obtained from the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (100 U per ml penicillin and 100 µg ml−1 streptomycin; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 67NR cell line (from F. Miller) was cultured in RPMI with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin–streptomycin. Melan-A cells (Wellcome Trust Functional Genomics Cell Bank) were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS, 1× penicillin–streptomycin and 0.2 µM 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (Sigma).

HPAECs (PromoCell, C-12241) were cultured in EC growth medium 2 (PromoCell). All experiments were performed using cells at passages 3–8. THP-1 Lucia cells were purchased from InvivoGen (thpl-nfkbv2). Primary osteoblasts and IMs were isolated from mouse bone and lung, respectively, as described below.

For EVP isolation, FBS was depleted of EVPs by ultracentrifugation at 100,000g for 4 h. Cells were then cultured in EVP-depleted medium for 3 days and supernatants were collected. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and routinely tested negative for Mycoplasma.

EVP isolation and characterization

EVPs were purified by sequential ultracentrifugation61. Cell contamination was removed from CM at 3–4 days or tissue explant supernatants by centrifugation at 500g for 10 min, followed by 3,000g for 20 min and 12,000g for 20 min to remove apoptotic bodies and large vesicles. EVPs were pelleted at 100,000g for 70 min in 38-ml or 94-ml tubes (355631 or 355628, Beckman Coulter), washed in PBS and repelleted at 100,000g using 70Ti or 45Ti rotors (Beckman Coulter Optima XE or XPE) at 10 °C. Final EVP pellets were resuspended in PBS and quantified by BCA assay (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For tissue explants, primary tumors (B16F10 or K7M2) were minced into millimeter-sized pieces and cultured overnight in RPMI with 1× penicillin–streptomycin. Supernatants were collected and filtered through a 70-µm strainer.

EVP size and concentration were measured using ZetaView (Particle Metrix) for mouse samples or NanoSight NS500 (Malvern Instruments, 405-nm laser) for human samples.

For iodixanol density gradients, crude EVP pellets were resuspended and adjusted to 45% iodixanol (OptiPrep, 60% stock) in a final volume of 4 ml, loaded at the bottom of the tube and overlaid with 1-ml layers of 35%, 30%, 28%, 26%, 24%, 22% (×2), 20% and 10% iodixanol, followed by ~200 µl of PBS. Gradients were centrifuged at 141,000g for 16 h at 10 °C in an SW28 Ti rotor. A total of 12 1-ml fractions were collected from top to bottom, diluted in PBS and ultracentrifuged at 100,000g for 70 min at 10 °C to remove iodixanol. Pellets were resuspended in PBS for downstream analyses.

EVP labeling

EVPs were labeled with CellVue burgundy (eBioscience) or PKH67 (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, washed in 20 ml of PBS and pelleted by ultracentrifugation (100,000g, 70 min, 10 °C).

Immunofluorescence staining of tissues and cells

For tissues, two lung lobes per mouse were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight and then incubated in 30% sucrose in PBS overnight. Tissues were equilibrated in 1:1 30% sucrose and Tissue-Tek OCT compound for 1 h, embedded in OCT and frozen on dry ice. Cryosections (10 μm) were processed using standard immunofluorescence protocols. Sections were dried, blocked (PBS with 3% BSA and 0.2% Triton X-100) and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 10) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 10) for 1 h, stained with DAPI (1 µg ml−1) and mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36970).

For HPAECs, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 2 min and stained as above.

Images were acquired using an LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss) with a ×40 differential interference contrast objective and analyzed using Zen Blue (Zeiss).

Histological analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C and subjected to paraffin embedding. Paraffin-embedded lung tissues were sectioned at 7-µm thickness and sections were processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and mounted with VectaMount medium (Vector Laboratories). Slides were scanned by PANNORAMIC 250 Flash (version 2.6, 3DHISTECH) with a ×20 (numerical aperture: 0.8) objective (Zeiss). The number of metastases was counted manually and each lesion’s area was measured by FIJI software62.

Flow cytometry

Digestion and staining followed a previously published protocol38. Briefly, lung tissues were minced and then digested at 37 °C for 1 h with an enzyme cocktail: collagenase A (1 mg ml−1), dispase (1 mg ml−1) and DNaseI (0.1 mg ml−1) (Roche, Sigma-Aldrich). Single-cell suspensions were filtered and then washed with PBS containing 2 mM EDTA and 3% BSA. Isolated cells were subjected to red blood celllysis and incubated with the primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 10). To define cell viability, DAPI (Thermo Fisher) was used. Data were acquired using an Aurora (Cytek) and analyzed by FCS Express 7 Research Edition (De Novo Software).

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy was performed as previously described63. Briefly, EVPs (0.1 mg ml−1 in PBS) were adsorbed onto Formvar/carbon-coated grids for 1 min. Grids were then negatively stained using four sequential applications of 1.5% aqueous uranyl acetate with blotting between each application. Following staining, grids were air-dried and examined using a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope operated at 100 kV and equipped with a Veleta 2k × 2K charge-coupled device camera (Olympus-SIS).

IM isolation

Lungs were digested and stained as described above. IMs were stained with the listed antibodies (Supplementary Table 10) and sorted using a BD FACS Melody. Typically, 100,000–300,000 cells were collected from each lung. Cells were cultured in RPMI with 10% FBS, GM-CSF (20 ng ml−1), 1 mM sodium pyruvate and 1× penicillin–streptomycin for 24 h and then subjected to EVP treatment.

Primary osteoblast isolation and culture

Primary mouse osteoblasts were isolated from BALB/c mouse bones as previously described64. Briefly, bones (tibia, femur and humerus) from 7-week-old female mice were collected and flushed to remove bone marrow using αMEM (BioConcept) supplemented with 2.2 g NaHCO3, 1× penicillin–streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.375× MEM amino acids (BioConcept) and 10% EVP-depleted FBS.

Bone fragments were minced and cultured in 10-cm dishes with 10 ml of digestion medium containing 1 mg ml−1 collagenase II (Sigma, C6885) for 90 min at 37 °C. The medium was then replaced with basal medium to allow cell migration and attachment. After 3 days, cells were detached using collagenase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) followed by TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Osteoblast identity was confirmed at passages 3–5 by Alizarin Red-S staining (>97% purity). CM from passage 3–5 osteoblasts was collected for EVP isolation.

Monocyte enrichment and differentiation into macrophages

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donor buffy coats. Whole blood (20 ml) was diluted 1:1 with PBS, layered over 30 ml of Ficoll-Paque and centrifuged at 400g for 30 min at 20 °C (no brake). The plasma layer was removed and mononuclear cells were collected, washed in PBS, and filtered through a 40-µm mesh.

Cells were resuspended at 12.5 × 106 cells per ml in PBS containing 0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA and incubated with MACS CD14 MicroBeads (4:1 ratio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were washed, passed through a second column to increase purity and eluted.

CD14+ cells were cultured in macrophage SFM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1% human serum, penicillin (100 U per ml), streptomycin (100 µg ml−1) and GM-CSF (50 ng ml−1; PeproTech). Nonadherent cells were removed after 1 h and on day 3 by medium replacement. Macrophages were used for experiments on day 5 (ref. 65).

THP-1 Lucia reporter cell (NF-κB) assay

Cells were differentiated into macrophages by treatment with 100 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate for 24 h, followed by a 48-h recovery period in fresh medium. NF-κB-driven luminescence was measured using the THP-1-NF-κB-luc2 reporter assay kit (InvivoGen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For cytokine analysis, IMs or human macrophages were treated with EVPs (1 μg ml−1) for 1 h or 3 h. CM was collected and analyzed using either a Proteome Profiler mouse cytokine array kit (R&D Systems) or ELISA (human IL-6, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Signal intensities were quantified using ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad) or standard ELISA analysis.

Western blot analysis

Total cell lysate (TCL) and EVP lysate were generated by lysing cells in RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min, 4 °C. The clear lysate was mixed with SDS sample buffer. The samples (both TCL and EVPs) were boiled for 5 min and 2.5–10 μg of samples were separated on a Novex 4–12% or 4–20% Bis–Tris Plus Gel (Life Technologies) and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (0.45 μm; Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature followed by primary antibody incubation overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature. The blots were analyzed by ChemiDoc XRS+ (Bio-Rad) and analyzed by Image Lab (version 6.1, Bio-Rad). All antibodies used and their dilutions are listed (Supplementary Table 10).

In vivo models

Mice were monitored for adverse effects and sacrificed if required. BALB/cJ and C57BL/6 female mice (Jackson Laboratory), aged 7–12 weeks, were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size and no randomization or blinding was applied. Mice that died before study endpoints were excluded. All animals were housed in cages in a specific-pathogen-free environment, with a 12-h light–dark cycle, a temperature range of 20–25 °C and 30–70% humidity, in the animal facility at Weill Cornell Medical College.

BALB/cJ mice (6–8 weeks) were used for implantation of 4T1 and K7M2 cells and for treatment with EVPs derived from osteoblasts, 4T1 cells or K7M2 cells. C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were used for B16F10 implantation and treatment with B16F10 or Melan-A EVPs.

For tumor implantation, 5 × 105 67NR or 4T1 cells (50 µl of PBS) were injected into the mammary fat pad (BALB/cJ), 5 × 105 B16F10 cells (100 µl PBS) were injected subcutaneously into C57BL/6 mice and 1 × 106 K7M2 cells (10 µl PBS) were injected into the tibia (BALB/cJ). Control mice received PBS. The tumors did not exceed the maximum volume allowed according to our IACUC protocol (2 cm3) in any experiments.

For CSF1R blockade, C57BL/6 mice bearing B16F10 tumors received anti-CSF1R (BE0213) or IgG control (BE0090, BioXCell) intraperitoneally (200 µg per mouse) every other day starting 2 days after implantation (three doses in total).

For EVP education experiments, 10 µg of control or ITGα5-KO EVPs were injected retro-orbitally every other day for 3 weeks, followed by tibial injection of 1 × 106 K7M2 cells. Mice were killed 4 weeks later.

For experimental metastasis assays, 5 × 104 B16F10 or 4T1 cells were injected through the tail vein and metastases were assessed after 14 days; for K7M2, 5 × 105 cells were injected and mice were analyzed after 2.5 weeks. Where indicated, tumor cells were injected 1 h after EVP administration.

AMs were depleted by intranasal administration of liposomes or clodronate (Liposoma; 60 µl per mouse). Depletion efficiency was assessed after 72 h or followed by EVP treatment.

For in vivo neutralization, antibodies to CSF1R (BE0213), Ly6G (BE0320), Ly6C (BE0203), IL-6 (BE0046), TNF (BE0058) or IgG control (BE0090, BioXCell) were administered intraperitoneally (200 µg per mouse) 18 h before EVP treatment. Anti-CXCL2 (MAB452) and anti-CCL3 (AB-450-NA, R&D Systems) were administered at 50 µg per mouse.

For CM experiments, EVP-depleted medium (after centrifugation at 100,000g, 70 min) was concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 filters (10-kDa cutoff, Millipore) at 4,000g, 4 °C, to 100 µl per injection.

Human specimens and processing

Colon tumor samples were processed as previously described66. Fresh tumor tissues were obtained from participants with colorectal cancer undergoing curative resection at the YCC. Samples were placed in ice-cold PBS immediately after collection and processed promptly.

Tissues were minced and cultured for 24 h in serum-free RPMI supplemented with penicillin (100 U per ml) and streptomycin (100 µg ml−1). CM was collected for EVP isolation. EVP size and concentration were measured using the NanoSight NS500 system (Malvern Instruments) with a 405-nm laser. All EVP samples described in this section were fully used during the experimental procedures and are no longer available.

Permeability assays

For in vitro assays, HPAECs were seeded on collagen-coated transwells (3 µm) and grown to confluence. Cells were treated with EVPs or CM in the presence of rhodamine–dextran (25 µg ml−1, 70 kDa) for 60 min. Fluorescence in the lower chamber was measured (excitation: 555 nm, emission: 580 nm) and normalized to controls.

For in vivo assays, mice were injected retro-orbitally with EVPs (10 µg), followed 1 h later by rhodamine–dextran (0.5 mg, 70 kDa). After an additional 1 h, mice were perfused, lungs were isolated and fixed and dextran fluorescence was quantified by confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 880) and normalized to DAPI.

Pharmacological treatments

EVPs were incubated with the ITGα5 inhibitor AV3 derivative (rYYraTY, 20 uM), for 1 h before use. For NF-κB inhibition, human macrophages were pretreated with TPCA-1 (10 µM) for 30 min before EVP stimulation. Recombinant IL-6 (PeproTech) was used at 40 nM for in vitro assays or administered intraperitoneally (500 nM) 1 h before dextran injection in vivo. DMSO-treated samples served as controls where applicable. For histamine, mice were injected retro-orbitally with 1 mg of histamine, 10 minutes before EVPs/cells/dextran injection.

EVP adhesion assay

Fibronectin (Sigma) was coated at a final concentration of 10 μg ml−1 on 48-well plates at 37 °C overnight. Unbound fibronectin was removed by PBS washing. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. B16F10 and K7M2 EVPs were labeled with PKH67 lipophilic dye as previously described. After staining, EVPs were incubated with 20 µM AV3 or DMSO for 1 h. After 1 h, 10 µg of EVPs were added to the wells and allowed to adhere to the fibronectin-coated plates for 30 min. Unattached EVPs were removed by PBS washing. Fluorescence was measured with excitation at 480 nm and emission at 535 nm. The relative fluorescence calculated was compared to control conditions.

Ex vivo whole-lung imaging assay

B16F10 cells were labeled with CellTracker green CMFDA dye (Invitrogen, C2925) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled cells (5 × 105) were injected intravenously through the tail vein 1 h after EVP or PBS administration.

After 4 days, lungs were harvested and placed in a custom imaging chamber with a coverslip base (thickness: 0.16–0.19 mm). To visualize the endothelium, anti-CD31–PE antibody (50 µg kg−1; BioLegend, 102408) was administered retro-orbitally 5 min before sacrifice. Lungs were inflated with 0.5 ml of air and maintained inflated during imaging36,67,68.

Tumor cell extravasation was assessed by confocal microscopy and quantified either directly from fields of view or by 3D reconstruction of tumor cells and vasculature using Imaris software.

Generation of ITGα5 and NCAM1 KO in B16F10 and K7M2 cell lines

Gene KO in B16F10 and K7M2 cells was generated by lentiviral transduction with lentiCas9-Blast (Addgene, 52962) to establish stable Cas9-expressing lines. After 2 weeks, cells were transduced with lentiGuide-Puro (Addgene, 52963) encoding sgRNAs targeting mouse integrins (sequences in Supplementary Table 11), designed using CHOPCHOP (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/). Control cells were transduced with nontargeting sgRNA lentivirus.

Lentivirus was produced in 293T cells by cotransfection of expression vectors with packaging plasmids pMDLg/pRRE (12251), pMD2.G (12259) and pRSV-Rev (12253) from Addgene using TransIT-X2 (Mirus).

CDH2 knockdown was performed using ON-TARGETplus Mouse CDH2 siRNA (Horizon, L-040206-00-0005) with TransIT-X2 (Mirus). CM was collected 48 h after transfection. ON-TARGETplus nontargeting siRNA (Horizon, D-001810-10-05) served as a control.

MS and proteomics

EVP samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography (LC)–MS/MS as previously described61. Samples were dried by vacuum centrifugation and redissolved in 30–50 μl of 8 M urea, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 10 mm DTT. Following lysis and reduction, proteins were alkylated using 20 or 30 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma). Proteins were digested with endopeptidase Lys C (Wako) in <4 M urea followed by trypsination (Promega) in <2 M urea. Peptides were desalted and concentrated using Empore C18-based solid phase extraction before analysis by high-resolution/high-mass-accuracy reversed-phase (C18) nano-LC–MS/MS. Typically, 30% of the sample was injected. Peptides were separated on a C18 column (12 cm × 75 μm, 3-μm beads, Nikkyo Technologies) at 200 or 300 nl min−1 with a gradient increasing from 1% buffer B and 95% buffer A to 40% buffer B and 60% Buffer A in typically 90 min or 120 min (buffer A: 0.1% formic acid, buffer B: 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile). MS instruments (Q-Exactive, Q-Exactive Plus, Q-Exactive-HF or Fusion Lumos, Thermo Scientific) were operated in data-dependent acquisition positive ion mode. Data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (version 1.4.1.14) and Mascot (version 2.5). Relative protein abundance was calculated from the average area of the three most intense peptides. For proteins identified by multiple UniProt identifiers, values were collapsed to the protein level using the entry with the highest intensity.

Data were normalized using the limma package in R (normalizeBetweenArrays, quantile method). Heat maps and clustering were generated using GENE-E (Broad Institute). Pathway analysis was performed using Metascape (https://metascape.org/). No batch correction was applied.

For RNA extraction, RT–qPCR and RNA-seq analysis, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit with on-column DNase digestion (Qiagen).

For RT–qPCR, 100–500 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed using a high-capacity cDNA RT kit. Here, 10 ng of cDNA was used per reaction with SYBR green and gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 11). GAPDH served as an internal control.

For RNA-seq, IMs were isolated and sorted as described above. For in vitro experiments, IMs were treated with EVPs for 3 h before RNA extraction. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Library preparation was performed by the Weill Cornell Medicine Genomics Core using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Library Prep kit (PN 20040534) on an Agilent Bravo system. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 (paired-end reads, 50 cycles). Raw BCL files were converted to FASTQ using bcl2fastq (version 2.19).

Counts were normalized using DESeq2 (median-of-ratios method)69. Differential expression was determined using the log2 fold change and Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P values. Genes were ranked by the DESeq2 statistic (log2 fold change divided by its standard error) and GSEA was performed using FGSEA69. Regulatory network analysis was conducted using RegEnrich70.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 11. The statistical tests and P values are all indicated in the figures and/or legends. Data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m. Data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications8. All mice were randomly assigned to the experimental groups. No data were excluded throughout the studies. P < 0.05 was considered significant unless otherwise stated. A two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to analyze the difference between two groups. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for differences in the means of three or more groups. The Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test were used for comparing survival outcomes.
