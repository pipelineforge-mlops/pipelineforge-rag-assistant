The interplay between vegetation and meteorological drought within the growing season involves complex dynamics. A key aspect of evaluating ecosystem stability lies in precisely determining the drought trigger threshold that leads to vegetation degradation. However, the dynamic evolution pattern and driving mechanisms of the drought trigger threshold remain unclear. To address this, the present study was conducted in the Weihe River Basin (WRB), with the core growing season (defined as May to September) serving as the temporal scope. A novel assessment framework was established based on the Copula joint function to determine the drought threshold triggering vegetation loss, where the drought trigger threshold was defined as the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) at which the conditional probability of vegetation loss first reached or exceeded 0.5. Subsequently, the dominant drivers of the threshold’s regional differentiation were investigated by combining the CatBoost model with the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method. The results show that: (1) Vegetation response time to drought shows clear seasonal and spatial patterns. Seasonally, the share of 1–6 month rapid response rises from 22% in May to 52% in June–July, before shifting to 9–24 month longer lags in August–September. Spatially, rapid response is found in the northwest loess hilly area, whereas prolonged lags are found in the Guanzhong Plain and Qinling Mountains. (2) The drought trigger threshold for vegetation loss shows clear spatial and seasonal patterns. Upstream forests endured severe drought down to − 1.9, whereas downstream agriculture was sensitive to mild drought above − 0.9. Drought tolerance peaked in midsummer at − 1.67 during June–July and weakened to − 1.27 in May and − 1.23 in September. (3) Water factors are the main drivers of the drought threshold. Precipitation (PRE) dominates throughout the growing season. The findings establish a scientific foundation for managing ecological risks and optimizing water resources within river basins.

Introduction

The functionality of terrestrial ecosystems is highly dependent on vegetation cover. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), as a widely used remote sensing parameter of plant growth, is commonly used to evaluate vegetation dynamics and ecosystem responses to climate change1–4. Drought duration and severity are major drivers of vegetation loss5,6. Drought reduces NDVI primarily by inhibiting physiological activities (e.g., stomatal closure and reduced chlorophyll activity) and altering canopy structure7–9. Severe drought can cause long-term vegetation degradation, undermining ecosystem stability10,11. Vegetation greenness exhibits a lagged response to drought due to the cumulative nature of drought events12,13, and this sensitivity varies greatly across ecosystems14–16. However, most studies have focused on identifying key environmental factors17,18 rather than quantifying the critical drought severity at which vegetation loss becomes irreversible. Precisely determining this drought trigger threshold is therefore essential for quantifying ecological impacts and implementing risk management. In this study, the drought trigger threshold is defined as the minimum drought severity required to trigger a specified level of vegetation degradation, expressed as a SPEI value. A lower SPEI threshold indicates that vegetation can withstand more severe water deficits before degradation occurs, reflecting stronger drought resistance.

The proposal of the SPEI laid the methodological foundation for quantifying drought thresholds19,20. With the maturation of the theoretical framework, monitoring and assessment, and methodology of drought, research has advanced in depth: on the one hand, studies have gradually shifted from static characteristics such as drought intensity, frequency, duration, and impact range to the dynamic process of drought propagation12,21,22; on the other hand, more research has applied the Copula method to determine drought thresholds, promoting the application of statistical methods in threshold research23. Current research methods on drought thresholds mainly focus on the following aspects: (1) Frequency probability methods for single variables, which seek statistical extreme points or change points from the historical distribution of the variable itself, such as quantile methods, extreme value theory methods, time-varying threshold level methods, and change point detection methods based on regression models24–27; (2) Conditional probability methods for multivariate dependencies, which diagnose critical points of system states by analyzing changes in statistical dependencies or joint distributions between two or more variables, such as Copula-Bayesian models and covariance threshold methods28–31; (3) Stochastic process methods, which view drought states as stochastic processes evolving over time and define thresholds through state transition probabilities, such as Markov chain models32.

Among them, the Copula-Bayesian model not only establishes joint distributions but also handles complex nonlinear dependencies, overcoming the limitations of single-variable methods or simple linear assumptions, providing key technical support for the quantification of conditional probabilities and drought thresholds, and has been widely applied in the field of drought28. Although the Copula-Bayesian model excels at capturing statistical associations among variables, it struggles to reveal underlying non-linear driving mechanisms. Several critical research gaps remain unaddressed. First, most existing drought threshold studies rely on annual or seasonal aggregates of NDVI and SPEI, failing to capture the monthly-scale dynamic response of vegetation during the core growing season33,34. Second, the Copula-Bayesian framework does not disentangle the non-linear pathways through which environmental drivers modulate drought thresholds spatially. Third, comparative analyses among different vegetation types along a continuous climate gradient remain scarce, particularly regarding their differential drought threshold characteristics26,35. Consequently, the precise drought tipping point at which vegetation transitions from reversible stress to irreversible loss remains poorly quantified, especially in climate transition zones such as the WRB.

Therefore, investigating the drivers of drought thresholds is crucial for advancing our understanding of drought development mechanisms36. The CatBoost model can avoid overfitting and capture the dependencies between various environmental factors and thresholds when dealing with complex nonlinear relationships that traditional methods find difficult to handle37,38. By combining the SHAP methodology for further interpretation of driving factors, it is not only possible to quantitatively identify the dominant factors driving the spatial differentiation of drought thresholds but also to deeply reveal the specific patterns of how a single factor affects the threshold within different value ranges39. This significantly breaks through simple correlation analysis and shifts towards the explanation of mechanisms.

The WRB is located in the climatic transition zone of China40, and holds a significant position in the ecological protection and high-quality development strategy of the Yellow River Basin (YRB). It connects the arid and semi-arid regions in the north with the humid regions in the south, and features a continuous vegetation gradient from forest steppe to typical steppe. The Guanzhong region within the basin is the political, economic and cultural core of Shaanxi Province, supporting 64% of the province’s population. The ecological quality of this area directly affects the ecological security of the middle and lower reaches of the YRB and has important implications for regional water resource security. It is an ideal area for studying the nonlinear response of vegetation to drought. As a typical ecologically fragile area, the basin has frequently experienced seasonal and extreme drought events over the past few decades, posing a continuous threat to the vegetation ecosystem41. Therefore, it is urgent to establish an analytical framework for probabilistic assessment and drought threshold identification to systematically reveal the critical laws of vegetation response to drought in the WRB.

Given the research context outlined above, this paper concentrates its investigation on the WRB, which, with its unique status as the largest tributary of the YRB, constitutes a critical geographical unit for analysis. To operationalize this study, the percentile method and the SPEI were employed to depict vegetation loss levels and meteorological drought grades, respectively. The analytical core integrates the Copula-Bayesian model with the CatBoost model and SHAP algorithm. Aiming to construct a dynamic and quantifiable assessment framework, this research examines the underlying probabilities and critical thresholds of drought-induced vegetation loss during the core growing season (May to September) in the WRB. This study aims to: (1) determine the lag period of NDVI in response to meteorological drought across different months of the core growing season (May to September) and quantify the conditional probability of vegetation loss under different drought severities. (2) identify the drought trigger thresholds for vegetation loss, with particular attention to their spatial heterogeneity, seasonal dynamics from May to September. (3) elucidate the key drivers of threshold variation and how the importance of these drivers changes across the growing season.

Materials and methods

Study area

According to the principle of “the farthest and longest source”, the source of the WRB can be traced back to the Bird and Mouse Mountain in Gansu Province. It flows into the YRB at Fenglingdu Port in Tongguan, Shaanxi Province, and is a first-level tributary of the YRB (Fig. 1). The WRB is located between the typical Loess Plateau in the north and the Qinling Mountain area, which is the dividing line between the north and south of China. The region encompassing the mainstream of the WRB, the Beiluo River Basin (BLB), and the Jinghe River Basin (JRB) falls within a temperate monsoon climate zone. Its climatic profile is marked by potential evapotranspiration of 800–1000 mm per year, with averages of 400–800 mm for precipitation and 7.8–13.5 °C for temperature. The distribution of water and heat within the basin conforms to the zonal law, with precipitation and temperature decreasing successively from south to north. Within the context of a warming climate, the basin is increasingly characterized by recurrent water and drought disasters alongside ecological deterioration, consequently ranking it as one of China’s most ecologically fragile areas41.Fig. 1 Geographical location and topographic characteristics of the study area. Data

This paper adopts basic meteorological data of China from 2003 to 2020, which are sourced from the National Meteorological Science Data Center of China, and calculates potential evapotranspiration (PET) and SPEI based on the above basic meteorological data. These meteorological data were originally recorded at discrete station locations and were subsequently interpolated to a 1 km × 1 km grid using ordinary Kriging before resampling. The 30-m-resolution digital elevation model (DEM) data were obtained from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM), jointly conducted by NASA and NGA, and downloaded from NASA Earthdata. The DEM represents static topographic conditions. The data of vapor pressure deficit is sourced from National Tibetan Plateau / Third Pole Environment Data Center. The surface soil moisture data is derived from the Climate Change Initiative Soil Moisture product of the European Space Agency, available at the ESA Climate Office. The NDVI data were obtained from the National Earth System Science Data Center. This dataset provides monthly maximum value composites at a spatial resolution of 1 km for the period 2001 to 2024, derived from MODIS MOD13A2 imagery. The original data values range from − 0.2 to 1. To ensure temporal consistency with other datasets, the NDVI time series was subset to the common period of 2003 to 2020. For this study, the following preprocessing steps were applied to the NDVI time series. First, linear detrending was performed to remove long-term trends unrelated to drought. Second, missing values, which accounted for less than two percent of the time series, were filled via linear interpolation. No additional smoothing or outlier removal was applied, because the monthly maximum value composite method already minimizes the influence of clouds and atmospheric disturbances. It should be noted that due to the 1-km resolution, narrow water bodies such as rivers are represented as mixed pixels, and their NDVI values do not reflect pure water conditions. The land use data is based on the classification products extracted from the remote sensing images of the US Landsat 8 satellite from approximately 2015, with a spatial resolution of 30 m, which was treated as a static land cover representation for the study period, sourced from the United States Geological Survey. The above data were processed under the Python and ArcGIS platforms for projection transformation, resampling, clipping, raster matching, and time series extraction. All datasets were resampled to a uniform 1-km spatial resolution. Method

Maximum correlation coefficient method

The lagged response of vegetation to drought is a direct consequence of the latter’s dynamic and propagative characteristics. For the quantitative assessment of the vegetation response lag to meteorological drought, the maximum correlation coefficient method was applied42. Fundamentally, the method posits that vegetation response to drought is not instantaneous but delayed. Consequently, the response time is defined as the optimal lag interval at which the vegetation index and the drought index exhibit their strongest statistical association. The computational procedure for each grid cell involved two steps: First, a series of Pearson correlation coefficients was computed between the NDVI sequence (May–September) and simultaneous multi-scale SPEI time series (1, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months)43. Second, the SPEI time scale associated with the maximum correlation value was identified as the vegetation drought response time44.

Conditional probability

Based on the conditional probability equation of the Copula joint distribution (Eq. 1), the probabilities of different vegetation losses caused by different drought stresses are calculated. Different drought stresses are represented by different drought grades, namely, mild drought (− 1 < SPEI ≤ − 0.5), moderate drought (− 1.5 < SPEI ≤ − 1), severe drought (− 2 < SPEI ≤ − 1.5), and extreme drought (SPEI ≤ -2). A percentile-based classification scheme was established to grade vegetation productivity loss, with thresholds set at the 40th, 30th, 20th, and 10th percentiles of the NDVI distribution45. In this study, vegetation loss levels are ecologically defined by NDVI percentiles: the 40th percentile represents moderate stress, the 30th and 20th percentiles represent progressively severe degradation, and the 10th percentile reflects vegetation loss approaching canopy collapse. In this study, the optimal Copula function was selected from candidate families including Gaussian, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, Joe, and Student’s t, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method. For the SPEI marginal distribution, a normal distribution was assumed given its standardized nature. For the NDVI marginal distribution, the best-fitting distribution was automatically selected from 17 candidate families (including normal, lognormal, gamma, Weibull, and others) using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Trigger threshold

NDVI, as an important indicator of vegetation greenness and biomass46. Its spatiotemporal variation patterns are influenced by multiple driving factors such as PRE, TMP, PET, DEM, VPD, SSM, and WIND47,48. To accurately identify the triggering mechanism of vegetation greenness decline under drought stress and effectively separate the potential interference of other environmental factors on threshold estimation16, we developed a drought threshold assessment framework based on the Copula-Bayes method to calculate the drought trigger thresholds under different vegetation loss scenarios (Fig. 2). In this study, we performed outlier treatment and linear detrending on the NDVI time series of each pixel and constructed the joint probability distribution of SPEI and NDVI based on the Copula function49,50. The drought trigger threshold for vegetation loss was determined through iterative calculations. For each pixel, the range of SPEI was set as the mean ± 2 times the standard deviation of its historical values. Starting from the upper limit of the range (the weaker drought side), the SPEI threshold was decreased stepwise by 0.1 (moving toward more severe drought conditions), and the conditional probability was calculated. When the conditional probability first reached or exceeded 0.5, the corresponding SPEI value was taken as the drought trigger threshold. The value of 0.5 serves as a natural statistical decision boundary, indicating that the probability of vegetation loss exceeds fifty percent. In binary decision theory, this threshold minimizes the expected misclassification error under balanced loss functions. From an ecological perspective, this threshold marks the critical transition point at which vegetation becomes more likely to be degraded than not under the given drought condition, making it a reasonable criterion for identifying drought trigger thresholds for early warning and risk management. This threshold setting follows similar applications of conditional probability thresholds in drought studies based on the Copula-Bayesian framework45,51,52. If the condition was not met throughout the entire range, the SPEI value with the conditional probability closest to 0.5 was selected as the threshold.

Interpretable CatBoost-SHAP machine learning model

As an implementation of gradient-boosting decision trees, the CatBoost algorithm possesses the inherent capability to process categorical variables directly, thereby avoiding the need for conventional preprocessing such as converting them into dummy variables. This significantly reduces the workload of preprocessing and can better utilize categorical information, greatly reducing the risk of overfitting and providing high prediction accuracy. It can conveniently calculate SHAP values for model interpretation53. Numerous studies have shown that CatBoost can effectively capture the complex interactions among environmental factors, and thus this method is widely used in hydrology and environmental science54,55. We leverage the CatBoost model to analyze how environmental variables influence the drought trigger threshold for vegetation loss. The dataset was randomly split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets using a fixed random seed of 42. The CatBoost model was trained with the following hyperparameters: learning rate = 0.05, iterations = 1000, depth = 6, l2_leaf_reg = 3, and early stopping rounds = 50. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). The SHAP interpreter is then used to evaluate the contribution and impact of individual features on these predictions.

Results

Response time of vegetation to meteorological drought

To quantify the lag response characteristics of vegetation to meteorological drought across the Weihe River Basin during the growing season (May–September), we used the maximum correlation coefficient method to identify the dominant SPEI timescale at which the NDVI–SPEI correlation reached its maximum. The results of this analysis are presented in Fig. 3. Prior to correlation calculation, linear detrending and pre-whitening were applied to eliminate temporal autocorrelation, trend effects, and potential spurious correlations. Notably, only grid cells that passed the significance test at p < 0.05 were retained for analysis and mapping. Obvious seasonal differences in vegetation response time were observed from May to September, corresponding to three phenological stages: the early growing stage (green-up, May) (Fig. 3a), the peak growing stage (June–August) (Fig. 3b–d), and the senescence stage (September) (Fig. 3e). In May, vegetation response was dominated by medium and long response times of 9–12 months, with short response times of 1–6 months accounting for only 22% of the significant grid cells (Fig. 3a). During the vigorous vegetation growth in June and July, the proportion of significant areas with short response times of 1–6 months increased sharply to 52%, reflecting high sensitivity of vegetation to recent meteorological drought (Fig. 3b,c). From August to September, the dominant response time shifted toward longer lag levels (Fig. 3d,e), the 24-month timescale reached its highest proportion in August (Fig. 3d), and the overall proportion of medium-to-long response times of 9–24 months remained above 60% among significant grid cells (Fig. 3d,e).Fig. 3 Spatiotemporal patterns of vegetation response time (RT) and maximum NDVI–SPEI correlation coefficient (CC) with meteorological drought across the WRB from May to September. Spatial patterns of the maximum correlation coefficient for significant grids also exhibited distinct monthly variations (Fig. 3f–j). The northwestern loess hilly area showed a rapid response time of 1–6 months, while the Guanzhong Plain and Qinling Mountains presented a prolonged lag effect with response times concentrated at 9–24 months. Overall, vegetation response time and correlation strength to meteorological drought in the WRB displayed significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity within statistically significant regions (p < 0.05), which was closely regulated by vegetation phenology, root traits, and local environmental conditions.

The triggering probability of vegetation loss

As shown in Fig. 4, the watershed is predominantly characterized by NDVI values greater than 0.1. The absence of distinct negative NDVI patches for rivers is attributable to the mixed-pixel effect at 1-km resolution. This part of the study addresses the conditional probability of varying vegetation loss levels (NDVI < NDVI40th, NDVI < NDVI30th, NDVI < NDVI20th, NDVI < NDVI10th) under different drought scenarios. Here, a lower percentile threshold corresponds to a higher degree of drought-induced vegetation damage. The research results show that during the growing season, as drought stress intensifies, the proportion and spatial extent of pixels facing an extremely high probability of vegetation loss (P > 90%) both exhibit a significant expansion trend (Fig. 4), indicating that during the growing season, vegetation has a high demand for water. As drought severity increases, drought stress intensifies, and the vegetation’s regulatory mechanism is insufficient to cope, leading to increased vegetation vulnerability. When NDVI < NDVI40th, studies show that vegetation is most sensitive to water stress during the peak of its physiological activity in June. During this period, the proportion of high-risk areas for vegetation loss (P > 90%) exhibits a strong response to drought intensity, rising sharply from 0.57% under mild drought conditions to 8.72% under moderate drought, and reaching 19.15% and 23.33% under severe and extreme drought, respectively. In sharp contrast, from July to September, even as drought intensity increases, the spatial extent of high-risk areas does not show significant fluctuations (Fig. 4). Spatial patterns of drought-induced vegetation loss trigger thresholds

The drought trigger threshold ranges from high-value areas (blue) to low-value areas (red), corresponding to the trigger thresholds for no drought, mild drought, moderate drought, severe drought, and extreme drought in sequence. In areas where the drought threshold is higher, meaning the SPEI value is closer to zero, even a mild drought can cause vegetation loss, indicating that the vegetation is sensitive and has weak drought resistance. In areas where the drought threshold is lower, meaning the SPEI value is more negative, vegetation responds only under severe drought conditions, indicating strong drought tolerance. From a spatial distribution perspective (Fig. 7), when NDVI < NDVI10th in June, the upstream area has a low drought threshold, with SPEI values ranging from − 1.5 to − 1.9, meaning that only severe drought triggers vegetation loss, reflecting strong drought resistance and recovery capacity. In the middle reaches of the Loess Plateau gully area, the drought threshold is − 1.3 to − 1.6, with a general drought recovery capacity. In the downstream area, the drought threshold is relatively high, with SPEI values ranging from − 0.5 to − 0.9, indicating poor natural drought resistance (Fig. 7b). From a temporal perspective (Fig. 8), when NDVI < NDVI10th, the average drought trigger thresholds for May to September are − 1.27, − 1.67, − 1.65, − 1.45, and − 1.23, respectively. The drought trigger thresholds in June and July are relatively low, meaning that more severe water deficits are required to trigger vegetation loss, and thus drought recovery capacity is stronger than in other months. In June and July, leaf area is the largest, root systems are the deepest, and carbon reserves are the most abundant, and the expected drought is short, so plants have strong drought resistance, and NDVI anomalies will not occur until SPEI < − 1.67. After August, the threshold rises, meaning that less severe water deficits can trigger vegetation loss, reflecting a more conservative drought resistance strategy. This indicates that the drought sensitivity of vegetation is strongly controlled by seasonal phenology, and midsummer (July) is the period with the strongest drought tolerance. Similar distribution characteristics are also observed when NDVI < NDVI20th, NDVI30th, and NDVI40th (Fig. 8). A comparative analysis across the four NDVI percentile thresholds (NDVI < NDVI10th, NDVI20th, NDVI30th, and NDVI40th) further confirms the robustness of these seasonal patterns (Fig. 8a–d). Across all four thresholds, the temporal distribution of drought trigger thresholds remained highly consistent: the lowest values consistently occurred in June and July, followed by a gradual increase from August to September, with the highest thresholds observed in May and September. As the threshold increased from NDVI < NDVI10th to NDVI < NDVI40th, the trigger thresholds became systematically less negative, indicating that milder drought conditions were sufficient to induce vegetation loss under less stringent definitions of vegetation damage. Discussion

The dominant role of moisture factors in the drought threshold

Across the entire growing season, PRE consistently ranked as the dominant driver of vegetation drought thresholds, indicating that water availability is the primary constraint on vegetation drought tolerance in the WRB. This result agrees with the semi-arid climatic characteristics of the basin and previous studies showing that vegetation on the Loess Plateau is particularly sensitive to water deficits during the main growing season56.

The dominant role of precipitation may be explained by the seasonal balance between soil water supply and atmospheric water demand. During the peak growing season, the combination of rapid canopy development, maximum leaf area index, and vigorous transpiration substantially increases vegetation water consumption, making vegetation more susceptible to even moderate water deficits57. At the same time, PET represents atmospheric evaporative demand and reflects the energy available for transpiration58,59. Consequently, increased PET intensifies the water potential gradient along the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum (SPAC), accelerating water loss and reducing vegetation drought tolerance.

The relative importance of individual drivers exhibited clear seasonal variations (Fig. 10). During the early growing season (May), precipitation remained the dominant factor, while VPD and DEM also contributed substantially. Newly emerging leaves are highly sensitive to short-term atmospheric moisture conditions, and topography regulates local hydrothermal redistribution by modifying temperature, precipitation, and soil moisture patterns60–63. As vegetation entered the vigorous growing stage (June–August), the contributions of PET and VPD increased markedly, reflecting enhanced transpiration demand and a transition from water acquisition to water regulation60. By September, SSM and WIND became more influential, indicating that vegetation increasingly relied on stored soil water while enhanced physical evaporation from soil and canopy further intensified drought stress64.Fig. 10 Importance of drought threshold driving factors under different vegetation loss scenarios. (a) NDVI < NDVI10th; (b) NDVI < NDVI20th; (c) NDVI < NDVI30th; (d) NDVI < NDVI40th.

These seasonal variations suggest that vegetation drought thresholds are jointly regulated by moisture supply and atmospheric evaporative demand. PRE primarily determines soil water availability, whereas PET and VPD control atmospheric water demand. Consequently, precipitation dominates drought thresholds throughout the growing season, while evaporative demand becomes increasingly important during peak vegetation growth and soil water storage plays a larger role during the late growing season65–67.

Response time and drought trigger threshold characteristics of vegetation cover types

In addition to hydroclimatic controls, vegetation characteristics also influence the spatial variability of drought responses across the WRB. Vegetation response time exhibited pronounced spatial variability across the WRB (Fig. 3). The northwestern region, dominated by grasslands, shrublands, and dryland agriculture, generally showed shorter response times (1–6 months), indicating a rapid response of shallow-rooted vegetation to meteorological drought. Because these vegetation types rely primarily on recent precipitation, their physiological activities respond more quickly to short-term moisture deficits, consistent with previous studies on vegetation water-use strategies in the Loess Plateau68,69. By contrast, forests in the southeastern mountainous region exhibited longer response times, reflecting the greater buffering capacity provided by deeper rooting systems and larger soil water storage. Seasonal variations in response time generally followed vegetation phenology, with drought sensitivity increasing during the peak growing season because of higher transpiration demand and declining toward the end of the growing season70,71.

The spatial differences in response time are consistent with the distribution of drought trigger thresholds among vegetation types (Fig. 11). Forests exhibited the lowest drought thresholds, indicating greater drought tolerance, whereas croplands showed the highest thresholds and were therefore more vulnerable to drought stress. Grasslands displayed the greatest threshold variability (standard deviation = 0.27), suggesting considerable heterogeneity in species composition and local environmental conditions72. Shrublands exhibited intermediate threshold values (− 1.20), reflecting their transitional ecological characteristics between forests and grasslands73. Together, these findings suggest that vegetation type is a major contributor to the spatial variability of drought thresholds in the WRB.Fig. 11 Distribution and Probability Density Analysis of Drought Thresholds by Vegetation Type (a) Box plot of drought threshold distribution; (b) Violin plot of drought threshold probability density.

These differences are likely associated with contrasting rooting characteristics and water-use strategies among vegetation types. Forest vegetation can generally access deeper soil water through extensive root systems, thereby maintaining physiological activity under prolonged drought conditions. For example, Robinia pseudoacacia on the Loess Plateau develops root fronts reaching depths of up to 18 m under dry environments74. In addition, Pinus tabuliformis seedlings achieve high drought survival through deep fine-root distribution and stable leaf stoichiometric homeostasis, with soil moisture identified as the dominant control on seedling survival75. In contrast, cropland vegetation generally depends primarily on shallow soil moisture replenished by recent precipitation or irrigation, making it more susceptible to mild drought.

Vegetation restoration may further contribute to these spatial differences. Previous studies have demonstrated that vegetation greening accounts for approximately 80% of the observed changes in regional evapotranspiration on the Loess Plateau, while afforestation generally consumes more water than grassland restoration76,77. These hydrological effects may partly help explain the lower drought thresholds observed in forested mountain regions and the relatively higher thresholds in cropland-dominated areas.

Overall, the spatial distribution of drought thresholds reflects the combined influences of vegetation type, topography, and regional hydroclimatic conditions. Low-threshold areas are mainly concentrated in the forested Qinling Mountains, whereas higher thresholds occur predominantly in cropland- and grassland-dominated areas of the Guanzhong Plain and northwestern WRB. These findings suggest that cropland areas may warrant priority in regional drought risk mitigation, whereas conserving natural forests remains important for maintaining regional ecological resilience.

Limitations and uncertainties

It is worth noting that this study still has some limitations. Regarding the definition of the trigger threshold, many studies have adopted the Copula-Bayesian model. In this paper, a calculation framework for the drought trigger threshold was designed (Fig. 2). For each pixel, the range of SPEI was set as the mean ± 2 times the standard deviation of its historical values. Starting from the upper limit of the range, the SPEI threshold was decreased by 0.1 in steps, and the conditional probability was calculated. When the conditional probability first reached or exceeded 0.5, the corresponding SPEI value was taken as the drought trigger threshold. However, some studies have set the range of SPEI as − 3 to − 0.527, and the fixed probability threshold is also not uniform, with some studies setting it at 0.678. The design of the calculation framework for the conditional probability and drought trigger threshold in this paper fully considers the following two aspects: setting the range of SPEI as the mean ± 2 times the standard deviation of its historical values covers approximately 95% of the SPEI values of the pixel in the historical period and avoids the interference of extreme outliers, making the threshold locally representative and robust. It also fully considers the dynamic changes in the threshold range, which is a critical point relative to the background environment rather than an absolute physical constant, and has strong spatial universality, adapting to different climate zones (such as humid and arid zones). On the other hand, the conditional probability is set at 0.5 because 0.5 is a clearly defined statistical inflection point. It means that when the SPEI is lower than this threshold, the probability of vegetation loss exceeds the probability of no loss, marking a critical state in mathematics and logic where vegetation transitions from “possibly affected” to “more likely affected”. Nevertheless, there is still room for in-depth exploration of different calculation frameworks for the drought trigger threshold.

Beyond the threshold calculation framework, several additional sources of uncertainty warrant consideration. NDVI saturation in dense forest areas may lead to an underestimation of vegetation loss, potentially biasing drought thresholds toward more conservative values. The choice of SPEI base period from 1982 to 2022 and the fixed three-month timescale may not adequately capture non-stationary climate conditions or short-term flash droughts. Spatial interpolation from limited meteorological stations to a one-kilometer resolution inevitably introduces uncertainty, particularly in topographically complex regions such as the Qinling Mountains and Loess Plateau gully areas. Furthermore, alternative Copula structures and different machine learning configurations, including various algorithms and hyperparameter settings, could influence both threshold estimation and the ranking of driving factors. Despite these limitations, the robustness of our main conclusions is supported by sensitivity analyses presented in Table 2 and cross-validation results shown in Table 3. Future research should explore alternative vegetation indices such as Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) to mitigate NDVI saturation, conduct systematic sensitivity analyses of SPEI base periods and timescales, apply higher‑resolution climate data with improved interpolation methods, and employ ensemble machine learning frameworks including Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM to assess the consistency of driver importance rankings.

Another limitation concerns the representation of land use. In this study, land use was treated as a static variable based on 2015 data. In the Wei River Basin, particularly in urban and peri-urban areas, gradual land use conversions (e.g., the transition from cropland to built-up land) exist, which may introduce uncertainty into the threshold estimation for these specific pixels. Future research should incorporate time-varying land use data to more accurately capture the dynamic effects of human activities on drought thresholds.

Conclusion

This study investigates vegetation response to meteorological drought in the WRB during the growing season, focusing on response mechanisms, probability and thresholds of vegetation loss, and their key drivers. The main conclusions are as follows:Vegetation response to meteorological drought exhibits distinct phenological stage dependence. A rapid response (1–6 months) dominates in grassland and dryland agricultural areas in the northwest, reflecting the strong reliance of shallow-rooted vegetation on current precipitation.

Both the probability of vegetation loss and the drought trigger threshold show significant spatial and seasonal variability. Under extreme drought, the high-probability loss area (P > 90%) reaches 23.33% in June. The threshold is lower in upstream forest areas and higher in downstream agricultural and urban areas, with the lowest threshold occurring in June–July, corresponding to peak vegetation drought tolerance.

Water availability is the primary driver of the drought threshold. Precipitation dominates throughout the growing season, while potential evapotranspiration becomes the second most important factor from June to August. The influence of precipitation on the threshold is nonlinear, with positive effects in low-precipitation areas and negative effects in high-precipitation areas.

Using SHAP analysis, this study quantifies the seasonal shift in the drivers of drought thresholds and shows that precipitation dominates throughout the growing season. A nonlinear U-shaped relationship between precipitation and drought thresholds is identified. The study also maps the spatial distribution of drought trigger thresholds across different vegetation types. Cultivated areas, especially in the Guanzhong Plain, are more sensitive to drought, whereas upstream forests are more drought-tolerant. Seasonal water management should focus on the period from June to August, when vegetation is most vulnerable to water shortages. Based on these findings, we recommend improving irrigation efficiency in croplands, promoting drought-tolerant crop varieties, and protecting deep-rooted native tree species in upstream forests.
