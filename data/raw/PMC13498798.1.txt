Background

Metabolic multimorbidity, characterized by the clustering of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly NAFLD) and metabolic syndrome (MetS), is increasingly recognized in young adults and may reflect early systemic metabolic dysfunction. However, simple biomarkers for identifying metabolic abnormalities in asymptomatic populations remain limited.

Objective

To evaluate the ALT/AST ratio as a marker of metabolic multimorbidity and examine its association with modifiable behavioral factors.

Methods

This community-based cross-sectional study included 3,796 asymptomatic adults aged 20–40 years. Moderate-to-severe fatty liver was assessed by ultrasonography. The discriminatory performance of the ALT/AST ratio was evaluated using ROC analysis and compared with ALT, white blood cell count, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent associations.

Results

An ALT/AST ratio >1.3 showed moderate discriminatory performance for fatty liver (AUC 0.791) and MetS (AUC 0.780), with modestly improved discrimination compared with ALT alone and inflammatory markers, particularly in reclassification analyses. Individuals with ALT/AST >1.3 were more likely to have fatty liver (OR 6.13, 95% CI 5.15–7.30) and MetS (OR 5.81, 95% CI 4.61–7.34). Regular physical activity and dental scaling were associated with lower odds of metabolic abnormalities, whereas smoking and betel nut chewing were associated with higher odds of metabolic abnormalities.

Conclusion

The ALT/AST ratio was associated with fatty liver and metabolic syndrome in young adults and showed moderate discriminatory performance. Further longitudinal and external validation is needed before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly referred to as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD]) is now a common condition worldwide and affects about one-quarter of adults. 1 It is closely linked to type 2 diabetes and other metabolic disorders. 2 With the updated MASLD nomenclature and metabolic framework, recent studies show a high prevalence in overweight and obese populations and improved characterization of metabolic dysfunction.3–5 These observations support the view that MASLD is not merely a hepatic condition but also part of a broader metabolic process.

MASLD is no longer viewed as a liver-only condition. It is associated with a range of adverse outcomes and reflects broader metabolic dysfunction. 6 Factors such as insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and central obesity contribute to both liver fat accumulation and systemic disease. 7 In practice, MASLD often coexists with metabolic syndrome (MetS), forming an early pattern of disease clustering. 8 This clustering is increasingly recognized as a key feature of metabolic multimorbidity, in which multiple interrelated conditions develop in parallel rather than sequentially. Longitudinal studies in young adults have demonstrated temporal associations between MASLD and subsequent metabolic abnormalities, 9 suggesting that hepatic steatosis may reflect underlying metabolic dysfunction.

This issue is particularly relevant in younger and non-obese individuals. Lean MASLD has been increasingly recognized and is associated with later metabolic deterioration.10,11 Studies from Asian populations show that a substantial proportion of MASLD occurs in people without obesity, suggesting that reliance on body mass index alone may be insufficient for identifying metabolic risk.12,13 These findings highlight a limitation of current risk assessment strategies, which may overlook metabolically vulnerable individuals despite the absence of overt obesity. Several indices have been proposed for metabolic risk assessment and fatty liver evaluation, including the hepatic steatosis index and the adiponectin–leptin ratio.14,15 While these tools are useful, they require multiple parameters and are not always practical in routine care or population-based assessment. In addition, most of these indices are designed to identify established disease rather than early metabolic changes, which limits their applicability in identifying early metabolic abnormalities.

The alanine aminotransferase-to-aspartate aminotransferase (ALT/AST) ratio is simple and widely available. Compared with ALT alone, the ALT/AST ratio may provide incremental information regarding hepatocellular injury and metabolic dysfunction. Previous studies have demonstrated associations between the ALT/AST ratio and insulin resistance, even after adjustment for obesity-related factors, and have suggested modest incremental discriminatory value for metabolic abnormalities. These findings support the possibility that the ALT/AST ratio may reflect underlying metabolic stress rather than liver injury alone. However, its relationship with metabolic clustering in young and asymptomatic populations remains unclear. Furthermore, metabolic risk is influenced not only by biological factors but also by modifiable behaviors. Lifestyle and oral health have both been linked to metabolic outcomes, but their combined relationship with early metabolic risk has not been well defined.

In this study, we evaluated the association between the ALT/AST ratio and metabolic clustering and examined its relationship with lifestyle and oral health factors in a community-based cohort of young adults.

Methods

This cross-sectional study analyzed data from a community-based health screening program conducted in Yunlin County, Taiwan, between March and December 2022. The research team outlined the study procedures, such as collecting fasting blood samples after an 8-hour overnight fast. The inclusion criteria were as follows 1 : age 20–40 years 2 ; ability to complete questionnaires in Mandarin via face-to-face interviews; and 3 agreement to provide informed consent. Participants who did not complete the health surveys had their laboratory data excluded from the study. Participants with known chronic viral hepatitis (HBV or HCV) were excluded from the analysis. The questionnaires utilized in this study were designed based on our previous research, encompassing topics such as healthy diet, exercise, oral hygiene, and substance use. Participants with missing data in any of the variables included in the analyses were excluded using a complete-case (listwise deletion) approach.

Measures

(a) Demographic characteristics: sex, age, and education level.

(b) Health-related behaviors:Adopting healthy diet was assessed using the frequency of at least three portions of vegetables and two portions of fruit per day, with responses categorized as “less: never or seldom” and “often: usually or always.”

Regular exercise was assessed using the participants’ responses that they [usually/always (often): seldom/never (less)] engaged in exercise for at least 30 min/day or three times per week.

Substance uses were assessed, including current/former using cigarette smoking, alcohol drinking, and betel nuts chewing, with responses categorized as “never” vs. “yes: former or current user.

Oral health and oral hygiene behaviors were assessed with 1 the frequency of using dental floss before bed, 2 uptake dental scaling each half or one year, with responses of “less: never or seldom” or “often: usually or always,” and 3 counting the number of natural teeth by self-reported.

(c) Cardiometabolic risk factors were assessed, including 1 central obesity was measured with waist circumference in males/females > 90/80 cm, 2 systolic/diastolic blood pressure > 130/85 mmHg, 3 fasting serum blood glucose (FBG) > 100 mg/dL, 4 fasting serum triglyceride (TG) > 150 mg/dL, and 5 fasting serum high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) in males/females < 40/50 mg/dL. The presence of three or more of the above components was classified as MetS.

(d) Fatty liver was assessed using ultrasound examinations performed by a team of gastrointestinal physicians and radiologists with 6–10 years of experience in abdominal ultrasonography. The severity of fatty liver was categorized into four grades (0–3): Grade 0, normal liver; Grade 1, mild fatty liver; Grade 2, moderate fatty liver with medium-grade diffuse increase in hepatic echogenicity; and Grade 3, severe fatty liver and marked increase in fine echoes in the parenchyma with poor or no visualization of the intrahepatic vessel borders, diaphragm, and posterior right lobe of the liver.

Statistical analyses

The characteristics of participants with and without moderate-to-severe fatty liver were compared using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the independent sample t-test for continuous variables. The distribution of liver enzymes and blood cell parameters was skewed and was compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The performance of liver enzymes and blood cell parameters in discriminating moderate-to-severe fatty liver from MetS was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The corresponding optimal cut-off point was determined using the Youden index. To evaluate and compare the performance of the ALT level and the ALT/AST ratio in classifying moderate-to-severe fatty liver and MetS, we utilized DeLong’s test for AUC comparisons, as well as the continuous net reclassification index (NRI) and the integrated discrimination index (IDI).

Finally, the factors associated with moderate-to-severe fatty liver and MetS were identified using a multivariable logistic regression model. Because the sample size and number of events were fully adequate, all covariates (demographics, lifestyle variables, and oral hygiene parameters) were concurrently entered into the multivariable model, avoiding selection methods based on partial significance such as stepwise approaches. To further minimize residual confounding, we additionally adjusted for waist circumference, triglycerides, and fasting glucose. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF values in the fully adjusted model ranged from 1.05 to 2.13, indicating that multicollinearity among the variables was not a significant concern.

All tests were 2-tailed, and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Results

Between March and December 2022, 11,225 adults underwent a community health check-up. Of these, 4,067 were young adults aged 20–40 years. After excluding those with missing data, 3,796 participants (93.3%) were eligible for the final analysis. Among the analyzed participants, 1,832 (48.3%) had no fatty liver, while 945 (24.9%) had mild fatty liver, 751 (19.8%) had moderate fatty liver, and 268 (7.1%) had severe fatty liver (Data not shown).

Association between ALT/AST ratio and moderate to severe fatty liver and MetS

In univariable logistic regression, an ALT/AST ratio >1.3 was positively associated with a higher risk of moderate-to-severe fatty liver (odds ratio [OR] 6.98, 95% CI 5.95–8.19) (Table 2). This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and oral hygiene parameters (OR 6.13, 95% CI 5.15–7.30). After further adjustment for waist circumference, triglycerides, and fasting glucose, an ALT/AST ratio >1.3 remained independently associated with moderate-to-severe fatty liver, although the magnitude was attenuated (OR 2.98, 95% CI 2.40–3.69). In the fully adjusted model, an ALT/AST ratio >1.3 was also independently associated with MetS (OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.27–2.40). In addition, the AUC for moderate-to-severe fatty liver increased from 72.5% in Model 1 to 77.7% in Model 2 and 90.5% in Model 3. Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the ALT/AST ratio was significantly associated with fatty liver and metabolic syndrome in young adults and demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance for metabolic abnormalities. In our investigation, the ALT/AST ratio showed modestly improved discrimination compared with ALT alone and inflammatory markers, particularly in reclassification analyses. This is consistent with previous studies showing that the ALT/AST ratio is associated with hepatic steatosis severity. 16 In addition, elevated aminotransferase have been associated with subsequent metabolic abnormalities. 17 Although aminotransferase ratios have also been evaluated for fibrosis assessment, dedicated fibrosis indices such as APRI and FIB-4 are generally used for advanced fibrosis risk stratification. 18 Related metabolic phenotypes have also been described in patients with type 2 diabetes. 19 Taken together, these results suggest that the ALT/AST ratio reflects underlying metabolic stress rather than liver injury alone.

One possible explanation is that the ALT/AST ratio captures early metabolic changes driven by insulin resistance. In this stage, hepatic lipid accumulation and altered hepatocyte function may lead to a relative increase in ALT. This pattern has been described in studies of metabolic dysfunction–associated liver disease. More broadly, MASLD has been recognized as a multisystem condition associated with adverse outcomes, 6 and metabolic risk factors contribute to both hepatic progression and extrahepatic disease. 7 These observations support the view that liver enzyme patterns may reflect systemic metabolic processes.

Our findings also show that fatty liver and metabolic syndrome commonly occur together. In our cohort, individuals with more advanced fatty liver had a markedly higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome. This is consistent with longitudinal evidence demonstrating temporal associations between MASLD and subsequent metabolic abnormalities, 9 and supports the concept of metabolic clustering. 8 Similar patterns have been reported in lean MASLD, where metabolic abnormalities are present even in individuals with normal body weight.10,11 Population-based studies further show that a substantial proportion of MASLD occurs in non-obese individuals, 12 particularly in Asian populations. 13 These findings suggest that reliance on body mass index alone may fail to capture underlying metabolic abnormalities.

Importantly, the coexistence of fatty liver and metabolic syndrome observed in this study supports the view that these conditions do not develop independently but share a common underlying metabolic process. Previous studies have shown that MASLD may represent an early stage of systemic metabolic dysfunction rather than a secondary manifestation. 9 Evidence from lean MASLD further supports this interpretation, as individuals without obesity may still exhibit significant metabolic abnormalities.10,11 In addition, population data indicate that metabolic risk remains substantial even in non-obese groups.12,13 Taken together, these findings suggest that fatty liver should be considered as part of a broader pattern of metabolic clustering. In this context, the ALT/AST ratio may reflect a metabolic phenotype associated with metabolic clustering, particularly in young adults in whom metabolic abnormalities may remain subclinical and may not be adequately captured by conventional anthropometric assessments.

Compared with other surrogate markers, the ALT/AST ratio has practical applicability. Indices such as the hepatic steatosis index and adiponectin–leptin ratio have shown diagnostic value but require multiple variables.14,15 Composite indices such as the fatty liver index (FLI) and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index require additional metabolic and/or anthropometric variables. 20 In contrast, the ALT/AST ratio is readily available, requires no additional calculation, and can be easily incorporated into routine clinical practice. This supports its potential utility as an accessible adjunctive marker for metabolic risk assessment, particularly in younger populations.

An important aspect of this study is the inclusion of behavioral and oral health factors. We found that regular exercise and dental scaling were associated with lower odds of metabolic abnormalities, whereas smoking and betel nut use were associated with higher odds of metabolic abnormalities. These findings are consistent with previous studies linking periodontal disease and metabolic conditions, including MASLD and metabolic syndrome. 21 22–24 Reviews have also highlighted the role of oral inflammation in systemic metabolic processes.25,26 In addition, oral health behaviors may influence metabolic outcomes through their interaction with lifestyle factors. 27 The association with betel nut chewing observed in our study further highlights the importance of region-specific behaviors in metabolic risk. 28

From a clinical perspective, the ALT/AST ratio may provide complementary information for metabolic risk assessment rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic tool. Given that liver enzyme patterns are associated with metabolic status, the ALT/AST ratio may help characterize metabolic risk profiles in young and asymptomatic individuals. Previous interventional studies have reported improvements in liver enzymes and metabolic parameters following lifestyle and metabolic interventions, including therapies targeting metabolic pathways and nutritional supplementation such as curcumin.20,28,29,30 However, the present cross-sectional study was not designed to evaluate causality, treatment responsiveness, or longitudinal metabolic outcomes. Therefore, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to further clarify the clinical implications of these associations.

An additional finding was that the overall discriminatory performance of the ALT/AST ratio was comparable between men and women, whereas the optimal cutoff values differed. Specifically, higher cutoff values were observed in men than in women for both fatty liver and metabolic syndrome. Although the present study was not designed to establish sex-specific cutoff values, these findings suggest that sex-related differences in liver enzyme patterns may influence the optimal interpretation of the ALT/AST ratio. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the study was not specifically designed to derive sex-specific thresholds. Future studies are warranted to determine whether sex-specific cutoff values could improve its clinical applicability in metabolic risk assessment.

With regard to fibrosis assessment, established non-invasive indices such as APRI and FIB-4 are clinically useful for stratifying advanced fibrosis risk. However, the primary objective of the present study was to evaluate the ALT/AST ratio as a marker of metabolic abnormalities in a relatively young and asymptomatic population rather than to assess liver fibrosis severity. In additional analyses, the ALT/AST ratio demonstrated only modest discrimination for APRI ≥0.7 (AUC = 66.8%), whereas discrimination for APRI ≥1.0 was poor and not statistically significant (AUC = 50.5%). Furthermore, because FIB-4 incorporates age as a major component, its applicability may be limited in younger populations such as the present cohort. These findings suggest that the ALT/AST ratio may have limited utility for fibrosis assessment and support its use primarily as a marker of metabolic abnormalities rather than advanced liver fibrosis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, this was a cross-sectional study, and causality cannot be determined. Although the ALT/AST ratio was strongly associated with both fatty liver and metabolic syndrome, the temporal relationship between liver enzyme changes and the development of metabolic abnormalities remains unclear. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal and clinical significance of these associations.

Second, fatty liver was assessed using ultrasonography rather than more sensitive methods such as MRI-based techniques or liver biopsy. As a result, mild steatosis may have been underestimated, and misclassification cannot be excluded. However, ultrasound remains widely used in clinical and population-based settings, which supports the practical applicability of our findings.

Third, lifestyle and oral health variables were self-reported and may be subject to recall or reporting bias. In particular, information on exercise, smoking, and oral hygiene practices may not fully capture long-term behavioral patterns. Residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors, such as dietary composition, sleep patterns, and socioeconomic status, may also have influenced the results.

Fourth, although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual and unmeasured confounding may still exist. In particular, important metabolic variables such as insulin resistance markers, inflammatory biomarkers, detailed dietary information, and socioeconomic factors were unavailable in the current dataset. These factors may partly explain the observed associations.

Finally, the study population consisted of relatively young adults from a single Taiwanese cohort. The findings may not be generalizable to older populations or to other ethnic groups with different metabolic profiles. In addition, the optimal cutoff values identified in this study may vary across populations, clinical settings, and sex groups. External validation in diverse cohorts is therefore needed.

Conclusion

The ALT/AST ratio was significantly associated with fatty liver and metabolic syndrome in young adults and demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance for metabolic abnormalities. Given its simplicity and accessibility, it may have potential utility as an accessible adjunctive marker in metabolic risk assessment. However, prospective longitudinal studies and external validation are required before clinical application can be established.
