ABSTRACT

Background

Severe and chronic illnesses such as an oncological disease in children, adolescents, and young adults (CAYAs) are accompanied by profound physical, psychological, and social challenges for the patients and their families. Consensus‐ and evidence‐based psychosocial standards of care aim to address these challenges but implementation is heterogeneous. The current paper presents the consensus‐ and evidence‐based psychosocial therapy tool “MyLogbook—I know my way around” which aims to operationalize and implement existing guidelines for psychosocial care in pediatric oncology, supporting patients from diagnosis through follow‐up care with psychoeducational and emotional resources.

Procedure

This multi‐center pilot study evaluated the feasibility and efficacy of “MyLogbook” in 240 participants (mean age = 9.00, SD = 2.78; 50.8% female) across 22 pediatric oncology treatment centers and rehabilitation centers in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, and Italy. The therapy program involves psychoeducational modules tailored to different medical treatment phases and includes assessments of emotional well‐being and health literacy. Psychosocial health care professionals (HCPs) of the respective center conducted selected modules with children, with each module consisting of two 60‐min sessions. Efficacy was determined through pre‐ and post‐session assessments of children's emotional states with validated measures and their self‐perceived, module‐specific expertise (health literacy) on a scale of [1;5].

Results

A significant improvement in subjective module‐specific health literacy was observed, with self‐reported expertise increasing from a mean of 2.97 (SD = 1.45) following session 1 to 3.97 (SD = 1.13) following session 2 (Wilcoxon V = 1597.5, p < 0.001). Negative emotions significantly decreased across sessions (B = −0.342, p < 0.001), as did neutral emotions (beta = −0.194, p < 0.001), whereas positive emotions significantly increased (beta = 0.051, p = 0.011). Notably, 38% of children and 40% of parents reported average QoL at baseline, which had no impact on the outcome of emotional well‐being over time. The highest efficacy of the therapy program “MyLogbook” was observed in psychoeducational modules addressing early treatment phases (e.g., more than half of used booklets are accounted for by “Everything there is to know!”, “My path to diagnosis” and “I’M Ready”).

Conclusion

The psychological therapy program “MyLogbook” effectively translates psychosocial care guidelines into clinical practice, improving emotional well‐being and health literacy among pediatric oncology patients and their families. To maximize its impact, future efforts should address structural challenges, integrate modules into a unified format, and expand accessibility through multi‐lingual adaptations. This published psychosocial therapy program demonstrates the feasibility of delivering standardized psychosocial interventions and highlights their potential to enhance the quality of life for young patients with cancer.

1 Introduction

Severe and chronic disease and its treatment are major life events connected to a variety of physical, psychological, and social strains on the affected children, adolescents, and young adults (CAYAs) as well as their families. On the one hand, the threat to the young person's life, the upcoming medical treatments, and the insecure prognosis [1] can trigger feelings of fear, anxiety, and guilt [2]. Moreover, the need to stay in the hospital during treatment, adhere to an intense treatment regime, and attend a multitude of appointments cause considerable changes to the affected persons' daily lives, leading to reduced school attendance and even unemployment in parents [3, 4, 5]. The inability to continue the usual work and school life as well as the financial burden and administrative tasks related to medical treatments, accommodation, and potential migration require additional resources for the already charged family system [6].

Robust scientific evidence has demonstrated the multiple psychosocial burdens associated with pediatric oncological treatment and has led to the inclusion of comprehensive psychosocial care in international pediatric oncology standards [3, 7, 8], though their implementation remains heterogenous [9]. Firstly, it is essential to provide individualized care [10, 11] and timely preventative crisis interventions during the crucial phase of diagnosis to promote treatment compliance and to obviate late effects such as post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and anxiety disorders [3, 4, 12, 13]. Therefore, patients and families must be provided with sufficient, understandable, and easily accessible information (psychoeducation) [4, 7] to strengthen health literacy [14], and empower patients to enhance comprehension of the diagnosis and therapies, anticipate consequences and navigate various possibilities and necessary actions [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Furthermore, confidence, trust, and treatment compliance need to be fostered by building a strong relationship between patients, their families, and health care professionals (HCP) as reference persons [20, 21, 22]. Finally, active, and profound engagement and involvement of patients and their families in decision‐making and the therapeutic process has been shown to empower patients [19], thereby reducing the risk of anxiety and increasing treatment compliance [19, 21, 23, 24, 25]. However, the implementation of these standards remains inconsistent across clinical settings, highlighting the need for structured approaches to effectively translate theoretical guidelines into routine care [9]. This heterogeneity has been attributed not only to insufficient establishment of psychosocial services and gaps in referral and provision [26, 27, 28] but also to the limited implementation of EBIs developed through cutting‐edge research into routine clinical practice [7].

To overcome the latency of around 17 years for EBIs to be translated into clinical care [29], the field of implementation research has emerged, providing powerful tools to assess, and overcome barriers to the effective adoption of novel EBIs [30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. According to Damschroder et al. (2022) reasons for the persisting “care gap” can be categorized into the following domains: characteristics of the proposed innovation (EBI) itself, the outer setting (community, system, culture), the inner setting (institutions), the individuals involved (professionals/users, recipients, stakeholders) and the implementation process [35]. The most prominently discussed issues include the lack of resources among psychosocial personnel [36], missing incentives to adopt novel techniques, and the insufficient integration of psychosocial care into the healthcare system (inner setting) [37]. Furthermore, EBIs are often developed by a single researcher rather than a team of all relevant individuals, and designed independently of the designated context, making them ill‐suited for application in the complex, dynamic, multi‐disciplinary setting of a hospital (innovations) [19, 36, 38, 39, 40, 41].

To ensure that newly developed EBIs are patient‐centered, consensus‐based and feasible within the dynamic and resource‐constrained context of psychosocial care, an increasing number of studies are incorporating Public and Patient Involvement and Engagement (PPIE) [42, 43, 44] as well as Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) cycles [45] in the development process. The hereby presented and evaluated quality improvement (QI) project “MyLogbook—I know my way around” draws on these methods to translate the consensus‐ and evidence‐based Guideline for psychosocial care in pediatric oncology and hematology [3] into a practical and comprehensive psychosocial support method that accompanies patients throughout the entire treatment process, from diagnosis to follow‐up care. It thereby aims to make the efficacy of psychosocial care measurable and to operationalize the underlying processes to make them visible and comprehensible for both patients and health care institutions. The methods used to develop and evaluate the structured psychosocial support method are discussed in detail in the accompanying protocol paper [46]. The present paper focuses on presenting the final version of the psychosocial support method called “MyLogbook” tool and on discussing the results of the multi‐center pilot study, which aimed to assess its the feasibility and therapeutic potential in improving health literacy and emotional well‐being.

2 Methods

2.1 Intervention Development and Pilot Implementation

The “MyLogbook” project originated from a local initiative by the psychosocial team at the pediatric neuro‐oncology unit of the Medical University and General Hospital of Vienna, aiming to develop a psychosocial support method that could serve as a basic standard of psychosocial care (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT04474678). Since its initiation in 2017 it has evolved into the herein presented consensus‐based translation of six consecutive modules of an evidence‐ and consensus‐based guideline [3] for psychosocial care in pediatric oncology and hematology into a practical psychosocial support method for patient‐centered care. The project lasted from 2017 to 2022 and followed the rationale of a QI project [35, 45] using repeated patient‐centered evaluation in pediatric oncology departments and consensus‐based integration of feedback from interdisciplinary health care professionals and patient advocates via Delphi surveys to develop a feasible and practicable psychosocial support method [37, 47]. A detailed description of the multilevel development process and all the applied methods is provided in the corresponding protocol paper [46]. Certain steps of the “MyLogbook” project (including structured Delphi rounds for incremental improvement) were discussed in more depth in separate publications [37, 46, 47, 48]; specific booklets [49, 50, 51] developed early in the process have already been evaluated priorly. The herein presented multi‐center pilot study used the final version of the now published “MyLogbook” tool [52, 53, 54] and aimed to provide first insights into feasibility and preliminary outcomes of its application to inform future, larger‐scale studies.

During the presently described pilot phase, pediatric oncological centers in the DACh region were invited to participate in the study. In total, n = 28 centers joined the pilot phase and each of them was provided with sufficient materials to complete the individually relevant modules with at least five children [37].

All intervention providers were psychosocial healthcare professionals who completed a standardized one‐day training workshop covering the theoretical background, intervention principles, role‐play exercises, and evaluation procedures. To support implementation fidelity, monthly investigator meetings were held throughout the study to discuss implementation experiences, address frequently asked questions, and ensure consistent delivery across study sites.

2.2 Psychological Treatment—“MyLogbook” Modules and Booklets

“MyLogbook” is a standardized psychological therapy program, a practical guide accompanying children through all stages of treatment. The consecutive steps throughout the treatment process are translated into six patient‐oriented “modules”: initial contact, diagnostic assessment, supportive care during medical treatment, follow‐up assessment, rehabilitation, and follow‐up care. Each module contains specific psychosocial care topics which are operationalized in a total of 16 interactive booklets. If accompanied by the “MyLogbook” throughout the entire treatment process, patients could therefore complete various modules or just be supported in one or few specific topics using the designated booklets. Figure 1 and supplement 2 provide an overview of all six modules and the corresponding booklets operationalized in the “MyLogbook” psychosocial support program as well as the number of participants who used each booklet.

FIGURE 1 Overview of all evaluated “MyLogbook” modules in chronological order related to the therapeutic cycle. Copy right attribution: 123RF (purchased license); Freepik Company S.L, Flaticon 2010‐2025 (purchased license).

Each booklet is designed for two face‐to‐face sessions, combining psychoeducation, practical exercises, and reflection, while allowing for an individualized approach. To ensure age‐appropriateness, the materials include interactive elements that actively engage children beyond the mere provision of information. The accompanied patient reported outcome measures document the individual treatment journey and allow for individual adjustments in psychosocial therapy. Structural information guides patients and families through therapy and prepares them for follow‐up care [46]. Implementation requires psychosocial expertise and must be conducted according to the intervention‐specific instructions [52] by trained specialists within the overall care network. A detailed description of the various psycho‐social methods applied within each booklet is available in Supporting Information S1: Supplement 1.

2.3 Participants

The study sample comprised n = 240 patients from n = 22 pediatric oncology treatment and rehabilitation centers across Germany, Italy, Austria, and Switzerland. Ethical approval was obtained for all participating centers. Children were eligible if they were aged 4–14 years (target range 6–12 years) and were treated in one of the participating pediatric hematology/oncology treatment or rehabilitation centers during the defined study period. Participation was possible at any stage from diagnosis to follow‐up care, and completion of all “MyLogbook” modules was not required. Patients were excluded only if they had no hematological/oncological disease or if written or oral consent/assent was not provided.

The patient's mean age was M = 9.00 (SD = 2.78) with 50.8% (n = 101) being female. Of the n = 131 patients with available information on diagnosis (55%), 49% (n = 64) had a brain tumor, 15% (n = 19) leukemia, and 37% (n = 48) a solid tumor. Also, of those, 47% (n = 62) of patients had completed surgery, 26% (n = 34) had undergone radiation therapy, 53% (n = 70) chemotherapy, and 3% (n = 4) stem cell transplantation. The relatively low rate of stated diagnoses (55%, n = 131) is attributable to missing data, which was primarily due to two factors: first, not all collaborating clinics consistently provided information about patients' medical diagnoses; and second, in cases where patient names had already been pseudonymized, it was no longer possible to identify the patients in order to retrieve this information. However, it can be assumed that every participant had a diagnosis within the hematological or oncological spectrum, as this study was conducted explicitly within pediatric hematology and oncology clinics. Regarding the setting, 38% (n = 160) of sessions were conducted in an outpatient setting, 16% (n = 66) in a day clinic, 42% (n = 178) in an inpatient setting, and 4% (n = 17) were mixed. Furthermore, 46% (n = 148) of sessions involved patients and their parents, 42% (n = 135) involved patients alone, and 12% (n = 39) involved both, sessions with and without parents. Most sessions were completed by psychologists as health‐care professionals (HCPs) (90.5%, n = 343). The remaining sessions were conducted by psychotherapists, art therapists, or educators. Details are provided in Table S1: Supplement 2.

2.4 Psychosocial Measures

Table 1 provides an overview of all assessments applied, including descriptions and questionnaire formats. All participants underwent standardized psycho‐social baseline assessments, including evaluations of quality of life [KINDL‐R self & proxy rating, Cronbach's α = 0.95] [55, 56], subjective personal strengths and difficulties [SDQ self‐ and proxy ratings, Cronbach's α = 0.82] [57, 58, 62], and health literacy [14, 59, 60]. At this stage of the multicenter pilot study, baseline data were used solely for sample characterization (i.e., to describe participant characteristics) and were not intended to serve as measures of change over time. Outcome measures include the assessment of emotional well‐being using 18 validated emotional icons [48] as well as subjective expertise regarding current health status and upcoming medical interventions, based on the Dreyfus & Dreyfus model (1980). To complement the dataset, available results from standard‐of‐care neuropsychological assessment (e.g., WISC) were retrospectively extracted from patient records.

2.5 Data Analysis

Data analysis and visualization was conducted using the statistical software R (version 4.1.0) [63] including the ggplot2 package [64]. To analyze the progression of positive, neutral, and negative emotions, generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) with Poisson error distribution (log link) were implemented using the lme4 package [65]. To investigate the differences in subjective expertise before and after the intervention, a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used. Additionally, a Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed to compare the differences between self‐reported expertise and proxy‐rated expertise by HCPs. Analyses were performed at both the booklet‐specific level, as well as over the treatment processes encompassing all booklets. At the process level, the moderating effects of the psychological parameters quality of life [KINDL [55, 56]] and personal strengths and difficulties [SDQ [57, 58]] on the progression of emotions and expertise were investigated. We used no missing data imputation techniques.

3 Results

3.1 Baseline Evaluation

Responses for the psychological assessments were available for the majority of patients. Specifically, 70% of patients provided data for both the SDQ and the KINDL, while responses for the health literacy scale were available for 25% of the sample. Across all administered instruments, most patients' scores fell within the normative (average) range. Both children and their parents rated children's own strengths and difficulties as average in most categories. At baseline, less than 22% of children scored in the clinical range on the SDQ subscales Kind Behavior, Hyperactivity, Overall Stress, and Behavioral Difficulties. The majority (78%–94%) scored within the age‐matched normative (non‐clinical) range. Clinical scores were more frequent on the Emotional Stress (31%) and Getting Along with Others (42%) subscales. Parent proxy ratings revealed a similar pattern, although slightly fewer parents rated their children within the clinical range; notably, only 24% of parents reported clinical scores for Getting Along with Others (Figure 2A).

(A) Results of the SDQ at baseline—percentages represent the proportion of patients (self‐report) and parents (proxy‐report) scoring within the normative range or in the clinical range compared to the age‐matched normative sample in each subscale. (B) KINDL‐R at baseline—percentages represent the proportion of patients with below average/average/above average quality of life compared to the age‐matched normative sample in each subscale.

Similar patterns emerged with the KINDL‐R, where children and parents rated themselves or rather their children as average across most categories. In the KINDL‐R questionnaire, 50% of children and 48% of parents rated the child's overall quality of life (QoL) within the average range compared to age‐normative reference data. Twelve percent of both children and parents reported an above‐average QoL, whereas 38% of children and 40% of parents indicated a below‐average QoL. At the subscale level, 37% of children reported below‐average scores for Physical Well‐Being, 42% for Emotional Well‐Being, 31% for Friends, and 30% for Everyday Functioning. Parent proxy ratings reflected a comparable pattern, although children tended to report lower QoL scores across subscales compared to parental ratings.

Generally, there was a tendency toward perceiving moderate Qol. Overall, 38% of patients and 40% of parents perceived a low Qol, while 50% of children and 48% of parents perceived an average Qol. Additionally, 12% of children and 12% of parents perceived a high Qol. For 35% of patients an indicator of general cognitive functioning was available; these patients showed a mean result of percentile rank PR = 39.

3.2 Subjective Health‐ or Treatment‐Related Expertise

As shown in Figure 3B, there was a statistically significant increase in self‐reported knowledge between the first and second sessions (Wilcoxon V = 1597.5, p <. 001, r = 0.35; first session: M = 2.97, SD = 1.45; second session: M = 3.97, SD = 1.13). Additionally, a significant, yet noticeably smaller difference was observed between the self‐reported knowledge and the knowledge evaluation provided by a healthcare professional in the second session (W = 37,938, p = 0.014, r = 0.10; HCP evaluation: M = 4.23, SD = 0.91). The results indicate that healthcare professionals provided a lower assessment of the children's knowledge, whereas the children themselves rated their own level of knowledge more positively. The use of the psychological therapy program organized in subsequent thematic booklets was found to correlate with the achievement of a high level of the predetermined goals that were set during their development.

3.3 Emotional Well‐Being

Given certain heterogeneities in the data due to inconsistent completion of sessions/booklet parts, the emotion count collected before and after the first and second session was utilized for the linear model. The progression of emotions is visualized in Figure 3A. Averaged across all booklets, positive emotions increased slightly but significantly over sessions (b = 0.051, SE = 0.02, z = 2.54, p = 0.011, R 2 marginal = 0.003, R 2 conditional = 0.269). Neutral emotion significantly decreased across the sessions (b = −0.194, SE = 0.03, z = −7.27, p < 0.001, R 2 marginal = 0.025, R 2 conditional = 0.181), with an even stronger decline observed for negative emotions (b = −0.342, SE = 0.03 z = −10.74, p < 0.001, R 2 marginal = 0.049, R 2 conditional = 0.319). This pattern appears across all booklets, however, it's more pronounced in specific ones. Analyzing the emotion trajectories for each of the 13 booklets separately confirmed that the decrease in negative emotions was the most consistent finding.

Moderation analyses revealed that most Time × Predictor interactions were non‐significant (see Table S2: Supplement 3). For negative emotions, however, both child‐reported quality of life and child‐reported difficulties significantly moderated change over time, with higher scores on either measure associated with a weaker decline in negative emotions—a somewhat contradictory pattern given that these measures reflect opposite directions of psychosocial functioning.

3.4 Differences Between Booklets

Booklet usage varied substantially across the 12 available modules (see Figure 4 legend), ranging from 6 to 105 completions out of a total of 424 booklets completed. The most frequently completed booklets were “I'M Ready” (n = 105, 25%), “Everything, there is to know!” (n = 74, 18%), and “My treasure chest” (n = 56, 13%).

The comparison of longitudinal results for subjective expertise showed statistically significant disparities in subjective knowledge during session 1 across the various booklets (F(15, 340) = 2.201 p = 0.006, 95% CI [0.015, 0.123]). However, in session 2, no statistically significant disparities in subjective knowledge were detected across the booklets (F(15, 280) = 1.337, p = 0.179, 95% CI [0.000, 0.081]). Additionally, the assessments of knowledge conducted by professionals exhibited a consistent pattern across all booklet variants, as well (F(15, 272) = 1.204, p = 0.268, CI [0.000, 0.082]).

As visualized by Figure 4, the frequency of positive, neutral, and negative emotions at the end of the second session varied across booklets (positive = F(15, 436) = 3.035, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.026, 0.123]; neutral = F(15, 436) = 1.791, p = 0.034, 95% CI [0.004, 0.082]; negative = F(15, 436) = 2.058, p = 0.011, 95% CI [0.005, 0.085]). Despite these observed differences, Tukey's post hoc tests did not reveal any statistically significant findings for either case.

4 Discussion

4.1 Key Potential of the MyLogbook Related to Baseline Assessment

The baseline evaluation of health literacy and quality of life in patients and their parents showed an elevated perceived risk to the quality of life of patients and parents, highlighting the need to provide adequate psychosocial support to all pediatric patients. The present study describes the first multi‐center pilot study to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of the psychosocial support method “MyLogbook” which translates guidelines for psychosocial care in pediatric oncology into practical interventions considering all phases from diagnosis to follow‐up care. The global aim is to ensure that all CAYAs are accompanied by psychosocial support throughout their entire therapeutic process, receiving standard‐of‐care evidence‐based interventions including psychoeducation, the deliberate and active exploration involving the application of diverse methodologies, such as neuropsychological frameworks, relaxation strategies, and related interventions and enhancement of psychosocial resources. While preceding evaluations of the project investigated the feasibility, obstacles, and benefits of implementing the “MyLogbook” tool [37], this first global evaluation of the tool itself provides the first evidence for the positive effects accompanying CAYAs and their families with the “MyLogbook” tool has on the patients’ emotional well‐being and their subjective expertise. Programs such as “MyLogbook” may have particular value in settings where resources are limited and comprehensive psychosocial services are not widely available [66].

4.2 Effects of Patient‐Centered Care on Health Literacy and Self‐Efficacy

This multi‐center pilot evaluation indicates that the psychosocial support method was generally received well by patients, families as well as interdisciplinary HCPs. Overall, the results show a positive trend in the CAYAs' subjective expertise regarding the module‐specific health topic which indicates elevated subjective health literacy. Differences between self‐ and proxy‐rated knowledge could be explained by children perceiving greater self‐efficacy and competence than anticipated by adults, as well as by differences in the perception and comprehension of future treatment‐related challenges. The ability to comprehend the present medical status as well as potential treatment alternatives can empower patients and their families to actively influence the therapeutic process and increase their feelings of self‐efficacy [67, 68, 69]. Moreover, the provision of relevant information can make up for the frequent lack of comprehensive psychosocial care, enabling patients and parents to ask concrete questions and direct them effectively to the personnel in charge [24, 70]. Finally, feelings of self‐efficacy and empowerment are important protective factors to prevent helplessness as well as anxiety disorders [69, 71], which are known to be of higher prevalence among young patients with chronic diseases [72, 73, 74].By helping children better understand their illness and supporting adaptive coping, the program may contribute to empowering young patients and extending psychosocial support to a larger number of children. Moreover, systematically evaluating the implementation of the intervention in future studies can make discrepancies between self‐ and proxy‐rated knowledge visible, uncovering potential underestimation of children's abilities and highlighting important opportunities for therapeutic dialogue and shared decision‐making.

4.3 Effects of Patient‐Centered Care on Emotional Well‐Being

In addition to the assessment and enhancement of health literacy, the application of the “MyLogbook” tool requires the evaluation of the patients' emotional well‐being at the beginning and at the end of each session with the psychosocial HCP. This longitudinal emotional assessment illustrates emotional processes, showing an overall decrease in negative and neutral emotions paired with an increase in positive emotions correlating with the “MyLogbook” training. On the one hand, repeated application creates a ritual between the patient and the HCP which can facilitate trust between the parties and enforce the therapeutic relationship [20, 69]. Furthermore, the decrease in neutral emotions could indicate the increased awareness for emotional well‐being and improved ability to name and discuss distinct emotional states related to the personal health status which is necessary for effective communication [75]. These first findings can serve as a valuable basis for the creation of health trackers including the evaluation of psychosocial well‐being to ensure that poor mental health states related to medical treatments are recorded and can hence be addressed with adequate psychosocial care as proposed by psychosocial care guidelines [3]. Moreover, the results compellingly demonstrate that even brief and regular interventions are associated with short‐term improvements in emotional well‐being when coping with the current situation. Finally, the continuous assessment of emotional well‐being enables a differentiated response to individual needs and allows for the derivation of concrete, personalized, and targeted measures for consecutive treatment planning.

4.4 Differences Between Booklets and Treatment Phases

The per booklet sample size differed significantly between different modules due to the focus on flexible implementation in real‐world conditions in this exploratory feasibility pilot study. Yet, the differences in module completion may reflect factors such as patient availability, clinical status, timing within treatment, or organizational structures across participating centers. Across modules and booklets, improved emotional response associated with hospital settings was observed during the application of the “MyLogbook” support method. Differences in the preliminary efficacy of the distinct treatment modules show that psychoeducation and resource activation hold the largest potential to increase patient well‐being and subjective module‐specific knowledge, especially in the initial treatment phase. These phases were associated with initially higher levels of negative emotionality, potentially coexisting with adaptive processes. This highlights the key role this first episode of the therapeutic process plays for treatment related well‐being [3, 4, 12, 13] and the ability of patients and families to successfully cope with the extreme burdens faced during cancer care [15, 16, 17, 18]. Moreover, intervention effects may depend on factors such as the therapeutic relationship, the relevance of the addressed topic at a given treatment stage, and patients' readiness to engage. This assumption is consistent with behavioral change models such as the Health Action Process Approach (HAPA), which emphasizes that intervention effectiveness depends on motivational and contextual factors [76].

4.5 Limitations and Future Directions

This study was designed as a pilot study rather than a randomized controlled trial; therefore, findings should be considered preliminary and interpreted cautiously. Although the overall sample size was moderate, the per‐module sample sizes were modest and differed between booklets, limiting statistical inference. Moreover, it cannot be ruled out that changes in expertise or emotional experience are influenced by factors independent of intervention. Examples include improved rapport between the child and the treatment team over time, habituation, psychoeducation or other supportive measures initiated by parents, contact with other families or extracurricular activities, or changes and improvements in the clinical condition itself. Yet, evaluation methods were conducted in timely manner, which limits potential confounding factors, particularly since the evaluation questions are specifically focused on the “MyLogbook” itself. From this perspective, the encouraging positive developments observed support the potential of the intervention and underscore the need for further studies with more rigorous designs to confirm and extend these results. Moreover, it underlines the essential need for psychosocial therapy optimization studies to systematically evaluate and validate such promising approaches.

While selective sampling may be desirable in later studies, it was beyond the scope of this first pilot study. Meanwhile, inclusion was handled liberally and all patients in the adequate age range could participate. As a result, sample sizes differed per module, since participants only completed the relevant booklets for their individual treatment phase, leading to varying evaluative reliability across booklets. Moreover, different types of chronic diseases were not balanced. However, baseline analyses and group comparisons across diagnostic categories did not indicate relevant differences in the observed trends, despite the limited sample size. These preliminary findings suggest that the tool may be applicable across different pediatric oncology diagnoses. Nevertheless, this aspect should be examined more systematically in future randomized controlled trials.

Despite its positive outcomes, the implementation of “MyLogbook” in n = 28 health care institutions faced structural challenges, such as limited psychosocial personnel, inconsistent hospital infrastructures, and language barriers (for detail see the corresponding implementation study [37]). The factors emphasize the need for systemic changes to integrate psychosocial care as a standard component of pediatric oncology. The modular structure of the intervention allows for flexibility and adaptability across different medical contexts, suggesting its potential applicability beyond oncology to other chronic pediatric conditions. One key challenge for international implementation will be cultural and linguistic differences which in the present pilot study have been addressed using structured cross‐cultural adaptation process such as forward and backward translation, expert consensus procedures (e.g., Delphi methodology).

It should be noted that, although the evaluation used a repeated‐measures design, no longitudinal follow‐up assessment was conducted after the intervention period. Consequently, we cannot determine the long‐term stability of any intervention effects. Future research should focus on addressing these structural barriers, expanding the accessibility of the intervention through multilingual adaptations, and integrating all modules into a unified format to streamline its application. Furthermore, longitudinal studies including follow‐up assessments are necessary to assess the long‐term impact of “MyLogbook” on patients' psychological resilience and quality of life. In the pilot phase of the “MyLogbook” project, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) and the KINDL questionnaire were used at baseline to describe the characteristics of the sample in terms of behavioral functioning and health‐related quality of life and rather get insight in the target group. No follow‐up assessments with these instruments were planned or conducted at this stage. For future studies especially interventional or rather therapy optimization studies, however, follow‐up assessments should be incorporated. In addition to the SDQ and KINDL, further instruments could be considered to provide a more comprehensive evaluation—including not only quality of life and behavioral aspects, but also the development of health literacy, self‐efficacy and parental burden/stressors. Finally, studies should additionally differentiate between different diseases, treatments, treatment phases and medical complications to further evaluate specific psychosocial needs and risk groups.

In addition, the intervention holds promise for transferability to other pediatric conditions that share similar psychosocial challenges or could benefit from the inclusion of disease‐specific modules tailored to particular clinical contexts. Developing a digital version of “MyLogbook” could facilitate standardized implementation, simplify screening processes, and serve as a supportive psychosocial support method in light of increasing staff shortages in pediatric healthcare settings. While not intended to replace direct therapeutic interactions, both the analog (paper‐pencil) and digital formats—grounded in psychological principles—can play a vital role in bridging existing care gaps and ensuring broader, more equitable access to low‐threshold, needs‐oriented psychosocial support.

4.6 Implications of the Presented Study

This multi‐center pilot study evaluated the feasibility and therapeutic potential of “MyLogbook”—I Know My Way Around, a structured psychosocial support program designed to translate evidence‐based S3‐guidelines [3] into clinical practice in pediatric oncology. Though randomized control trials are necessary to properly evaluate efficacy and causality, the results of this pilot‐phase show promising preliminary results. The intervention was associated with a significant increase in subjective health literacy, as well as improved emotional well‐being, marked by reduced negative and neutral emotions alongside an increase in positive emotional states. Furthermore, children and adolescents showed elevated scores on the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) and quality of life (KINDL‐R) which suggests enhanced resilience as well as a reduction in psychosocial burden correlating with the application of the “MyLogbook” intervention as part of a basic psychosocial care approach.

The perceived efficacy of psychoeducational “MyLogbook” modules especially in early treatment phases highlights the importance of timely psychosocial support to mitigate anxiety and enhance treatment compliance. The standardized structure of the “MyLogbook” facilitates the professionalization of psychosocial care processes, enabling consistent quality across diverse clinical settings. Moreover, it is the first psychosocial support method to provide a comprehensive framework for therapy optimization studies, enabling systematic evaluation and continuous improvement of psychosocial interventions. By enhancing the visibility and perceived importance of psychosocial support, the published psychosocial tool “MyLogbook” [52, 53, 54] contributes to establishing these services as an integral component of comprehensive pediatric oncology care.

5 Conclusion

In conclusion, “MyLogbook” represents a feasible, effective psychosocial support method for enhancing psychosocial care in pediatric oncology apt for the evaluation in clinical trials. By operationalizing evidence‐based guidelines into practical interventions, it offers a promising pathway to improve both emotional well‐being and health literacy among young patients and their families, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes and quality of life. The “MyLogbook” tool, including a basic and oncology specific version as well as a handbook for health care professionals has been published in German language and is openly available for purchase [52, 53, 54].
