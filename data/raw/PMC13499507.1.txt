Objective

To develop a brief, clinician-administered Chinese-language Breakthrough Cancer Pain Assessment (BCPA) instrument and evaluate its content validity, inter-rater reliability, and ability to detect change in selected rescue medication-related items.

Methods

The 11-item BCPA was developed from literature review, clinical input, and three Delphi rounds with 14 experts. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated through independent physician and nurse interviews with 30 participants with cancer and breakthrough cancer pain (BTcP). In an exploratory observational phase, selected BCPA responses were compared in 77 participants before and 7 days after routine-care initiation of fentanyl buccal soluble film.

Results

In the third Delphi round, all items had an item-level content validity index of 1.00 and the scale-level content validity index based on universal agreement (S-CVI/UA) was 1.00. Kappa coefficients for categorical items ranged from 0.507 to 0.888, and intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC(2,1)] values for numerical items ranged from 0.836 to 0.979. The proportion reporting pain relief within 15 minutes increased from 33.8% to 66.2% (matched-pairs odds ratio, 4.57; 95% CI, 2.02–10.35; P < 0.001). Mean satisfaction with the effectiveness of rescue medication increased from 5.96 to 7.60, and perceived improvement in sleep and mood increased from 6.01 to 7.40 (both P < 0.001).

Conclusions

The Chinese-language BCPA showed preliminary content validity and inter-rater reliability. Selected rescue medication-related items detected pre/post changes in an uncontrolled observational sample. Further evaluation of response processes, criterion-related validity, cross-cultural validity, and responsiveness is required before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Breakthrough cancer pain (BTcP) is a critical clinical issue in patients with cancer and is defined by a sudden, intense flare-up of pain that occurs even when the patient's baseline pain is otherwise well managed.1 A systematic review reported that the prevalence of BTcP among patients with cancer ranges from 39.9% to 80.5%, underscoring the widespread impact of this condition.2 BTcP is notorious for its rapid onset and severe intensity, which can profoundly disrupt a patient's quality of life and daily functioning. Patients experiencing BTcP often report reduced physical functioning, impaired mobility, and limitations in daily activities, as well as significant psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and emotional instability.3, 4, 5 A prospective multicenter study has also demonstrated that cancer patients' sleep quality can be significantly improved after individualized therapy for BTcP.6 A study conducted by the American Pain Foundation found that breakthrough pain negatively affects emotional health in 82% of patients, causes suffering in 82%, wakens patients at least once a month in 73%, and also impacts their ability to perform routine tasks (76%) as well as their willingness to participate in activities (83%).7 Moreover, BTcP imposes a substantial burden not only on patients but also on their families. A recent scoping review by Li et al. reported that the unpredictability of BTcP increases caregivers' physical and psychological stress, with many caregivers experiencing sleep deprivation, anxiety, and interruptions to work.8

The effective management of BTcP is crucial for improving the overall quality of life for cancer patients. However, the episodic nature of BTcP makes it challenging to assess using standard tools, such as Brief Pain Inventory and McGill Pain Questionnaire, which are designed for chronic pain and measure average or overall pain rather than time-locked, short-lived spikes.9,10 Consequently, these tools frequently fail to capture the unique characteristics of BTcP, such as rapid onset, short duration, and the specific triggers that may exacerbate these episodes. A qualitative study reported that patients often find it difficult to communicate breakthrough pain to health care professionals, and some participants had difficulty differentiating between continuous background pain and intermittent breakthrough pain.11

A comprehensive and easily administered tool can provide valuable insights into the nature and impact of BTcP, enabling health care providers to develop more effective pain management strategies. The Breakthrough Pain Assessment Tool (BAT) is a self-report instrument designed to evaluate the characteristics of BTcP.12 The BAT assesses various dimensions of BTcP, including the frequency, duration, and intensity of pain episodes, as well as the triggers and relief measures. Although the BAT has been instrumental in advancing our understanding of BTcP, it does not evaluate the impact of BTcP on sleep and mood, which are often seriously disrupted in patients with cancer. A large European observational study has documented sleep disturbance as one of BTcP's adverse effects;13 another study with Canadian patients also reported similar impacts.14 A Korean nationwide survey also linked BTcP with poor pain control satisfaction and sleep disturbance.15 In addition to the BAT, other instruments such as the Alberta Breakthrough Pain Assessment Tool16 and the Breakthrough Pain Questionnaire17 have been developed to assess BTcP. Although these tools have contributed substantially to the characterization of BTcP, certain practical needs remain in routine clinical settings. Pain assessment is inherently grounded in patients' subjective experiences, and the newly developed Breakthrough Cancer Pain Assessment (BCPA) likewise relies on patient-reported information. However, its structured clinician-administered format may facilitate rapid assessment and treatment monitoring during busy clinical consultations, particularly for patients who have difficulty completing self-administered questionnaires due to pain, fatigue, cognitive burden, or limited health literacy. In this context, the intended niche of the BCPA is to provide a brief, structured format for documenting key BTcP characteristics, treatment response, and clinically relevant impacts such as sleep and mood during routine consultations.

In response to this critical need, this study aimed to (1) develop a brief clinical tool to rapidly assess the characteristics of BTcP and its impact on sleep and mood, and (2) evaluate the initial psychometric properties of this new instrument, including its reliability, validity, and sensitivity to change for use in clinical settings.

Methods

To achieve the study objectives, the research was conducted in three phases: (1) item generation and content validity testing, (2) inter-rater reliability testing, and (3) sensitivity testing. Internal consistency is not an appropriate aspect of reliability to evaluate because the BCPA is not designed to produce a total score.

Item generation and content validity testing

The BCPA was developed based on an evidence-informed multidimensional framework of BTcP assessment. This framework was informed by classical descriptions and clinical recommendations for BTcP, general principles of pain assessment, and existing BTcP instruments, including the Breakthrough Pain Assessment Tool (BAT).1,3,5,9,10,12 BTcP was conceptualized as a transient pain exacerbation with distinct temporal characteristics, triggers, intensity, patient-perceived impact, and response to rescue medication. Accordingly, the initial item pool was organized into three domains: (1) pain episode characteristics, including pain location, daily frequency, triggers, time to peak intensity, episode duration, and usual intensity (Items 1–6); (2) patient-perceived impact, including the effects of BTcP on sleep quality and mood (Items 7–8); and (3) rescue medication response, including onset of meaningful pain relief, satisfaction with pain relief, and perceived improvement in sleep and mood after rescue medication (Items 9–11). These domains were further refined by literature review, clinical experience, and expert consensus obtained through the PI meetings. The initial item pool was generated by a small group of oncologists, including the first author and colleagues, through a review of relevant literature on the concept and similar tools. The initial version, consisting of 11 Chinese items, was designated as the BCPA. To ensure adequacy and clinical usability, a Delphi study was conducted involving 14 experts who rated the relevance and importance of each item. The Delphi method was chosen over the focus group method to gather consensus in order to prevent superficial discussions influenced by the strong leadership of senior experts. For the purposes of this study, an “expert” was defined as a physician with specialized expertise in BTcP and/or pain instrument development, recognized by their peers as a key opinion leader. Experts were selected based on their clinical experience, involvement in BTcP management, professional recognition within their institutions, and willingness to participate in the Delphi process. The Delphi experts were physicians from major cancer treatment centers across Taiwan, representing institutions from northern, central, and southern Taiwan, with expertise covering medical oncology, hematology-oncology, head and neck oncology, cancer care, and pain medicine. In addition, a methodological consultant provided expertise in instrument development, psychometrics, and statistical analysis. Details of the Delphi expert panel and methodological consultant are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The experts were asked to rate the relevance and importance of each item on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant/important, 2 = somewhat relevant/important, 3 = quite relevant/important, 4 = highly relevant/important). Experts were also asked to provide qualitative feedback on items rated as “not” or “somewhat” relevant/important. Item summary statistics, including the item mean and item-level content validity index,18,19 along with the expert ratings, were provided for subsequent rounds of rating, if needed. A coefficient of variation (CV) of < 0.2 for all items was adopted as the stopping criterion for subsequent Delphi rounds.20 Agreement among experts was defined as an item-level content validity index (I-CVI) of 0.8 or higher for all items. The I-CVI was calculated as the proportion of experts rating an item as quite relevant or highly relevant (ratings of 3 or 4). The scale-level CVI, universal agreement (S-CVI/UA) was calculated as the proportion of items rated 3 or 4 by all experts divided by the total number of items.18 According to Lynn and Polit & Beck, an I-CVI of at least 0.78 is considered acceptable when six or more experts are involved, whereas the acceptable threshold for S-CVI/UA is 0.80.21,22 In addition to the S-CVI/UA, Kendall's W coefficient was computed to evaluate the statistical significance of overall agreement in each Delphi round.

Inter-rater reliability testing

After confirming content validity, we proceeded to test the reliability of the BCPA in patients with BTcP. Since this new tool is designed for clinicians to rapidly assess patients' BTcP through face-to-face interviews, establishing inter-rater reliability is a critical concern. In this study, BTcP was identified as a transient exacerbation of pain occurring despite relatively stable and adequately controlled background pain. To properly identify patients with BTcP, physicians had to evaluate patients’ baseline pain control status and distinguish BTcP from poorly controlled background pain or end-of-dose failure based on their clinical judgment. This assessment procedure was iteratively refined and confirmed during the PI meeting.

Patients treated at the participating hospitals were included in the study if they met the following criteria: (1) Age between 18 and 80 years. (2) Experienced BTcP for at least two days in the past two weeks, but no more than three times per day (as determined by the physician). (3) Opioid tolerance (since fentanyl buccal soluble film, the analgesic to be used in the sensitivity testing of the BCPA, is contraindicated in opioid-naïve patients), defined as an oral morphine equivalent of ≥ 60 mg/day for at least one week. Each patient was independently interviewed using the BCPA by a physician and a nurse, with the two interviews conducted less than 3 hours apart. The intended short interval between two interviews was to minimize the potential change of the breakthrough pain being assessed. All items in the BCPA were assessed with reference to a defined time frame (“in the past week”) to ensure consistency in patient responses. To enhance consistency between physician and nurse raters, both raters received standardized orientation before patient interviews. The orientation covered the purpose of the BCPA, the intended meaning of each item, the defined recall period (“in the past week”), response categories, and standardized wording for administration. Raters were instructed to administer the BCPA as a structured interview and to avoid adding non-standard explanations unless clarification was necessary.

The linear weighted kappa statistic was used to assess the agreement between two raters for items with ordinal response options (Items 2, 3, 4, 5, and 9), while the two-way random, absolute agreement, single intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC (2.1)] was used to evaluate the agreement between two raters for items with continuous responses (Items 6, 7, 8, 10, and 11). We anticipated a moderate weighted kappa value of 0.5.23 To test whether a kappa of 0.5 is significant at a one-tailed alpha level of 0.05 and with a power of 0.9, the required sample size was determined to be 30.

Sensitivity testing

To evaluate the sensitivity of the BCPA as an assessment tool in detecting changes, we conducted an exploratory before-and-after assessment to examine whether selected item scores differed significantly after routine-care use of fentanyl buccal soluble film, a prescription opioid medication used for the management of BTcP in opioid-tolerant adult patients.24 This sensitivity testing was intended to assess whether the BCPA could detect expected changes in rescue medication-related outcomes, rather than to evaluate treatment efficacy. Fentanyl buccal soluble film was selected because it was the first rapid-onset opioid introduced to the Taiwanese market on April 12, 2013.25 Accordingly, physicians have gained extensive prescribing experience with this agent. In addition, the use of a single rescue medication provided a relatively standardized clinical context for assessing the sensitivity to change of rescue medication-related BCPA items and reduced heterogeneity that might arise from evaluating different rescue analgesics. The decision to prescribe fentanyl buccal soluble film, including the dose and timing of use, was made by the treating oncologists according to routine clinical judgment, the approved label, and local practice. The study did not assign, randomize, or mandate fentanyl buccal soluble film treatment. Patients with unsatisfactory control of BTcP were invited to participate in this study. The patients’ treating oncologists conducted interviews using the BCPA before prescribing fentanyl buccal soluble film and again 7 days later, as pain outcomes in outpatients are typically assessed at the subsequent weekly visit.

The eligibility criteria for sensitivity testing were similar to those used for inter-rater reliability testing, including BTcP episodes in the preceding two weeks and opioid tolerance. Initially, eligible patients were required to be aged 18–80 years; however, during the study period, an IRB-approved protocol amendment removed the upper age limit, after which adult patients aged ≥ 18 years were eligible. All patients included in the final sensitivity analysis met the IRB-approved eligibility criteria applicable at the time of enrollment. Patients were excluded if they had a high risk of opioid-induced respiratory depression, were pregnant or breastfeeding, had prior use of fentanyl buccal soluble film, or had a history of hypersensitivity or intolerance to fentanyl.

Three BCPA items (Items 9, 10, and 11), which evaluate the effects of rescue analgesics, were selected to test sensitivity. These items measure the onset of analgesic action, the extent of pain relief, and the degree of improvement in sleep and mood, respectively. An onset time of 15 minutes or less was considered an indicator of a good response to the rescue analgesic. We also anticipated that, after using fentanyl buccal soluble film for breakthrough pain, patients would report higher levels of satisfaction with their pain relief and perceived improvements in sleep and mood.

McNemar's test was conducted to compare the percentages of patients who experienced pain relief within 15 minutes before and after using fentanyl buccal soluble film. A paired t test was employed to assess the differences in the degree of pain relief and perceived improvements in sleep and mood. Additionally, the reliable change index (RCI) was computed for Items 10 and 11 to determine whether the change scores were reliable. RCI was originally proposed by Jacobson and Truax (1991) to determine whether the change in an individual's score exceeds what would be expected from measurement error. It is calculated by dividing the difference between the post-test and pre-test scores by the standard error of the difference score, where standard error of difference = 2×standarderrorofmeasurement(SEM). An RCI greater than 1.96 indicates that the change is considered reliable.26 In addition, inter-item correlations among the BCPA items in the pre-fentanyl buccal soluble film sample were examined using Spearman's rank correlation.

The sample size determination for the sensitivity study was based on the difference in the proportion of patients with an analgesic onset of less than 15 minutes. For the McNemar test, with an odds ratio of 5, α = 0.05, and a power of 0.9, the required sample size was calculated to be 77.

Data presentation and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation (SD), range, and percentages, were used to summarize participant characteristics. The assessment of the content validity, inter-rater reliability, and the sensitivity to change of the BCPA has been described above. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 23.6.2.

Sensitivity of the BCPA

From October 2023 to May 2024, another 107 potential participants completed the pre-test; however, 30 did not complete the post-test, resulting in an attrition rate of 28%. Among these 30 patients, 15 did not proceed to post-test assessment because their existing rescue medication already provided effective BTcP relief within 15 minutes. Other reasons included absence of BTcP episodes at follow-up (n = 8), patient decision not to change rescue medication or high satisfaction with existing rescue medication (n = 3), death (n = 2), exclusion after eligibility confirmation because the patient exceeded the prespecified age criterion applicable before the IRB-approved amendment removing the upper age limit (n = 1), and loss to follow-up (n = 1). Consequently, data from 77 patients across ten teaching hospitals were included in the sensitivity analysis of the BCPA. Comparison of baseline characteristics between participants who completed the post-test and those who did not revealed that completers tended to have higher background pain intensity, higher oral morphine equivalent (OME) doses, and a greater proportion of patients with head and neck cancer (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S2). During the pre-post sensitivity testing period, the ATC analgesic dose remained unchanged in 54 of 77 patients (70.1%), decreased in 6 patients (7.8%), and increased in 17 patients (22.1%). Discussion

Main findings

This study aimed to develop a brief tool enabling clinicians to rapidly assess the characteristics of BTcP in clinical settings and evaluate its psychometric properties. Following three rounds of modification in a Delphi study, the final Chinese-language BCPA demonstrated excellent content validity, with an I-CVI of 1.0 for all items. The results of inter-rater reliability testing indicated that most BCPA items exhibited satisfactory inter-rater reliability. The exceptions were Items 4 and 5, which had relatively lower weighted kappa values, though these values still exceeded 0.5. When applied to patients with cancer who received fentanyl buccal soluble film for their BTcP, the Chinese-language BCPA demonstrated effectiveness in detecting the expected changes, especially in items related to pain relief. However, given the absence of a control group, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting sensitivity to change of the instrument rather than treatment efficacy.

One possible reason for the relatively lower inter-rater reliability for Item 4 (“How long does it usually take for your breakthrough pain to reach maximum severity?”) is the high prevalence of quick onset in the sample. Approximately 75% of participants reported an onset of less than 5 minutes. This finding is consistent with previous studies, which have shown that peak breakthrough pain often occurs within 3–5 minutes.29 The well-known “base rate problem” paradox explains that a high prevalence rate can lead to high chance agreement, which in turn results in a low kappa value.30 Another possible reason may be the unclear phrasing of the time interval. For example, one response option for Item 4 (5–10 minutes) was worded as “over 5 minutes but within 10 minutes.” This overly precise wording may have confused respondents and contributed to inconsistent responses across the two interviews. In future revisions and administration of the Chinese-language BCPA, efforts should be made to ensure that the wording remains as simple and clear as possible. Still another potential source of the low kappa value may be rater bias. However, the data collected show an observed agreement of 0.8 and a bias index of 0.1, indicating that rater bias is unlikely to be the major source of the low kappa. A similar explanation applies to the low weighted kappa found for Item 5 (“How long does your breakthrough pain usually last?”). In this reliability sample, pain exceeding 60 minutes was reported by approximately 82% of participants.

One reason the RCI for Item 11 (“Overall, how effective are your rescue medicines in improving your sleep and mood?”) falls below the selected criterion of 1.96 is that this item does not differentiate between the effects of pain medication on sleep and mood. For some patients, the medication may improve sleep but not mood, or vice versa, making it difficult to provide a definitive response. Future revisions should consider separating these constructs into two distinct items: one evaluating the effect of pain medication on sleep and the other evaluating its effect on mood. This modification would not only improve item clarity but also allow for a more appropriate assessment of sensitivity by evaluating sleep and mood as independent outcome domains.

From the descriptive data of the pre-fentanyl buccal soluble film sample, a possible ceiling effect was observed for Items 6–8, which represent pain intensity, sleep impact, and mood impact, respectively. The clustering of ratings toward the upper end of the scale (0–10) may reflect the nature of inadequately controlled breakthrough pain, as suggested in the literature.8,13 Pain assessment tools are particularly vulnerable to ceiling effects, especially in BTcP, because the pain often occurs rapidly and is intense and severe. One consequence of a ceiling effect is a reduced ability of the items to differentiate between patients with severe pain and those with extremely severe pain. The benefit of increasing the scale range may be offset by increased confusion for patients when responding. However, the current scale (0–10) still preserved room for improvement toward lower scores following treatment. Post-hoc analyses showed that the difference scores between the pre- and post-test assessments for all three items were statistically significant (P < 0.05; data not shown because these were not the primary outcome items selected for this study). Nevertheless, ceiling effects may attenuate correlations with external variables, thereby reducing criterion-related validity. In Supplementary Table S3, the mean score for Item 6, which assessed the usual intensity of BTcP, decreased slightly from 7.96 to 7.57 between the pre- and post-test assessments. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because Item 6 was included to describe the distribution of the Chinese version of the BCPA item responses but was not one of the prespecified items selected for sensitivity testing. In addition, fentanyl buccal soluble film, as a short-acting rescue medication, should not be interpreted as directly altering the intrinsic usual intensity of recurrent BTcP episodes over a one-week period. The small decrease may reflect patients’ overall perception of improved pain experience after treatment, and the possibility of recall bias or a halo effect cannot be excluded.

In examining the inter-item correlations, the high correlation between Item 10 and Item 11 suggests potential conceptual overlap. Both items assess the effects of rescue pain medication, with Item 10 focusing on pain relief and Item 11 focusing on improvements in sleep and mood. Although some degree of correlation was expected because both items involve rescue pain medication, the high correlation warrants careful consideration of the conceptual meaning of each item. For example, it is possible that improvements in sleep or mood may represent an important component of patients’ perceived satisfaction with pain relief. The direction for future refinement of the BCPA may depend on its intended purpose. If rapid assessment is the primary goal, the item assessing the effect of pain relief may be retained while the item assessing improvements in sleep and mood could be removed. In contrast, if a more comprehensive assessment of functional outcomes is desired, Item 10 could be removed and additional items assessing other functional domains beyond sleep and mood could be added.

Although the definition of BTcP was well recognized by all participating physicians, the background pain level was relatively high in both the reliability and sensitivity samples. Of note, background pain intensity was extracted from medical records and reflected the most severe pain documented during recent follow-up visits; therefore, these values may not necessarily represent continuously uncontrolled baseline pain at the exact time of BCPA administration. Nevertheless, the relatively high background pain level suggests that some episodes may have occurred alongside fluctuations in background pain control, rather than representing strictly classical BTcP. The high background pain may be partly explained by the predominance of patients with head and neck cancer in our cohort. Many of these patients were undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy, during which treatment-related toxicities such as radiation-induced mucositis and pharyngitis tend to worsen progressively. As a result, background pain may become increasingly difficult to control, contributing to higher baseline pain levels. This issue may have implications for construct validity and should therefore be considered when interpreting the current preliminary validation findings; future studies with more strictly defined patient populations and better-controlled baseline pain are warranted.

The Chinese-language BCPA and the BAT share several core items assessing BTcP: location, frequency, triggers, episode duration, typical intensity, and measures of analgesic effectiveness and onset. They differ in scope and intended clinical use: the Chinese-language BCPA uniquely includes items on time to peak intensity, the impact of breakthrough pain on sleep and mood, and an item assessing how rescue medication affects sleep and mood, whereas the BAT includes additional items not found in the Chinese-language BCPA such as worst-episode severity, whether anything relieves the pain, the degree of distress and interference with daily life, and details of usual analgesics (type and dose) (Supplementary Table S4). Therefore, the BAT remains a more comprehensive instrument for detailed BTcP assessment. In contrast, the Chinese-language BCPA was designed as a brief, structured, clinician-administered tool to support rapid assessment and treatment monitoring in routine oncology practice, while also documenting clinically relevant impacts such as sleep and mood. These complementary strengths suggest opportunities to harmonize item sets for a more comprehensive assessment. Importantly, the Chinese-language BCPA was developed based on a multidimensional conceptual framework that considers BTcP as a complex clinical phenomenon involving pain characteristics, functional impact, and response to treatment. Rather than serving as a strict diagnostic tool for BTcP, the Chinese-language BCPA is intended to characterize episodic pain exacerbations and their clinical consequences. This distinction is particularly relevant in real-world settings, where the differentiation between classical BTcP and inadequately controlled background pain may be challenging, and pain exacerbations may exist along a spectrum.

Limitations

The newly developed Chinese-language BCPA has demonstrated satisfactory preliminary reliability and validity, supporting its utility for quickly capturing the characteristics of patients' breakthrough pain. In addition, it may serve as an outcome measure in clinical studies evaluating the pharmacological management of BTcP. However, several limitations need to be addressed. First, we did not solicit the viewpoints of target patients regarding the BCPA using techniques such as cognitive interviews, and this lack of patient input may pose a significant threat to content validity. Second, the Chinese-language BCPA was tested only in a sample of patients with cancer in Taiwan. Its global applicability could be enhanced by developing an English version using a rigorous translation methodology. The World Health Organization six-step translation strategy and cultural adaptation procedure31—comprising forward translation, expert panel review, backward translation, pretesting and cognitive interviewing, final version development, and documentation of the process—will be followed in future development of the English version. Third, to minimize the questionnaire burden on patients experiencing pain, evaluation of the Chinese-language BCPA's psychometric properties was limited in this preliminary study. For example, criterion-related validity should have been evaluated by comparing the Chinese-language BCPA results with those of the BAT, which is a self-report questionnaire. Further work is needed to comprehensively evaluate its psychometric properties such as item analysis, internal structure, and criterion-related and predictive validity. In addition, because pain is inherently subjective, a self-report component may be incorporated in future refinements of the BCPA. Fourth, in evaluation of inter-rater reliability, although a short interval between the two assessments was intentionally used to minimize changes in patients' breakthrough pain status, we could not completely eliminate the possibility of recall bias resulting from patients' memory of the previous assessment. Consequently, the observed level of agreement between raters may have been overestimated. Fifth, the relatively small sample size (n = 30) may have contributed to wide confidence intervals for the kappa estimates of certain items in the Chinese-language BCPA. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to enhance the precision of these estimates. Sixth, breakthrough pain may differ across cancer populations due to variations in tumor biology, disease involvement, and treatment-related factors. Although the current sample included patients with various cancer diagnoses, the most participants had head and neck cancer, which may limit the generalizability of the study findings. Future studies should include more cancer types. Seventh, the study sample included only opioid-tolerant patients due to the prescription requirements of fentanyl buccal soluble film. This sampling criterion limits the external validity of the findings. Therefore, the psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the BCPA evaluated in this study may not be generalizable to patients who are opioid-naïve or to those receiving other analgesics instead of fentanyl buccal soluble film. Finally, the sensitivity testing was included as an exploratory component of this tool-development study to evaluate the Chinese-language BCPA's ability to detect expected changes in rescue medication-related outcomes, rather than to provide a definitive evaluation of fentanyl buccal soluble film efficacy. Among 107 patients who completed the pre-test assessment, 30 did not complete the post-test, resulting in an attrition rate of 28%. Comparison between completers and non-completers in baseline characteristics indicated that completers had a higher background pain intensity, OME doses, and a greater proportion of patients with head and neck cancer. Because most non-completers did not proceed to post-test assessment due to adequate relief with existing rescue medication, absence of BTcP episodes at follow-up, or lack of need for treatment modification, post-test completers may represent a selected subgroup with ongoing BTcP symptoms and greater need for rescue-medication modification. This potential selection bias may have increased the likelihood of detecting pre–post changes in rescue medication-related items and may limit the generalizability of the sensitivity findings. Therefore, the sensitivity testing results should be interpreted as preliminary. The absence of a control group was also a major methodological constraint. Without a control group, biases such as placebo effects, regression to the mean, and expectancy effects may have been introduced, thereby limiting causal interpretation of the observed differences between the pre- and post-assessments. Future analyses using a larger dataset of patients receiving fentanyl buccal soluble film in routine clinical practice should further evaluate factors associated with non-completion and prospectively assess changes in background pain control.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Based on the initial validation findings, the preliminary Chinese-language BCPA shows promise as a tool to support clinical assessment and decision-making in routine oncology practice. At the individual patient level, this new tool may help clinicians rapidly identify key BTcP characteristics, including frequency, triggers, temporal pattern, intensity, and response to rescue medication. This information may support discussions and decisions regarding optimization of background analgesia, adjustment of rescue medication, and evaluation of whether the current treatment provides sufficiently rapid and satisfactory relief. By explicitly assessing the impact of BTcP on sleep and mood, this new tool may also help clinicians identify patients who may benefit from additional supportive care or closer follow-up. At the group level, Items 9 and 10, but not Item 11, of the preliminary Chinese-language BCPA may have potential as practical outcome measures for evaluating therapeutic interventions for BTcP in future clinical studies.

Conclusions

The newly developed Chinese-language BCPA has demonstrated preliminary evidence of inter-rater reliability and content validity, supporting its utility as a practical instrument for physicians and nurses to efficiently assess BTcP characteristics while minimizing the burden of completing a self-administered questionnaire on patients. The preliminary Chinese-language BCPA also demonstrated partial sensitivity to change in selected rescue medication-related items. In light of the current evidence of sensitivity, future studies that incorporate a control group would further substantiate the use of BCPA as an outcome variable for validating the effects of BTcP treatment. Further psychometric evaluation, including assessments of criterion-related validity, construct validity, factor structure, and cognitive interviewing, is warranted.

Recommendations

Incorporate the Chinese-language BCPA into clinical practice to standardize clinician assessment of BTcP, especially to identify patients with pain-related impacts on sleep and mood.

Develop and validate additional items for sleep and mood, as separate constructs, and consider adding items on psychological distress, functional interference, quality of life, and side effects to increase comprehensiveness.

Compare the Chinese-language BCPA results against established BTcP instruments, pain diaries, and objective treatment outcomes to quantify criterion and convergent validity.

To increase generalizability to broader populations, cross-cultural validation and testing in more diverse clinical settings are warranted.
