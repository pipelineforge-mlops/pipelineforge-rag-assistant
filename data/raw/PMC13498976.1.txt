Vanazacaftor-Tezacaftor-Deutivacaftor (VTD) is a novel cystic fibrosis transmembrane receptor (CFTR) modulator therapy. This development added another highly effective modulator therapy to the market as an alternative to the standard of care, Elexacaftor-Tezacaftor-Ivacaftor (ETI). Phase III trial data comparing VTD and ETI demonstrated non-inferiority in outcomes and similar adverse effect profile and rates. Here we present a case series demonstrating known and potentially unknown side effects, specific to VTD. From January 2025 to June 2025, 38 out of 400 patients at our adult CF center, were switched from ETI to VTD. Six of those patients experienced potential VTD related adverse events. Liver function testing (LFT) elevations were seen in 2/6 cases, worsening pulmonary symptoms in 3/6 cases, dermatologic manifestations in 2/6 cases, hypertension in 1/6 cases, neuropsychiatric symptoms in 2/6 cases, and gastrointestinal symptoms in 2/6 cases. Notably, one case led to hospitalization, with development of a severe rash, thrombocytopenia, and LFT elevations. In each case, all symptoms and laboratory manifestations resolved within days after switching back to ETI. This case series demonstrates both previously recognized and unrecognized adverse effects of VTD in a “real-world population.” The majority of our patients’ presenting symptoms were described in phase III clinical trials, including LFT elevations, pulmonary exacerbations, rash, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. On the contrary, thrombocytopenia, gastrointestinal manifestations, and hypertension have not been described thus far with VTD. Further research and more robust monitoring are needed to determine the overall incidence and risk of lesser-known side effects of novel CFTR modulators.

1 Background

Vanzacaftor-tezacaftor-deutivacaftor (VTD) is the newest Cystic Fibrosis transmembrane receptor (CFTR) modulator combination approved for use in December 2024. This development added another agent to the class of highly effective modulator therapy (HEMT), serving as an alternative to the previous standard-of-care, elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor (ETI), which was approved in 2019. The advent of ETI was a major step forward in the revolution of cystic fibrosis (CF) management, providing patients with a therapy that led to less frequent exacerbations, improved lung function, and positively impacted quality of life. Recent phase 3 clinical trials have shown that VTD is non-inferior to ETI in terms of improvement in lung function; however, VTD did lower sweat chloride concentrations significantly more than ETI, indicating greater physiologic efficacy [1]. Another major benefit of VTD is once daily dosing, which can improve medication adherence.

Adverse effect monitoring is an important aspect of post-approval research for newer therapies. Initial phase 3 data from ETI reported events most commonly related to worsening pulmonary symptomatology, diarrhea, fatigue, and oropharyngeal pain. Serious adverse events included elevations in liver function tests (LFTs), rash, and rise in blood pressure [2]. After six years on the market, other side effects have been observed as well, specifically neuropsychiatric issues such as insomnia, concentration difficulties and memory problems [3]. Notably, these side effects are primarily related to cognition itself and are not correlated with worsening anxiety and depression [3,4]. Data from clinic trials with VTD demonstrated both similar adverse effect profile and rates compared with ETI, however there is a lack of real-world observational studies as VTD was only recently approved for use in the general population [1]. These observations are important for clinicians to recognize and monitor as a larger proportion of patients transition from ETI to VTD. Here we present a series of six cases demonstrating known and potentially unknown adverse effects of VTD treatment, along with clinical course following discontinuation.

2.1 Case 1

The first case follows a 29-year-old male CF patient (F508del/G542X) with cirrhosis secondary to CF-related liver disease (CFLD), hearing loss, pancreatic insufficiency, CF-related diabetes (CFRD), iron deficiency anemia, gastroesophageal reflex disorder (GERD), and tobacco use disorder. He transitioned from VTD from ETI in January 2025 after multiple hospital stays for CF exacerbations and an elevation in LFTs in November and December 2024. However, within days of VTD initiation, he reported increased productive cough consisting of thickened, purulent sputum and associated difficulty breathing. These symptoms were persistent for 2-3 weeks until he was hospitalized for a CF exacerbation and was subsequently started on intravenous antibiotics for several days. The patient's forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) dropped from a baseline of 1.67L (39% predicted) to 1.44L (34% predicted) on admission. The patient continued to have thick sputum production and dyspnea while inpatient despite aggressive airway clearance and antibiotic therapy. The decision was made to transition back to ETI. His respiratory symptoms resolved, and secretion burden improved within 24 hours of discontinuation of VTD. His FEV1 returned to baseline on day of hospital discharge at 1.61L (38% predicted). At his post hospital discharge follow-up visit 4 weeks later, the patient maintained his FEV1 at 1.64L (39% predicted).

2.2 Case 2

Our next case involves a 21-year-old female CF patient (508del/G542x) with CFRD, iron deficiency anemia, Mycobacterium abscessus (MAB) infection (diagnosed in May 2023) on chronic therapy of clofazimine, omadacycline, and linezolid, and experimental phage therapy (started in April 2025).

She was transitioned from ETI to VTD in March 2025 due to recurrent elevations in LFTs while taking ETI. Since initiation of VTD, the patient reported improvement in sputum production. However, 2 months into VTD therapy, she developed a significant rash requiring hospitalization. On the day of admission, clofazimine, omadacycline, and linezolid for chronic MAB was discontinued. The rash was described as painful, non-pruritic, and centrifugal in distribution. She denied any associated fevers, myalgias, or arthralgias. The physical exam revealed pink and violaceous non-blanching papules, some of which coalesced to form plaques. A skin biopsy was obtained which showed a mixed pattern of eosinophilic and neutrophilic infiltrates. Dermatology felt that the clinical symptoms, physical exam findings, and biopsy results were reflective of a morbilliform drug eruption versus early onset Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS). In addition to the skin rash, the patient developed thrombocytopenia (104 k/uLA) and elevated LFTs (ALT 56 U/L, AST 41 U/L, ALP 230 U/L). Hepatic and biliary ultrasound obtained during hospitalization was insignificant. No other medication had been started aside from VTD, so it was decided to discontinue VTD and transition back to ETI. At post discharge hospital follow-up 3 weeks later, the rash had fully resolved and repeat lab work showed normalization of platelet count (104 K/μL to 363 K/μL) and LFTs (ALT 56 U/L, AST 41 U/L, ALP 230 U/L to 23, 20 U/L, 139 U/L respectively). The chronic MAB treatment regimen was restarted at this visit, and she has not had any further rashes or lab abnormalities noted. She began phage therapy after her hospital discharge in late April 2025 and has not had any associated adverse events.

2.3 Case 3

Case 3 is a 22-year-old male CF patient (Q98X/G551D) with pancreatic insufficiency and cytokine release syndrome (diagnosed in 2018) who was transitioned to VTD from ETI in May 2025 after the patient expressed interest in the new medication due to its once daily dosing. At that time, he was tolerating ETI well but admitted to missing doses occasionally given his busy work schedule. After 2-3 weeks of taking VTD, the patient reported onset of elevated blood pressure readings (systolic 140-160/diastolic 80-90), heart palpitations, and cognitive disturbances which included brain fog and slowed speech. His blood pressure prior to transition to VTD in clinic was 112/68. He was quickly transitioned back to ETI with complete resolution of the symptoms within days. His blood pressure returned to his baseline as well at 113/84. Other causes of hypertension including renal disease, changes in diet or supplementations, were ruled out by a normal basic metabolic panel (BMP) without electrolyte disturbances or creatinine changes, and an unremarkable urinalysis. The patient experienced these palpitations while at home and felt they may have been associated with anxiety. While we could not obtain an electrocardiogram at the time of his symptoms, from a patient perspective, his symptoms began after VTD initiation and resolved with VTD discontinuation. The patient reported that he has not had any further palpitations after transitioning back to ETI. Regarding his cognitive changes including brain fog while on VTI, work-up to rule out other diagnoses were negative. Vitamin levels, including vitamin A, D-25 hydroxy, and B12 were within normal limits. His complete blood count was also normal, including hemoglobin, platelets, and white blood cell count. Iron studies, including ferritin, iron saturation, and total iron binding capacity were normal and did not demonstrate anemia. His morning testosterone level was also within normal limits. A hemoglobin A1c of 5.7 was also measured, indicating adequate blood glucose control.

2.4 Case 4

Case 4 is a 39-year-old male CF patient (F508del/F508del) with chronic rhinosinusitis, pancreatic insufficiency, and gout who transitioned to VTD in April 2025 after experiencing insomnia with ETI. Two months after starting VTD, his alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was noted to have acutely increased from 66 U/L to 2616 U/L. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and total bilirubin all remained within the normal range (respectively 17 U/L, 22 U/L, 9 U/L, 0.8 mg/dL). Clinically, he remained asymptomatic. Hepatic and biliary ultrasound revealed biliary sludge, but otherwise no abnormality was seen. After consulting with hepatology, VTD and febuxostat were held, although they were notably uncertain if this abnormality was hepatobiliary in origin as both bilirubin and GGT were normal. Following these changes, the alkaline phosphatase level slowly normalized over the following 2 months with a trend of 401 U/L, 266 U/L, 151 U/L, 108 U/L, and finally 85 U/L. Given this improvement, further imaging and invasive procedures such as liver biopsy were deferred. ETI and febuxostat were restarted in June 2025 without complication and the ALP remained within normal limits.

2.5 Case 5

Case 5 is a 36-year-old CF female (F508del/F508del) with chronic bacterial airway colonization (Methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas, and Stenotrophomonas), pancreatic insufficiency, chronic rhinosinusitis, and CFRD. She also has a history of depression, anxiety, and prior pulmonary embolus. She started VTD in April 2025 after several years of stable treatment with ETI due to concerns about mood disturbances as a side effect of ETI. 1-2 weeks after initiation of VTD, she began to experience increased dyspnea and hemoptysis, consistent with her typical symptoms during a CF exacerbation. She also noted gastrointestinal distress with frequent loose, greasy stools. Additionally, at that time, her FEV1 decreased from a baseline of 2.3L (88% predicted) to 2.17L (83% predicted). She switched back to ETI in June 2025, and her respiratory symptoms fully resolved subjectively after one month of treatment, although there was no improvement in her FEV1 at 2.18L (83% predicted) at her clinic follow-up visit. In this case, symptom onset and resolution correlated directly with VTD use, implicating it as the most likely causative agent. Malabsorption and pancreatic insufficiency were well controlled before this medication change and only began after 1-2 weeks of treatment, making improper absorption of VTD an unlikely factor contributing to her symptoms.

2.6 Case 6

Case 6 is a 63-year-old CF female (F508del/c.1399C > T) with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, reactive airway disease, chronic joint pain, iron deficiency anemia, and vitamin D deficiency. She was hospitalized for a CF exacerbation secondary to a viral upper respiratory infection in May 2025. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) standard and acid fast bacilli (AFB) cultures grew Mycobacterium paragordonae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas fluorescens. She was discharged after 3 days of observation with a fourteen-day course of trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. ETI was switched to VTD on the date of discharge. She was seen in the post discharge hospital follow-up clinic 2 weeks later. At that time, she reported worsening respiratory clearance and cough. She also noticed worsening anxiety, panic attacks, and generalized fatigue. Her FEV1 had acutely decreased from admission date of the aforementioned hospitalization (2.78L, 113% predicted down to 1.74L, 70% predicted), although this was attributed to the resolving infection and CF exacerbation. Given these confounding factors, she remained on VTD at this clinic visit despite her respiratory symptoms with plans for close follow-up. She returned to clinic again 3 weeks later with persistence of her symptoms as well as new symptoms of increased bruising with associated skin sensitivity over these areas and bowel irritability in the form of tenesmus and diarrhea. Her fatigue remained so severe that she could not tolerate more than 15 minutes of activity, when previously she could work outside for multiple hours without issue. However, at this visit, her FEV1 had returned to her baseline (2.55L, 103% predicted), and other laboratory markers including LFTs and platelets remained at baseline. A decision was made to switch back to ETI at this visit given the continued respiratory and neuropsychiatric symptoms. All symptoms resolved by the next follow-up appointment one month later with a stable FEV1 2.62L (106% predicted).

3 Discussion and conclusions

These patient cases demonstrate both previously reported and unreported adverse events while taking VTD that required discontinuation of the medication. In prior phase 2 clinical trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of VTD, investigators found a relatively tolerable safety profile, with only 3 out of the 66 patients (4.5%) of those in the VTD arm developing adverse effects leading to drug discontinuation [5]. In phase 3 clinical trials comparing VTD to ETI, the rate of adverse events leading to discontinuation of the drug regimen were exactly equal between groups (4%) [1]. The most notable adverse effects from this study include pulmonary exacerbation, upper respiratory symptoms, neuropsychiatric symptoms, diarrhea, fatigue, and headache, although LFT elevations were the most frequent contributor for drug discontinuation [1]. Generally, the rates and types of adverse events seem to be similar with VTD and ETI, several of which are represented in our case series.

Similar to ETI, the most common adverse events in patients treated with VTD were related to common CF pulmonary symptoms, such as infective exacerbations, cough, and increased sputum production [1]. This was seen in three of the cases presented here (Case 1, Case 5, and Case 6). This was also reflected in Phase 3 clinical trials, with 96% of the pooled VTD group and 96% of the pooled ETI group having at least one adverse event, most of which were consistent with the above manifestations such as infectious exacerbations (28% VTD, 32% ETI) [1]. However, no patients in the pooled VTD treatment regimen had a reduction in FEV1. Here, we present two patients with decreased FEV1 after initiation of VTD (Case 5 and Case 6). It is worth noting that in Case 6, the initial decrease in FEV1 was attributed to a CF exacerbation, but it did persist beyond the expected recovery period and symptom improvement correlated with switching back to ETI. In general, these findings support close monitoring of lung function with spirometry after initiation of VTD as part of routine safety observation.

Elevated LFTs were a significant adverse effect in phase 3 trial data, with 7 patients from the VTD arm developing elevated transaminases leading to treatment discontinuation [1]. Among our cases, two patients (Case 2 and Case 4) exhibited clinically significant elevations in LFTs after initiation of VTD. Case 4 had a solitary elevation in ALP, although there was no clear cause seen on hepatic ultrasound. In phase 3 trial data, one patient (0.2%) in the ETI group and 2 patients (0.4%) of the VTD group had a significant elevation in ALP that led to discontinuation of the treatment regimen [1]. It is unclear based on data reporting whether these patients underwent further workup for the elevated laboratory value such as abdominal imaging or serum GGT levels for further differentiation between hepatobiliary involvement and bone-related etiologies. Our patient had a normal GGT, and a hepatic ultrasound that demonstrated mild biliary sludge but no other abnormality. Given this difference compared to other cases and other examples from trial data which showed disturbances in ALT and AST as well, clinicians should consider bone or isolated small bile duct etiology. Routine hepatic function testing is already an important aspect of monitoring with HEMT; however, it is important to be mindful of different patterns and mechanisms of liver damage apart from hepatocellular disease.

Regarding rash events in phase 3 trial data, 58 out of 480 (11%) patients in the VTD arm and 38 out of 491 (8%) patients in the ETI arm exhibited at least one rash event, most of which were considered mild to moderate [1]. Only one patient had a rash event that led to discontinuation of VTD [1]. This occurred one month after treatment initiation, similar in onset time to our patient in Case 2. The rash in our case was on the severe end of the spectrum and required hospitalization. Although the ultimate diagnosis of our case was still debated, there are no known individual case reports detailing DRESS syndrome in a patient taking VTD. Furthermore, it was not specified if the patients in the aforementioned trial underwent skin biopsy or had clear diagnosis of their rashes. Clinicians should be mindful of potentially severe dermatologic reactions, which may require hospitalization for diagnosis and management.

Interestingly, in phase 3 trial data, there was no identified clinically relevant changes in mean blood pressure readings or other changes in vital signs, electrocardiogram findings, and oxygen saturation during the study period in both treatment arms [1]. Our case series presents one patient (Case 3) who developed elevated blood pressure readings two weeks after initiation of VTD, which resolved after discontinuation of the treatment regimen. Prior data from the phase 3 trial of ETI compared to ivacaftor or tezacaftor-ivacaftor showed no clinically significant reports of hypertension [2,6]. However, some case series concerning ETI have described instances of new onset hypertension requiring antihypertensive medication after initiation of ETI [7,8]. This may suggest that CFTR modulators, including VTD, may, in rare instances, cause clinically significant elevated blood pressures warranting continued monitoring and careful consideration in patients with increased cardiovascular risk factors.

Finally, Case 2 also highlights thrombocytopenia coinciding with the previously discussed rash, both of which resolved after VTD discontinuation. Platelet disorders are not a known adverse effect of VTD seen in prior clinical trials [1]. Furthermore, thrombocytopenia is not a recognized or expected adverse event with prior CFTR modulators [[9], [10], [11]]. Preclinical data has shown that CFTR modulators may improve osteoblast function, but there is no evidence of beneficial or adverse events to bone marrow suppression or hematopoiesis in humans [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. It is possible that the thrombocytopenia was not a direct action of the medication itself, but rather a result of the generalized inflammatory reaction leading to bone marrow suppression. Alternative etiologies of thrombocytopenia could also be considered such as infection or nutritional deficiencies.

Overall, the adverse effect profile of VTD seen in these patients is comparable to prior clinical trial data, although we do highlight some rarer and even unreported manifestations. Continued studies evaluating post-market VTD safety and adverse events are needed to modify treatment guidelines, dictate required monitoring, such as laboratory and pulmonary function measures, and further investigate long term effects. In addition, it is worth discussing with patients who intend to switch from ETI to VTD that the side effect profile might be individually different and close monitoring during the switch is of great importance. Future studies will require larger sample sizes, diversity in patient populations, and longer study duration times. Clinicians will need to develop a framework for monitoring and discontinuation of novel CFTR modulators when patients develop severe adverse effects, especially within the period of initiation or transition from older therapies.
