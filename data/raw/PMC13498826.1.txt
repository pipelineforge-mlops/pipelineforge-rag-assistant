Background:

Recurrence after minimally invasive hallux valgus correction remains a concern, particularly following combined minimally invasive transverse–Akin (META) and distal minimally invasive metatarsal osteotomies (DMMOs). The influence of deformity severity on recurrence remains unclear.

Methods:

This retrospective multicentre cohort study included 120 patients treated with combined META and DMMO between 2019 and 2024. Patients were stratified into mild, moderate, and severe deformity groups (n = 40 each). Radiographic outcomes and PROMIS scores were assessed preoperatively and at 3 and 12 months. Recurrence was defined as hallux valgus angle (HVA) > 20°. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of recurrence.

Results:

All groups demonstrated significant radiographic correction and clinically meaningful PROMIS improvement. Recurrence rates at 12 months were 0% in mild, 30% in moderate, and 42.5% in severe deformities (P < .0001). Baseline HVA independently predicted recurrence (OR: 1.13 per degree; 95% CI: 1.07-1.21; P < .001).

Conclusion:

Combined META+DMMO was associated with substantial functional outcomes; however, recurrence increases substantially with deformity severity.

Level of Evidence:

Level III, retrospective cohort study.

Introduction

Hallux valgus (HV) is among the most prevalent forefoot deformities, affecting up to 23% of adults and more than one-third of the elderly population, with a clear female predominance.1-3 It is characterized by progressive lateral deviation of the hallux and medial deviation of the first metatarsal, leading to pain, footwear difficulties, altered gait, and decreased quality of life. 4 Surgical correction remains the mainstay for symptomatic cases unresponsive to conservative management; however, postoperative recurrence continues to represent a major challenge, with reported rates ranging from 10% to >30%, depending on surgical technique, patient selection, and radiographic definitions.5-7 Factors consistently associated with recurrence include higher preoperative hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA), sesamoid malalignment, and residual postoperative deformity.8,9

In recent years, minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has gained popularity because of its reduced soft tissue trauma, faster rehabilitation, and improved cosmetic outcomes compared with traditional open techniques.10-12 Among MIS procedures, particularly in its contemporary third- (minimally invasive chevron and akin [MICA]) and fourth-generation (minimally invasive transverse–Akin [META]) adaptations has shown excellent outcomes, including substantial radiographic correction, high patient satisfaction, and low complication rates.13-15 Nevertheless, recurrence after MIS bunion correction remains a concern, particularly in patients presenting with more severe deformities, with radiographic recurrence rates reported between 3% and 17%.16-18

The distal minimally invasive metatarsal osteotomy (DMMO) is frequently combined with MICA to address concomitant metatarsalgia by decompressing the lesser metatarsal heads, redistributing plantar pressure, and restoring a more physiologic forefoot loading pattern.19-21 Although this combined approach may improve pain and forefoot balance, alteration of the lesser metatarsal parabola may also influence the biomechanical stability of the first ray. Specifically, shortening and lateral translation of the second-fourth metatarsals may reduce the stabilizing support of the central forefoot and alter load-sharing across the medial column. This may theoretically increase susceptibility to medial column drift, first metatarsal rotation, sesamoid imbalance, or gradual loss of correction following hallux valgus surgery, particularly in patients with severe deformity or baseline first-ray instability.20-23

Our previous work demonstrated that although combined MICA+DMMO 2-4 provides marked improvements in alignment and function, a notable proportion of patients exhibit mild radiographic recurrence at short-term follow-up. Although most of these recurrences were asymptomatic, the findings highlighted the potential impact of deformity severity on postoperative stability and the need for further investigation.22,23 Our recent study demonstrated a similar pattern when comparing outcomes of MICA alone vs combined MICA and DMMO. 23 In that cohort, radiographic recurrence was significantly more frequent in the MICA+DMMO group; however, this did not translate into worse patient-reported outcomes. Functional scores and satisfaction levels remained comparable between groups, suggesting that despite higher recurrence rates, the combined procedure may still provide meaningful symptomatic benefit for patients with concomitant metatarsalgia.

The present study aims to assess recurrence rates following META+DMMO 2-3-4 across 3 strata of preoperative hallux valgus severity (mild, moderate, and severe) to determine whether deformity severity itself represents an independent risk factor for recurrence. We hypothesize that patients with more severe baseline deformity may be particularly predisposed to recurrence, despite adherence to the same surgical correction principles. This may reflect greater intrinsic structural deformity, persistent soft tissue imbalance, and altered first-ray biomechanics that are not fully corrected by the addition of DMMO.

Definition of Severity

Preoperative hallux valgus severity is classified according to standard standing foot radiographic parameters based on the HVA and IMA as follows: mild, HVA < 20° and/or IMA = 9-11; moderate, HVA 20°-40° and/or IMA 11°-16°; severe, HVA > 40° and/or IMA > 16°. This classification aligns with previously published thresholds widely adopted in the HV literature and clinical guidelines (Table 1). 24

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective comparative cohort study conducted at 2 high-volume foot and ankle centres specializing in minimally invasive forefoot surgery. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to study initiation. All procedures were performed by fellowship-trained foot and ankle surgeons experienced in fourth-generation minimally invasive transverse–Akin (META) osteotomy and DMMO techniques. The present investigation focuses exclusively on patients treated with combined META+DMMO of the second, third, and fourth metatarsals, stratified into 3 preoperative hallux valgus (HV) severity groups to compare recurrence and functional outcomes.

Patient Selection

We reviewed the records of adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent minimally invasive HV correction with META+DMMO 2-3-4 between January 2019 and December 2024, with a minimum of 12 months of clinical and radiographic follow-up. All patients presented with symptomatic HV unresponsive to ≥6 months of conservative treatment (shoe modifications, orthoses, analgesics, and activity modification) and had clinical evidence of central metatarsalgia (plantar tenderness under the lesser metatarsal heads). Inclusion criteria were symptomatic hallux valgus requiring surgical correction, undergoing META+ DMMO 2-3-4, clinical metatarsalgia, age ≥ 18 years, and minimum 12-month radiographic and clinical follow-up. Exclusion criteria included prior forefoot surgery on the affected side, Charcot arthropathy, neuromuscular conditions, or inflammatory arthropathy (except stable rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis), inadequate follow-up (<12 months).

A total of 151 consecutive patients who underwent META+DMMO 2-3-4 during the study period were screened for eligibility. Thirty-one patients were excluded, including 24 with less than 12 months of follow-up and 7 with previous forefoot surgery on the affected side. The final study cohort consisted of 120 eligible patients.

Patients were stratified into 3 groups of 40 patients each, based on standardized radiographic criteria. Patients were enrolled consecutively based on eligibility criteria during the study period. Equal subgroup sizes reflect the final number of eligible patients available in each cohort and were not created through selective sampling.

Surgical Techniques

Minimally Invasive Transverse–Akin

All procedures were performed using a fourth-generation minimally invasive hallux valgus correction technique based on the third-generation MICA principles originally described by Redfern and Vernois and subsequently refined by Peter Lam and colleagues.14,18,19 The procedure involved a percutaneous straight (transverse) first-metatarsal osteotomy fixed with 2 bevelled screws (a 4.0-mm screw and either a 3.0- or 4.0-mm screw, according to surgeon preference).

A percutaneous Akin osteotomy was performed per surgeon’s decision intraoperatively.

Distal Minimally Invasive Metatarsal Osteotomies

DMMO was performed through small dorsal incisions without internal fixation, using a 45° oblique osteotomy to allow controlled decompression, shortening, and elevation of the overloaded lesser metatarsals while preserving MTP joint motion.14,18,19,25 The sequence of DMMO and META correction was based on the operating surgeon’s intraoperative judgment and was not standardized across all cases.

Postoperative Protocol

All patients were allowed immediate full weightbearing in a rigid postoperative shoe for 6 weeks, followed by gradual transition to athletic footwear and a structured physiotherapy program emphasizing range-of-motion restoration, gait training, and progressive return to activities. The postoperative dressing was routinely changed at 2 weeks by the operating surgeon, in accordance with commonly adopted MIFAS protocols.

Outcome Measures

Radiographic Assessment

Standardized weightbearing anteroposterior radiographs were obtained preoperatively and at 3 and 12 months after surgery. Two fellowship-trained foot and ankle surgeons independently measured HVA and IMA. In case of significant disagreement, a third blinded reviewer provided the final measurement. Radiographic recurrence was defined as HVA >20° at final follow-up. 22 To assess the reliability of radiographic measurements, inter- and intra-observer agreement was evaluated for HVA and IMA. Intra-observer reliability was assessed by repeat measurements performed by the same observers after a 30-day washout period. Reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a 2-way random effects model and absolute agreement. ICC values were interpreted according to standard thresholds, with values >0.75 considered good and >0.90 considered excellent reliability.

Clinical Outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes were collected using the PROMIS domains: physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI). Scores were recorded preoperatively and at 3 and 12 months. PROMIS questionnaires were administered electronically using validated local language versions, in accordance with PROMIS guidelines. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds were set as 4.5 to 6.0 points for physical function and pain interference.22,23 Complications such as nerve injury, infection, non-union, and hardware symptoms were documented.

Follow-up data collection were complete for all included patients. No missing data were present for PROMIS physical function, PROMIS pain interference or radiographic outcome measures at any study time point, and all 120 patients completed the scheduled preoperative, 3-month, and 12-month assessments.

Sample Size Calculation

The primary endpoint was radiographic recurrence at 12 months following combined META+DMMO correction. Prior studies of severe hallux valgus have reported recurrence rates ranging from 20% to as high as 60% to 75%,20-23 particularly in patients with advanced deformity. To allow meaningful comparison of recurrence across severity strata (mild, moderate, and severe), the study was designed to include 40 patients in each severity group. Assuming recurrence rates potentially approaching 70% in severe deformities and substantially lower rates in mild cases, a sample size of 40 patients per severity category provides adequate power (α = .05, 80% power) to detect clinically relevant differences in recurrence proportions between groups while ensuring stable estimation of recurrence risk within each severity level. This sample size also supports secondary functional outcome analyses without risk of underpowering.

Statistical Analysis

Patients were analysed by HV severity group (mild, moderate, severe) at baseline (T0), 3 months (T3), and 12 months (T12). Continuous variables included HVA, IMA, PROMIS scores, and visual analog scale pain scores. Normality of each outcome at each time point was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Although some variables demonstrated deviations from normality, results are presented as mean ± SD to facilitate consistent reporting and comparison across outcomes and follow-up intervals.

For normally distributed continuous variables, between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t tests. When normality assumptions were violated, nonparametric alternatives, including the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test, were used as appropriate. Within-group longitudinal changes over time were analysed using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests based on data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Absolute and percentage change-from-baseline values (Δ and %Δ) were calculated for all outcomes to account for baseline severity differences. Statistical significance was defined as P <.05. Analyses beyond the primary endpoint were considered exploratory, and secondary P values should therefore be interpreted in the context of effect size and clinical relevance. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). No loss to follow-up or missing patient-reported outcome data occurred.

Results

A total of 120 patients were included in the analysis, with 40 patients in each severity category (mild, moderate, and severe). Baseline demographic characteristics were comparable across groups. Mean patient age ranged from 50.0 to 52.8 years, with an overall mean of 51.2 ± 13.7 years, and no statistically significant difference between severity groups (P = .7039). Females constituted the majority of the cohort (80%-87.5% per group; overall 83.3%), with similar gender distribution across groups (P = .7488). Mean body mass index (BMI) ranged from 25.7 to 26.9 and did not differ significantly between groups (P = .1142). Active smoking prevalence was low overall (11.2%) and evenly distributed (P = .7419). Most patients had no documented comorbidities (88.3%), whereas diabetes mellitus type 2 (7.5%-10%) and hypertension (2.5%-5%) occurred infrequently and without significant intergroup differences (P = 1.0000; Table 2).

Surgical correction was associated with in substantial improvement in radiographic alignment. Postoperatively, IMA values converged across groups. At 3 months, mean IMA values were 8.4° (95% CI, 7.9°-8.9°), 8.2° (95% CI, 7.7°-8.7°), and 8.7° (95% CI, 8.1°-9.3°) for mild, moderate, and severe deformities, respectively (P = .053). Similar values were observed at 12 months (8.2°, 8.6°, and 8.6°, respectively; P = .075), suggesting stable correction across all severity groups (Table 3, Figure 1). Baseline HVA differed significantly across groups (19.1°, 35.0°, and 44.9°; P < .0001). At 3 months, substantial correction was observed, with mean HVA values of 10.3° (95% CI, 9.6°-11.0°), 12.7° (95% CI, 11.8°-13.6°), and 11.6° (95% CI, 10.7°-12.5°) in the mild, moderate, and severe groups, respectively (P < .0001). At 12 months, a mild increase in HVA was noted, but values remained markedly improved relative to baseline: 11.6° (95% CI, 10.8°-12.4°), 16.8° (95% CI, 15.6°-23.0°), and 16.9° (95% CI, 15.7°-24.1°) for the mild, moderate, and severe groups, respectively (P < .0001). The magnitude of correction increased with deformity severity. Mean ΔHVA at 12 months was −7.5° (95% CI, −8.4° to −6.6°) in the mild group, −18.2° (95% CI, −19.6° to −16.8°) in the moderate group, and −28.0° (95% CI, −29.8° to −26.2°) in the severe group (P < .0001; Table 3, Figure 2).

At 12 months, the vast majority of patients achieved improvements exceeding the established MCID threshold for both PROMIS-PF and PROMIS-PI. For PROMIS-PF, MCID achievement rates were 38/40 (95%) in the mild group, 36/40 (90%) in the moderate group, and 37/40 (92%) in the severe group. For PROMIS-PI, MCID achievement rates were 37/40 (92%), 37/40 (92%), and 37/40 (92%) in the mild, moderate, and severe groups, respectively. No significant differences were observed among severity groups with respect to the proportion of patients achieving clinically meaningful improvement (P = .32 for PROMIS-PF; P = .45 for PROMIS-PI).

Complication rates were low and similar across groups (P = .96). Most patients (87.5%) experienced no complications. Hardware removal occurred in 5% of patients. Non-union occurred in 4 lesser metatarsal osteotomies (1.1% of 360 DMMO osteotomies) and did not occur at the first metatarsal. All non-union were asymptomatic and required no revision surgery. Two superficial infections (1.6%) resolved with oral antibiotics, and 3 cases of transient neuropathy (2.5%) resolved spontaneously (Table 4).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified preoperative HVA as the only independent predictor of recurrence. Each additional degree of baseline HVA increased the odds of recurrence by 13% (OR 1.13; 95% CI, 1.07-1.21; P < .001). Other variables, including age, sex, BMI, baseline IMA, and PROMIS scores, were not significantly associated with recurrence. The model demonstrated moderate explanatory capacity (Tjur R2 = 0.173) and an overall accuracy of 79.2%, compared with a baseline accuracy of 75.8% (Figure 6).

Other demographic, radiographic, and patient-reported outcome measures were not significantly associated with recurrence risk.

Discussion

This multicenter cohort study demonstrates that combined META with DMMO provides substantial and clinically meaningful improvement in both pain and function for patients with hallux valgus across all levels of preoperative deformity severity. The primary outcome, radiographic recurrence at 12 months, differed significantly between severity groups (P < .0001), occurring in 0% of mild, 30% of moderate, and 42.5% of severe deformities. Mean improvements in PROMIS physical function and PROMIS pain interference exceeded established MCID thresholds by a considerable margin in every severity group. PROMIS physical function improved by 30.1 points in mild deformities, 33.0 points in moderate deformities, and 35.0 points in severe deformities, whereas PROMIS pain interference improved by 29.8, 30.8, and 29.6 points, respectively. These improvements greatly surpass the commonly reported MCID values of 4 to 8 points for lower-extremity musculoskeletal disorders and confirm the strong clinical effectiveness of this minimally invasive combined procedure.22,23 Collectively, these findings indicate that meaningful symptom relief and functional recovery were observed regardless of the initial severity of deformity. These results are consistent with our prior work,22,23 which reported similarly improvements in pain and functional outcomes following combined MICA/META and DMMO procedures despite relatively high rates of radiographic recurrence. Together, these studies highlight an increasingly recognized phenomenon in hallux valgus surgery: structural alignment and patient-reported outcomes do not always progress in parallel. Patients may experience marked functional improvement even when radiographic parameters demonstrate partial loss of correction over time. This disconnect between radiographic and clinical outcomes has been reported in both open and minimally invasive bunion surgery and underscores the importance of evaluating surgical success using patient outcome measures in addition to radiographic alignment.18,22-27 Although clinical outcomes were uniformly favourable across all severity groups, radiographic recurrence was clearly influenced by baseline deformity magnitude. Patients with severe hallux valgus demonstrated higher rates of recurrence compared with those with mild or moderate deformity. This observation is consistent with well-established biomechanical and pathological principles.26-28 Larger preoperative HVAs reflect not only greater deformity magnitude but also more advanced forefoot imbalance, including sesamoid displacement, soft tissue adaptation, and altered load distribution across the metatarsal heads. In such cases, even when substantial initial correction is achieved, maintaining long-term alignment may be inherently more challenging.27,28 This pattern was evident in the postoperative radiographic findings. At 12 months, mean HVA measured 11.6° in the mild group compared with 16.8° and 16.9° in the moderate and severe groups, respectively. Although all groups demonstrated marked improvement from baseline, residual deformity remained greater in patients with more severe preoperative alignment abnormalities. The magnitude of correction itself was substantial across all groups, with mean ΔHVA values ranging from −7.5° in mild deformities to −28.0° in severe deformities. These findings indicate that the surgical technique achieved effective initial correction even in advanced deformity, but long-term stability appeared to be more difficult to maintain in patients with larger baseline deformities. Interestingly, the severe deformity group demonstrated loss of HVA correction despite maintained IMA correction over time. Although the precise biomechanical explanation for this pattern cannot be definitively determined from the present data, similar findings have been previously reported following hallux valgus correction.22,23,29 This suggests that recurrence in severe deformities may not be driven solely by recurrent intermetatarsal widening, but also by factors such as persistent soft tissue imbalance, sesamoid malalignment, rotational instability, or residual hallux pronation. 29

Our multivariable logistic regression analysis further supports the central role of baseline deformity severity. Preoperative HVA emerged as the only independent predictor of recurrence, with each additional degree of deformity increasing the odds of recurrence by approximately 13%. This finding is consistent with prior studies of both open and minimally invasive hallux valgus correction, which have repeatedly identified preoperative HVA as a key determinant of postoperative stability.16,27,28 From a clinical perspective, this emphasizes the importance of careful preoperative counselling in patients with severe deformity. Although these patients can expect substantial improvement in pain and function, the likelihood of radiographic recurrence may remain higher than in patients with milder deformity.

An additional observation from this study is that recurrence appears to be influenced not only by deformity severity but also by the clinical scenario in which adjunctive DMMO is performed. Patients undergoing combined minimally invasive bunion correction and DMMO typically present with hallux valgus accompanied by symptomatic metatarsalgia and altered forefoot load distribution. In this setting, the coexistence of advanced hallux valgus deformity and lesser-ray overload may reflect more complex biomechanical pathology. Our findings suggest that patients with severe deformity who also require DMMO may represent a particularly high-risk subgroup for radiographic recurrence. The combined effects of significant deformity and altered forefoot mechanics may compromise the long-term maintenance of correction. Similar observations were reported in our prior studies that documented relatively high recurrence rates following combined minimally invasive bunion correction–DMMO procedures despite excellent clinical outcomes.22,23 Importantly, recurrence in the present study was defined radiographically as HVA greater than 20°, and therefore represents structural recurrence rather than symptomatic failure. This distinction is clinically relevant. Even among patients meeting radiographic recurrence criteria, patient-reported outcomes remained highly favourable, and functional improvement was maintained across all severity groups. Previous studies have similarly reported that patients may remain satisfied and functionally improved despite mild radiographic recurrence.22-24,26-28 The long-term clinical implications of such recurrence remain uncertain, but at 12 months postoperative functional outcomes were comparable regardless of alignment durability.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and residual confounding. Although patients were included consecutively and treated using standardized operative techniques and postoperative protocols at 2 high-volume centres, the decision to perform adjunctive DMMO was made preoperatively based on clinical findings rather than through random allocation. This reflects contemporary management of hallux valgus with concomitant metatarsalgia but limits the ability to fully isolate the independent contribution of DMMO to recurrence risk. To mitigate this limitation, patients were stratified according to standardized radiographic severity criteria and multivariable regression analysis was performed to adjust for baseline deformity magnitude. Second, follow-up was limited to 12 months. Although this duration captures the critical early postoperative stabilization phase and is consistent with many contemporary minimally invasive hallux valgus studies, it may underestimate late recurrence or delayed clinical deterioration. Longer-term follow-up will be necessary to determine whether early radiographic recurrence remains clinically tolerated or eventually leads to worsening symptoms or revision surgery. Third, this study did not include a contemporaneous control group treated with isolated META, which precludes direct comparison between isolated and combined procedures. Nevertheless, the study design allowed focused evaluation of recurrence patterns in a clinically relevant cohort presenting with both hallux valgus and metatarsalgia. Fourth, the degree of lateral translation achieved in the DMMO group was not quantitatively measured or standardized, limiting the ability to assess its potential impact on forefoot biomechanics and recurrence risk. Future studies should aim to quantify and calibrate the extent of lateral translation and develop a reproducible classification system to better define its role in surgical planning and outcomes. Finally, recurrence was defined using radiographic criteria, which may not fully capture patient-perceived outcomes. To address this limitation, validated PROMIS instruments were collected longitudinally, allowing parallel assessment of structural and functional outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study has several important strengths, including its multicentre design, standardized surgical technique, rigorous radiographic assessment, and comprehensive use of validated patient-reported outcome measures. By integrating radiographic stratification with multivariable analysis and functional outcomes, the present study provides meaningful insight into the complex relationship between deformity severity, adjunctive lesser-metatarsal osteotomy, and recurrence following minimally invasive hallux valgus correction.

Conclusion

Combined META osteotomy with DMMO was associated with substantial improvement in pain and function that consistently exceeded established MCID thresholds. However, radiographic recurrence remains strongly associated with greater preoperative deformity severity and may be further influenced by the need for adjunctive DMMO in patients with concomitant metatarsalgia. These findings highlight the importance of careful patient selection, thoughtful surgical planning, and individualized patient counselling. Future prospective multicentre studies with longer follow-up are needed to further clarify the interaction between deformity severity, adjunctive lesser-metatarsal procedures, and the long-term durability of correction.
