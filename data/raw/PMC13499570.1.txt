Background

Idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (IHH) is a rare endocrine disorder characterized by deficient gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion or action, resulting in delayed puberty and infertility. Its clinical distinction from functional hypothalamic amenorrhea can be challenging because of overlapping features. Pulsatile GnRH therapy offers a physiological approach to fertility induction in affected women; although pregnancies following this treatment have been reported previously, detailed longitudinal documentation from diagnosis through pregnancy and delivery in women with IHH remains valuable for clinical education.

Case Presentation

We present a 27-year-old nulligravid woman with clinical and biochemical features consistent with normosmic IHH who presented with primary amenorrhea and absent secondary sexual characteristics since adolescence. Evaluation revealed markedly suppressed gonadotropins (FSH 0.41 IU/L, LH 0.10 IU/L) with undetectable estradiol, normal pituitary magnetic resonance imaging, and a 46,XX karyotype. Normosmia distinguished this case from Kallmann syndrome. Following six months of hormone replacement therapy with regular withdrawal bleeding, the patient initiated pulsatile subcutaneous GnRH therapy (10 μg every 90 minutes) for fertility induction. Natural conception following pulsatile GnRH therapy occurred within two months of treatment initiation. The pregnancy progressed uneventfully with appropriate β-hCG doubling and normal ultrasonographic development. GnRH pump therapy was maintained until 12 weeks of gestation to support early pregnancy. The patient subsequently delivered a healthy term infant.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that pulsatile GnRH therapy can be an effective and physiological approach for achieving pregnancy in women with IHH. Although based on a single case, the favorable outcome achieved within a short treatment duration supports considering this modality as one of the treatment options for fertility induction in appropriately selected patients. In the absence of genetic confirmation and given the overlap with functional hypothalamic amenorrhea, the diagnosis is best regarded as consistent with, rather than definitive for, congenital IHH.

Introduction

Idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (IHH) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by absent or incomplete pubertal development due to deficient secretion or action of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH).1 The estimated prevalence ranges from 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 86,000 individuals, with a significant male predominance (male-to-female ratio approximately 4:1).2,3 In affected females, the clinical presentation typically includes primary amenorrhea, absent or incomplete breast development, and infertility.4

The differential diagnosis of IHH includes functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA) and Kallmann syndrome. Differentiation from Kallmann syndrome relies on olfactory function assessment: patients with normosmic IHH demonstrate intact olfaction, whereas those with Kallmann syndrome exhibit anosmia or hyposmia.5 Distinguishing congenital IHH from FHA is often more challenging, as both present with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and may share overlapping clinical features; this distinction can be particularly difficult in women with low body mass index who present with profound hypogonadotropism in the absence of obvious stress-, weight-, or exercise-related triggers. Both IHH and Kallmann syndrome share overlapping genetic etiologies, with mutations identified in numerous genes involved in GnRH neuronal migration, secretion, and action.6

Fertility induction in women with IHH presents unique challenges due to the complete absence of endogenous gonadotropin pulsatility. Therapeutic options include exogenous gonadotropin therapy (human menopausal gonadotropin or recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone combined with luteinizing hormone) and pulsatile GnRH administration.7,8 Pulsatile GnRH therapy offers a more physiological approach by restoring the native hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, thereby promoting monofollicular development and reducing the risks of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome and multiple gestations.9

Recent studies have demonstrated favorable pregnancy outcomes with pulsatile GnRH therapy in hypogonadotropic amenorrhea, with live birth rates exceeding 65% and predominantly monofollicular ovulation.10,11 Although successful pregnancies following pulsatile GnRH therapy have been reported previously, comprehensive documentation detailing the clinical trajectory from diagnosis through pregnancy and delivery in women with IHH remains limited. Herein, we report the longitudinal management of a young woman with features consistent with IHH who achieved natural conception following pulsatile GnRH therapy and delivered a healthy infant, with emphasis on the educational and clinical-management aspects of the case.

Case Presentation

Chief Complaint and History of Present Illness

A 27-year-old nulligravid woman presented to the Department of Gynecology at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital with primary amenorrhea and a desire for pregnancy. She had never experienced spontaneous menarche despite reaching adulthood. Her olfactory function was intact, ruling out Kallmann syndrome. Initial evaluation at age 22 years (April 2022) at an outside institution had established the diagnosis of hypothalamic amenorrhea. Sequential hormone replacement therapy (HRT) with estradiol valerate and progesterone had been administered, resulting in regular withdrawal bleeding.

The patient was referred to our center in November 2023 for further evaluation and fertility management. She had been married for three years and desired conception. Her husband’s semen analysis was within normal parameters.

Family and Personal History

Family history was notable for late menarche in her mother (age 19 years). The patient had two younger sisters who experienced menarche at ages 14 and 15 years, respectively. There was no family history of anosmia, cleft palate, hearing loss, or renal abnormalities. The patient denied any history of significant weight loss, eating disorders, excessive exercise, or psychological stress.

Physical Examination

On examination in July 2024, the patient’s height was 170.00 cm, weight 52.00 kg, and body mass index (BMI) 17.99 kg/m2. Vital signs were within normal limits. General appearance was notable for eunuchoid proportions. Breast development was Tanner stage IV following HRT. Pubic hair was Tanner stage III. Pelvic examination revealed a small uterus. Olfactory testing demonstrated normal function bilaterally.

Diagnostic Workup

Initial hormonal evaluation (April 2022) revealed profound hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with FSH 0.41 IU/L, LH 0.10 IU/L, estradiol 53.43 pmol/L, progesterone 1.17 nmol/L, testosterone 1.83 nmol/L, and prolactin 264.20 μIU/mL (Table 1). Karyotype analysis confirmed 46,XX. Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium contrast demonstrated normal pituitary morphology and enhancement; however, brain MRI revealed cavum septum pellucidum. At our center (November 2023), repeat hormonal assessment demonstrated persistent hypogonadotropism: FSH 1.69 IU/L, LH 1.31 IU/L, estradiol <55.00 pmol/L, progesterone 4.14 nmol/L, testosterone 2.52 nmol/L, and prolactin 170.42 μIU/mL. Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) was 4.72 ng/mL, suggesting preserved ovarian reserve. A GnRH stimulation test was performed to assess pituitary gonadotroph function. Following administration of 100 μg GnRH intravenously, serial measurements demonstrated LH response from 1.31 to 9.20 to 9.94 to 9.28 to 7.74 IU/L at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes, respectively. FSH increased from 1.69 to 7.05 to 8.34 to 7.96 to 9.27 IU/L over the same time points. The robust response to exogenous GnRH confirmed intact pituitary function and localized the defect to the hypothalamic level, consistent with IHH.

Management

The patient received cyclic hormone replacement therapy with Femoston (estradiol/dydrogesterone) for six months, which induced regular withdrawal bleeding and uterine development. Pelvic ultrasonography following HRT demonstrated improved uterine dimensions and endometrial thickness.

Given her desire for pregnancy, pulsatile GnRH therapy was initiated on July 4, 2024. A portable subcutaneous infusion pump was programmed to deliver 10 μg of GnRH (gonadorelin) every 90 minutes continuously. Pre-treatment pelvic ultrasonography confirmed uterine development following HRT. AMH remained stable at 4.78 ng/mL, confirming preserved ovarian reserve (Table 1). The patient was instructed regarding pump maintenance, injection site rotation, and recognition of potential complications.

Ovulation monitoring was performed using serial transvaginal ultrasonography and serum hormone measurements. Within one month of therapy initiation, regular menstrual cycles resumed. Follicular tracking on cycle day 12 (August 19, 2024) demonstrated a developing follicle measuring 13×10 mm in the left ovary. By cycle day 15 (August 22, 2024), a dominant follicle of 20×16 mm was visualized with an endometrial thickness of 8 mm, confirming ovulation induction. On September 13, 2024 (5 weeks and 3 days gestational age), serum β-hCG was 512.60 IU/L with progesterone 135.00 nmol/L and estradiol 1172.00 pmol/L at an outside institution. These values were confirmed at our center the following day (September 14, 2024): β-hCG 3059.52 IU/L and progesterone 102.90 nmol/L. Discussion

This case illustrates the successful application of pulsatile GnRH therapy for fertility induction in a woman with features consistent with normosmic IHH, resulting in natural conception within two months of treatment initiation and subsequent delivery of a healthy infant. The favorable outcome achieved with this physiological approach underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis and appropriate therapeutic selection in managing reproductive dysfunction associated with hypothalamic-pituitary axis disorders.

The diagnosis of IHH requires systematic exclusion of other causes of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, including functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA), pituitary lesions, hyperprolactinemia, and chronic systemic illness.12,13 Distinguishing congenital IHH from FHA was particularly relevant in this patient, who had a relatively low body mass index (BMI 17.99 kg/m2) and had initially been diagnosed with hypothalamic amenorrhea at an outside institution. However, the absence of identifiable precipitating factors for FHA (weight loss, excessive exercise, or psychological stress), the presence of primary amenorrhea with absent spontaneous pubertal development, normal pituitary imaging, and normal prolactin levels favored a congenital rather than a functional etiology. The preserved response to exogenous GnRH stimulation confirmed pituitary gonadotroph competence and localized the defect to the hypothalamic level, while normal olfactory function differentiated this case from Kallmann syndrome.14 Nevertheless, in the absence of genetic testing, the distinction between congenital IHH and FHA cannot be established with complete certainty, and the diagnosis is most appropriately regarded as consistent with normosmic IHH.

The choice between pulsatile GnRH therapy and exogenous gonadotropin therapy for fertility induction in IHH remains a subject of clinical discussion. Recent comparative studies have demonstrated that pulsatile GnRH therapy offers several advantages.15 Everaere et al compared outcomes between patients with FHA and congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism treated with pulsatile GnRH, reporting comparable ongoing pregnancy rates (21.5% per initiated cycle in FHA versus 22.0% in CHH).11 Quaas et al demonstrated a cumulative live birth rate of 65.9% per treatment with pulsatile GnRH in FHA, with 75% of cycles resulting in monofollicular ovulation.10

Compared with exogenous gonadotropin therapy, pulsatile GnRH administration restores the native hypothalamic signal that drives gonadotropin secretion. By mimicking the endogenous GnRH pulse frequency and amplitude, it allows the pituitary-ovarian axis to regulate follicular development through intact feedback mechanisms, resulting in predominantly monofollicular ovulation with lower risks of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome and multiple pregnancy.16,17 These features represent the principal practical advantages of pulsatile GnRH therapy over gonadotropin-based ovulation induction in this setting.

The optimal GnRH pulse parameters vary among protocols, with frequencies typically ranging from 60 to 120 minutes and doses from 5 to 20 μg per pulse.18 Our patient received 10 μg every 90 minutes, a regimen that successfully induced ovulation and conception. Because women with IHH lack adequate endogenous GnRH-driven luteinizing hormone support, corpus luteum function may be insufficient to sustain early pregnancy until the luteal-placental shift is complete at approximately 8 to 12 weeks of gestation; for this reason, pulsatile GnRH therapy was continued until 12 weeks in our patient.19 It should be acknowledged, however, that the optimal duration of GnRH support in early pregnancy is not universally standardized, and reported practices range from discontinuation after conception with luteal-phase progesterone supplementation to continuation throughout the first trimester.

This case also highlights the importance of comprehensive evaluation, including assessment of ovarian reserve. Our patient demonstrated preserved AMH levels (4.72–4.78 ng/mL), suggesting an adequate follicular pool despite chronic hypogonadism. Although it is tempting to attribute the rapid treatment response to this preserved ovarian reserve, such a causal relationship cannot be established from a single case and should be interpreted with caution.20

An additional clinically noteworthy observation is that this patient achieved relatively advanced secondary sexual development, with Tanner stage IV breast development, following prolonged hormone replacement therapy despite profound hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. This illustrates the responsiveness of end-organ tissues to appropriately administered exogenous sex steroids even after delayed and incomplete spontaneous pubertal development.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This is a single case report, and individual outcomes may vary according to patient characteristics, ovarian reserve, and treatment protocols. Importantly, genetic testing was not performed; consequently, specific mutations underlying the hypogonadotropic hypogonadism could not be identified. The absence of genetic evaluation limits diagnostic certainty, precludes definitive differentiation of congenital IHH from other causes such as FHA, and prevents genotype-phenotype correlation that may carry prognostic and reproductive implications, including the potential for reversibility and heritability.6 These considerations reinforce the more cautious diagnostic terminology adopted throughout this report.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that pulsatile GnRH therapy can be an effective and physiologically appropriate approach for fertility induction in women with IHH. Although the successful pregnancy outcome was achieved within a short treatment duration, these observations derive from a single case and should not be broadly generalized. Pulsatile GnRH therapy may nonetheless be considered a physiological option for fertility induction in appropriately selected patients, and further studies are warranted to define its role relative to alternative approaches. Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including olfactory assessment for differentiation from Kallmann syndrome, careful distinction from functional hypothalamic amenorrhea, pituitary function testing, and ovarian reserve assessment, is essential for optimal management planning. Continuation of GnRH therapy through early pregnancy may help support the luteal-placental transition in this patient population, although its optimal duration remains to be defined.
