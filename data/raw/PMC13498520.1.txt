Background

Pharmacovigilance is the science and practice of safety monitoring for medicines-related problems; it is also applicable to a broader scope of health products, including natural health products (NHPs). The NHPs industry is a key stakeholder in pharmacovigilance for NHPs, but academic review of regulatory requirements concerning pharmacovigilance for the NHPs industry is limited.

Objectives

To map, describe and compare regulatory requirements for the NHPs industry regarding pharmacovigilance for NHPs in selected international jurisdictions.

Methods

The top 30 countries/regions from the World Health Organization’s (WHO) list of nations with the highest Current Health Expenditure in 2020 were identified. For these countries/regions, publicly accessible regulatory instruments describing pharmacovigilance requirements for the NHPs industry, available in English on official governmental websites and selected secondary resources, were sought.

Results

Regulatory documents from 12 countries/regions (Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, India, Japan, Republic of Korea, South Africa, Switzerland, UK, USA and the EU) were included. Definitions of NHPs vary across jurisdictions, while regulatory frameworks most clearly diverge between single medicine-based systems that include NHPs, and those that apply separate medicine- and food-based primary legislation to different NHP categories. Most included jurisdictions require the NHPs industry (i.e., product sponsors, market authorisation holders, registration/license holders, manufacturers) to report serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs) within 15 days of becoming aware of such reports. However, requirements for submitting foreign ADR reports, periodic safety update reports, conducting literature screening, and employing a Qualified Person for Pharmacovigilance differ. In jurisdictions that apply separate medicine- and food-based primary legislation to different NHP categories, pharmacovigilance requirements for the industry correspondingly vary. There are usually fewer (or no) requirements for products regulated as food compared with those included under medicine-based regulations.

Conclusions

Pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements for the NHPs industry vary between and within countries/regions, primarily focusing on reporting serious ADRs. To support effective benefit–risk assessment of NHPs and safeguard public health, future policy-oriented research should assess the impact of these regulatory differences on industry practice and for pharmacovigilance for NHPs, examine requirements in countries not included here, and explore pathways towards international regulatory harmonisation.

Plain Language Summary

Pharmacovigilance is the science and practice of safety monitoring for medicines-related problems; it is also applicable to a broader scope of health products, including natural health products (NHPs). The NHPs industry (such as manufacturers, sponsors, market authorisation holders, registration/license holders) is a key stakeholder in pharmacovigilance for NHPs. This review discusses pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements for the NHPs industry in 11 countries (Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, India, Japan, Republic of Korea, South Africa, Switzerland, UK, USA) and the European Union (EU). Pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements vary between and within countries/regions. In countries/regions with regulations for NHPs, there are some requirements for the NHPs industry to report to the regulatory authority serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs) associated with NHPs, or some specific types of NHPs. Typically, serious ADRs must be reported by the NHPs industry within 15 days of becoming aware of the event. Also, within some countries/regions, the NHPs industry has fewer (or no) ADR reporting requirements for NHPs regulated as food than for those regulated as medicines. To support effective benefit–risk assessment of NHPs and safeguard public health, future policy work should examine how differences in regulations affect the industry and safety monitoring of these products, review regulations in countries not covered in this study, and explore strategies towards aligning regulations internationally.

Key Points

Definitions/classifications of natural health products (NHPs) and pharmacovigilance requirements imposed on the industry vary between and within jurisdictions internationally.	
A common regulatory pharmacovigilance requirement is for industry reporting of serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs) within 15 days, while other obligations (e.g., submitting periodic safety update reports, having a Qualified Person for Pharmacovigilance) differ across jurisdictions.	
The impact of differences in definitions/classifications for NHPs and in regulatory requirements on industry practices and, ultimately, on pharmacovigilance for NHPs is unclear and warrants further study.	

Background

The term ‘natural health products’ (NHPs) generally refers to products made from natural ingredients, such as plants, vitamins, minerals and non-human animal products, and intended for health-related use. Various terms are used across jurisdictions when referring to NHPs or subcategories thereof [1, 2]. For example, NHPs are referred to as complementary medicines in Australia and dietary supplements in the United States of America (USA) [3]. NHPs can encompass several subcategories, including herbal products, homeopathic remedies, traditional medicines, food/health supplements, nutraceuticals and others [4]. For the purposes of this review, the term ‘NHPs’ will be used when referring to these products collectively.

Despite their reputation as ‘low-risk’ products, and regardless of their legal status, NHPs can still pose potential harm through their use. Harms arising from use of NHPs can be due to: (1) intrinsic toxicities of constituents of the ingredient itself, e.g., nephropathy following exposure to aristolochic acid(s) in some Aristolochia species [5]; (2) interactions between constituents of NHPs and ‘conventional medicines’ and other NHPs, e.g., ephedra and other substances with sympathomimetic effects [6] and/or (3) extrinsic factors, such as contamination or adulteration of NHPs. Studies exploring the quality of marketed NHPs have raised concerns regarding product contents, lack of botanical authentication and heavy metal contamination, amongst other issues [7–9]. Some safety issues have resulted in serious harms to users of NHPs. In 2024, certain red yeast rice food supplements manufactured by a company in Japan were implicated in cases of renal damage leading to hospitalisations and deaths [10].

To identify signals of safety concerns associated with the use of NHPs, it is essential that pharmacovigilance activities are undertaken. The scope of pharmacovigilance extends beyond ‘drugs (medicines)’ to detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing adverse effects of a broader range of health products, including NHPs [11]. Other terms that are used to refer to pharmacovigilance for NHPs include ‘nutri/nutravigilance,’ and (for herbal products) ‘phytovigilance’ [12–14]. At present, spontaneous reporting of suspected adverse drug reactions (ADRs) remains the cornerstone of pharmacovigilance for NHPs. However, while many countries operate a national pharmacovigilance system for collecting and analysing reports of suspected ADRs, not all actively encourage reporting of suspected ADRs associated with NHPs and, in some jurisdictions, reports for these products are not accepted [11, 15]. Recently, the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) has published an updated definition of the term ‘ADR’ as a reaction that, ‘as defined by regional and local requirements, concerns a noxious and unintended response to a medicinal product,’ whereby ‘response to a medicinal product means that a causal relationship between a medicinal product and an adverse event (AE) is at least a reasonable possibility’ [16]. While standardised definitions for key pharmacovigilance terms are available, this review retains the terminology used within individual regulatory jurisdictions where appropriate, particularly when describing or citing country-specific regulations.

Pharmacovigilance systems for NHPs involve key stakeholders, including consumers of NHPs, traditional and complementary medicine (T&CM) practitioners, regulatory authorities for healthcare products, national pharmacovigilance centres, NHP retailers, health professionals and the NHPs industry [17]. For the purposes of this review, the term ‘NHPs industry’ is used when collectively referring to sponsors, market authorisation holders (MAHs), registration holders, licensees, manufacturers, etc., of NHPs. As a potential point of contact for consumers, and as the entity that oversees the production and distribution of its products, the NHPs industry plays a key role in pharmacovigilance for NHPs [17]. However, there remains limited information on consumer reporting of suspected ADRs associated with NHPs directly to the industry and the subsequent management of such reports [18]. Regulatory obligation is likely a primary driver for the NHPs industry’s engagement in pharmacovigilance [18, 19]. In the USA, the introduction of mandatory reporting requirements for serious AEs by manufacturers of dietary supplements, when such events are made known to them by others (e.g., consumers, health professionals), was followed by a marked increase in the number of (mandatory) AE reports submitted to the Centre for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition’s Adverse Event Reporting System (CAERS; now known as the Human Foods Complaints (HFC) System) [20]. A similar upward trend was observed in Germany for homeopathic solutions for injection [21]. Other motivators beyond meeting regulatory requirements have not been well explored [18]. For the purposes of this review, the terms ‘regulations’ and ‘regulatory requirements’ are used broadly to collectively refer to legislation, secondary regulations, and directives, and their associated provisions and requirements.

The World Health Organization (WHO) Global Report on Traditional and Complementary Medicine 2019 reported that 84 WHO member states had market surveillance systems for monitoring the safety of herbal medicines [15]. Nevertheless, based on the available information, gathered via surveys in 2005 and 2012, it is unclear whether these pharmacovigilance systems place any obligations on the NHPs industry to collect and report (to a regulatory authority) suspected ADRs associated with their products [15]. A more recent review, published by WHO in 2018, combined information gathered from a literature review and a survey among 10 South East Asian countries. Of the eight countries that indicated they had a clear/designated pharmacovigilance system/centre in place, four systems (Bhutan, India, Indonesia, Thailand) included T&CM products [11]. This review also reported that five countries (Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Thailand) have legal provisions requiring Marketing Authorized Holders to monitor adverse effects, though details of these requirements were not provided [11].

Several published reviews have compared regulatory frameworks for NHPs, or subcategories thereof [1, 2, 4], or pharmacovigilance regulations in general, in selected countries [22, 23]. However, none has focused on the regulatory requirements for the NHPs industry specifically regarding pharmacovigilance for NHPs. Owing to the potentially significant impact that regulations would have on the contributions of the NHPs industry towards pharmacovigilance, this review aimed to explore current regulatory requirements regarding pharmacovigilance for the NHPs industry, as outlined in the regulations of selected high health expenditure countries.

Methods

To address the research question, this review focused on grey literature, i.e., regulatory instruments. This was a scoping review undertaken following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) manual of evidence synthesis [24], and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [25] checklist where feasible.

Research Question

What are the similarities and differences in regulatory requirements for pharmacovigilance of NHPs imposed on the NHPs industry across selected high health expenditure countries?

This review focuses on countries with high health expenditure, which have made significant investments in healthcare and related services. Prior to commencing the review, these jurisdictions were expected to be more likely to have well-established pharmacovigilance practices for conventional pharmaceutical medicines and more advanced or, at least, evolving approaches to pharmacovigilance for NHPs, as well as accessible regulations in the English language. Focusing on these settings was considered an appropriate and feasible starting point to enable meaningful comparisons, seek insights, and identify reference points for strengthening pharmacovigilance for NHPs.

Of the 30 countries (11 were from the European Union (EU)) screened, 12 jurisdictions were identified as having general and/or specific regulations covering NHPs, or a subcategory thereof to some extent, and met the inclusion criteria. The final list of jurisdictions comprised Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, India, Japan, the Republic of Korea, South Africa, Switzerland, the United Kingdom (UK), USA and EU. NHPs Regulatory Frameworks

Across the jurisdictions included, there are three major types of NHPs regulatory frameworks, which are outlined in Fig. 2.Fig. 2 Regulatory frameworks for NHPs with an example country or region and its corresponding regulations for NHPs [NHP, natural health product]

Type 1 frameworks include general primary legislation that mentions NHPs as a subcategory of medicines, i.e., Acts that encompass both conventional therapeutic products and NHPs without having standalone secondary regulations for NHPs, such as in Australia [29–31] and South Africa [32, 33]. Australia and South Africa regulations define their NHPs as a single (broad) category of medicines—complementary medicines.

Type 2 frameworks have a general Act (primary legislation) that encompasses all therapeutic products with related legislation/regulations that are specific to NHPs, such as in the USA and Canada. For instance, the USA regulates dietary supplements under the (1) Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act 1938 [34] (2) Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act 1994 [35] and (3) Dietary Supplement and Nonprescription Drug Consumer Protection Act 2006 [36]. Both the USA and Canada regulate NHPs as a single category: dietary supplements in the USA [34] and natural health products in Canada [37].

Type 3 regulatory frameworks have specific primary legislation (and subsequent secondary regulations) for different categories of NHPs. Typically, in such jurisdictions, there are (1) certain categories of NHPs that are governed by a general (primary) medicine/therapeutic product legislation, which either includes or has separate secondary regulations for NHPs, while (2) other categories of NHPs are governed by distinct food-related regulations. This is the case in the UK [38–44], Republic of Korea [45–47], India [48–50], Japan [51–53], Switzerland [54–57] and EU [39, 58, 59]. In these jurisdictions, NHPs are regulated as ‘therapeutic product-type’ NHPs or ‘food-type’ NHPs under distinct primary legislation. For example, in the EU and UK, ‘therapeutic product-type’ NHPs include herbal medicinal products and homeopathic products, while ‘food-type’ NHPs are regulated as food supplements and novel food. In some instances, distinct ministries and regulatory agencies oversee regulations of ‘therapeutic product-type’ NHPs and ‘food-type’ NHPs. Within the EU, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) produces scientific evidence to advise the European Commission (EC) and member states regarding the safety of ‘food-type’ NHPs, while the Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC) of the European Medicines Agency (EMA) evaluates the scientific evidence (including safety and post-marketing information) for ‘therapeutic-type’ NHPs. This is similar for Japan and Switzerland. India also has separate regulators for ‘therapeutic product-type’ NHPs and ‘food-type’ NHPs, though traditional medicine knowledge-based ‘therapeutic product-type’ NHPs (not including phytopharmaceutical drugs) are under the purview of the Ministry of AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homoeopathy), separate from the body responsible for regulating conventional pharmaceuticals. Meanwhile, in the Republic of Korea, though regulated by different primary legislation, both ‘therapeutic-type’ and ‘food-type’ products are under the purview of Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (see online resource: Supplementary Table S3).

Terms Used in Regulatory Instruments

Several terms were used to refer to NHPs, the NHPs industry, and suspected ADRs involving NHPs across and within countries/regions (see online resource: Supplementary Tables S3 and S4).

Definition of NHPs

Various terms and definitions are used when referring to NHPs in regulatory instruments across jurisdictions, for example, natural health products in Canada [37], complementary medicines in Australia [31] and Switzerland [60] and dietary supplements in the USA [34]. Some countries/regions refer to subcategories of NHPs: for example, herbal medicinal products and food supplements in the UK and EU [39, 58, 59]. Some countries have specific NHP categories according to their local traditional medicine systems, e.g., Ayurvedic, Siddha or Unani drug and homeopathic medicines in India [48–50], Traditional Chinese Medicines (TCM) in China (further divided into Chinese Materia Medica (CMM)/prepared slices of Chinese crude drugs (PSCCDs), herbal oils and extracts, Chinese patent medicines (CPMs), simple preparations PSCCDs) [61] and Kampo medicines in Japan [51]. Supplementary Table S4 presents the definitions for NHPs and their subcategories used across the included countries/regions.

There is variation across jurisdictions in what is considered to be ‘natural’ sources or ingredients. For example, some countries, such as Canada [37], the Republic of Korea [62] and Australia [31], consider synthetic duplicates of substances originating from natural sources to be NHPs. In the USA, products containing a metabolite of a natural substance (e.g., vitamin, mineral, herb, amino acid or other dietary substance), and isolated constituents of dietary ingredients, are also permitted as ingredients of dietary supplements [34]. In this instance, no references are made to the processing methods to obtain such metabolites within the Act [34]. Some countries, such as Brazil, explicitly exclude isolated or highly purified active substances regardless of whether they were produced synthetically, semi-synthetically or isolated from natural raw materials [63]. In the UK [43] and EU [39], novel food products refers to food consisting of, isolated from or produced from: microorganisms, fungi or algae; material of mineral origin; plants or their parts; animals or their parts; cell culture or tissue culture derived from animals, plants, microorganisms, fungi or algae; or food with a new or intentionally modified molecular structure.

References to the NHPs Industry

Several terms are used across regulatory instruments when referring to the NHPs industry, including sponsor, manufacturer, MAH, product license/registration/certificate holder/applicant and responsible person. Regarding food supplements, functional foods, novel foods, foods with health/functional claims (e.g., in the UK, Republic of Korea, India, Japan and EU), the terms food business operators and business person are used (see online resource: Supplementary Table S3).

Pharmacovigilance Terms

Several terms are used when referring to suspected ADRs associated with NHPs in the pharmacovigilance-related regulatory provisions (see online resource: Supplementary Table S3). The terms adverse reaction (AR) (5 countries) and adverse event (AE) (4 countries) are most used. The term AR is used explicitly in the regulations of four countries/regions, i.e., Australia, Canada, UK, EU, while the term AE is used by USA regulations. Switzerland referred to both AE and AR in its regulatory instruments, while South Africa and India used AE, AR and/or ADR. In India, the reporting of product complaints may also be relevant in addition to AEs and ARs. In the Republic of Korea, the terms side effect, adverse experience, ADR and AE are used.

Pharmacovigilance Regulatory Requirements for Marketed NHPs

Of the 12 jurisdictions reviewed (see online resource: Supplementary Table S3), the same pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements apply to all medicinal products, including NHPs [N.B. only for those NHPs that are regulated as medicines or a subcategory thereof], in nine countries/regions (Australia, Brazil, India, Japan, Republic of Korea, South Africa, Switzerland, UK and EU); while two countries (Canada and USA) apply specific pharmacovigilance regulations for NHPs. In jurisdictions with separate categories of ‘food-type’ NHPs (e.g., India, Republic of Korea, Japan, Switzerland, UK, EU), different requirements apply to these NHPs when compared with ‘therapeutic-type’ NHPs (see online resource: Supplementary Table S3). [N.B. South Africa’s regulations for complementary medicines have been confirmed by the South African Supreme Court of Appeal to be lawful to the extent that they would apply to products/substances that are medicines or scheduled substances as defined in the Medicines and Related Substances Act, 1965 (Act 101 of 1965). The regulations must be read with this interpretation pending their amendment. A roadmap and transitional process for regulating complementary medicines, as outlined in Guideline 7.02, is being revised. As a result, products regulated as complementary medicines in South Africa are in the process of being registered and facilities associated with their supply licensed, but the process is not yet complete. These products are intended to be governed by regulations as registered medicines [64–67].] This review was unable to access and assess further information from China and Brazil due to language restrictions.

Serious ADRs/AEs

Six countries/regions (Australia [68], Canada [69], Republic of Korea [45, 46], South Africa [64, 65], UK [70], EU [71]) require the NHPs industry to report all serious AEs/ADRs (expected and unexpected) relating to NHP use (or a subcategory thereof) within 15 days of becoming aware of the information. Two countries (USA and India) require sponsors to report serious AEs associated with the use of NHPs within 15 days of knowledge [72, 73]. Japan requires “holders of marketing authorization” to submit reports of “death-occurrences suspected to be caused by the side effects of pharmaceuticals” [N.B which include some NHPs e.g., licensed herbal medicines] and unexpected “serious cases suspected to be caused by some side effects of the pharmaceuticals” within 15 days, while reports of other serious ADRs should be submitted within 30 days [51, 74].

Non-serious ADRs/AEs

In addition to submitting reports of serious AEs, India also requires MAHs of phytopharmaceutical drugs to report non-serious AEs within 90 days of receipt of information from any source [73]. Similarly, UK and EU regulations also require MAHs/registration holders of herbal medicinal products to submit reports of non-serious ADRs within 90 days [70, 71]. Switzerland requires “any person manufacturing or distributing ready-to-use therapeutic products” [N.B which can include complementary and herbal medicines] to notify regulators of “any AE or reaction which (…) may endanger or damage the health of the consumer, of the patient (…);”, the reporting timeline is not explicitly mentioned in the included regulatory instruments used for this review that are accessible in English [54, 60].

Other Reports

Beyond ‘routine’ reporting, four counties/regions require notification of significant or emerging safety issues meeting definitions as set out in regulations to be reported within shorter timeframes. Australia [68] requires sponsors to report significant safety issues (SSIs) within 72 h, the UK and EU require MAHs to report emerging safety issues (ESIs) within 3 working days [75, 76], and South Africa [65] requires holders/applicants of certificates of registration to report SSIs in five calendar days. SSIs and ESIs in the relevant regulatory instruments refer to similar definitions, i.e., major safety issues, which can arise from spontaneous reports, published literature, active or recently completed studies and other sources, that require the urgent attention of a competent authority [68, 75]. This requirement applies to all therapeutic products, including conventional medicines and NHPs.

Reporting of ADRs/AEs that Occurred Outside a Country/Region

Five countries/regions (UK, EU, Canada, Republic of Korea, Japan) explicitly mentioned reporting of ADRs associated with NHPs distributed or marketed by manufacturers/sponsors/MAHs that occurred in another country/region. The UK [70], EU [71] and the Republic of Korea [45, 46] impose the same requirements on reporting timelines for serious ADRs related to NHPs within and outside their country/region. Likewise, Japan’s reporting requirements and timeline for foreign pharmaceuticals [N.B. which could include licensed herbal medicines] with expected and unexpected “death occurrence suspected to be caused by the side effects” is the same as for local cases [74]. In contrast, Canada requires case reports of only serious unexpected ADRs outside Canada to be submitted within 15 calendar days, while requiring reports of both expected and unexpected serious ADRs that occurred within Canada to be submitted within 15 calendar days [69]. In South Africa, though routine reporting of ADRs (serious or not) that occurred outside the country is not required, reporting an individual case report of an ADR in the case of an SSI that has been acted upon by a third country’s regulator still needs to be done within five calendar days [65].

Three countries (USA, Australia, India) explicitly outline requirements to report cases of serious AEs/ARs/ADRs that occurred within the country only. That said, in Australia, “serious AR reports from overseas” are to be included as part of the periodic safety update report (PSUR), where one is required, or when specifically requested by the regulator [68].

In addition to fulfilling ADR reporting requirements, MAHs in the UK and EU must maintain detailed records of all reports of suspected ADRs received, including non-serious ADRs that occurred within and outside the country/region. MAHs are also expected to regularly screen or monitor (i.e., at a frequency specified by competent authorities or at least every 2 weeks) medical and scientific literature databases for ADR cases involving their products, including published full case reports, draft manuscripts and abstracts from meetings [70, 77]. India recommends that MAHs (of phytopharmaceuticals only) screen published electronic literature on a weekly basis [73]. Australian sponsors of medicines also must report serious ARs that occurred within Australia that are identified through worldwide literature screening, though frequency and types of literature screening are not explicitly mentioned [68].

Periodic Safety Update Reports (PSURs)

PSUR submissions are required or conditionally required in the UK, EU, Republic of Korea, India, Australia, South Africa. PSURs requirements and exemptions for NHPs are explicitly mentioned in the UK [78], EU [71], Republic of Korea [46] and India’s regulatory instruments (primary legislation and/or secondary regulations/directives) [79], while it is mentioned in relevant guidance documents for Australia [68] and South Africa [65]. The requirements for submitting PSURs are not universal to all NHPs within jurisdictions. In India, PSUR submissions are required for phytopharmaceutical drugs but not for other NHP categories [79–81]. In the UK and EU, PSUR requirements are exempted for well-established use, homeopathic, and traditional herbal medicinal products unless specifically requested by the competent authority [82, 83]. Similarly, in Australia and South Africa, PSURs are to be submitted upon request by the relevant authorities or as laid out as a specific condition for a particular product [65, 68]. Adopting the EU Good Pharmacovigilance Practices (GVP) guidance document, a typical submission timeline for PSURs is every 6 months for the first 2 years, then annually, across jurisdictions. However, PSURs submission frequency may still vary depending on product approval dates and authorisation conditions, and can be “defined on the basis of risk-based approaches” [46, 65, 68, 71, 78, 79, 83].

‘Therapeutic Product-Type’ NHPs Versus ‘Food-Type’ NHPs

There is variation in the applicability of pharmacovigilance regulations across different subcategories of NHPs. Generally, ‘food-type’ NHPs are not regulated with the same pharmacovigilance requirements as are ‘therapeutic-product-type’ NHPs. In most jurisdictions, requirements to report (as well as to a set timeline) ADRs by the NHPs industry to regulatory authorities are not explicitly stated in regulations for ‘food-type’ NHPs. For example, the food supplement industry in the EU is not subject to the same regulatory requirements for pharmacovigilance as are MAHs/registration holders for herbal medicinal and homeopathic products, even where they contain the same substance. In the EU, food supplements are regulated as food, and the safety of food supplements is the responsibility of the seller/importer, where food business operators shall immediately inform the relevant authorities in cases where they believe that their marketed food product may be harmful to human health [84]. There are similar requirements in India [85] and the Republic of Korea [86].

There are also within-category (i.e., within ‘therapeutic-product-type’ NHPs and ‘food-type’ NHPs) variations in pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements. For instance, in the EU, registration holders of homeopathic products (a subcategory of ‘therapeutic-type’ NHPs) registered under the simplified registration procedure are exempt from pharmacovigilance requirements [87, 88], while for novel food products, a subcategory of NHPs regulated as food, the authorities may impose requirements for post-marketing monitoring on a case-by-case basis. The requirement is similar in the UK [43, 89, 90].

Personnel for Pharmacovigilance

Three countries/regions, the UK [91], EU [71] and South Africa [65, 92], require MAHs and “applicants/holders of certificates of registration to have permanently and continuously at its disposal a suitably qualified person for pharmacovigilance (QPPV). ” In the Republic of Korea [93], the term safety control manager is used instead [93]. The qualified person is responsible for operating and maintaining a pharmacovigilance system for the registered products, including NHPs (or ‘therapeutic-product-type’ NHPs, where applicable). Similarly, in India, a Pharmacovigilance Officer-in-Charge should be authorised by MAHs of phytopharmaceuticals to handle pharmacovigilance activities [73].

In the EU, in addition to the QPPV, a contact person at the national (or local) level who reports to the QPPV may be nominated by the MAHs [71]. Australia also requires a contact person for pharmacovigilance [68].

Regulatory Requirements for Pharmacovigilance in Clinical Trials of NHPs

Regulatory instruments from 10 included countries/regions (Australia, Canada, India, Japan, Republic of Korea, South Africa, Switzerland, UK, USA, EU) reference sponsor responsibilities in reporting ADRs or AEs that occur during clinical trials [94–106]. The same general regulatory requirements for AE and ADR reporting in clinical trials apply to all therapeutic products, including NHPs, in Australia, India, Japan, Republic of Korea, Switzerland, South Africa, UK, USA and EU. In Canada, there are specific regulations for NHPs (i.e., the Natural Health Products Regulations 2009) [95], which mirror those for conventional pharmaceutical medicines [107].

Reporting timelines were specified for serious suspected ADRs (fatal/life-threatening and non-fatal/non-life threatening) for Australia, Canada, South Africa, Switzerland, UK, USA and EU. Discussion

This scoping review focused specifically on regulatory requirements for the NHPs industry regarding pharmacovigilance for NHPs. The key findings are that regulations for NHPs, and subcategories of NHPs, vary between and within countries and regions and, as a result, pharmacovigilance requirements for NHPs also differ within and across jurisdictions.

Variations in Pharmacovigilance Regulatory Requirements, Possible Impact, Future Studies

This review identified that reporting of serious ADRs related to the use of NHPs is a common pharmacovigilance regulatory requirement for the NHPs industry among the countries/regions studied (which have regulations for NHPs). While the reasons for requiring reporting of serious ADRs are obvious, the reasons for excluding non-serious suspected ADRs associated with NHPs are less clear and not usually explicitly stated in regulatory instruments, although such reporting is required in some jurisdictions for conventional medicines. Non-serious symptoms may be associated with a serious underlying issue [111]. For example, patients with arrhythmia (which may be considered a serious ADR in this context [77]) may initially present with light-headedness, dizziness, fatigue, and/or fainting [112, 113]. At the outset, these symptoms could be reported and classified as non-serious ADRs unless they resulted in serious outcomes (e.g., hospitalisation) or are subsequently determined to be serious following evaluation by regulators and/or pharmacovigilance assessors within the NHPs industry [16]. A case in point illustrating such risks associated with NHPs is the herbal substance ephedra (Ephedra sinica Stapf), a stimulant due to the sympathomimetic constituents, ephedrine and pseudoephedrine. Ephedra species exposure has been associated with cardiac AEs [114]. The omission of non-serious ADRs from routine reporting requirements could potentially result in safety concerns being unreported and undetected, or detected at later stages, when the harm has become greater. Recognising the importance of reporting all ADRs, industry stakeholders in certain jurisdictions (e.g., MAHs the EU) are required to report all serious and non-serious ADRs [111], including for herbal medicinal products.

There are also variations in regulatory requirements for PSURs, literature screening, inclusion of ADRs from foreign countries and QPPV. Based on the vigilance module of the WHO global benchmarking tool, developed for evaluation and benchmarking of regulatory systems, the availability of these components forms a more mature pharmacovigilance system. Nevertheless, the WHO global benchmarking tool currently focusses on medicines and vaccines and does not explicitly address NHPs [115]. Despite the mandate in several jurisdictions to report serious ADRs associated with NHPs, there appear to be fewer (or less explicit) regulatory requirements for mandating or maintaining a comprehensive pharmacovigilance system. For example, in the USA and Canada, regulatory instruments explicitly outline the requirements for reporting serious AEs/ADRs without specifying the need for maintaining a pharmacovigilance system [69, 72]. In contrast, some other jurisdictions, including Australia, UK and EU, require sponsors/MAHs to operate an effective pharmacovigilance system [68, 71, 91]. Most recently, in July 2025, enhanced pharmacovigilance system requirements and responsibilities for MAHs, including stronger oversight of outsourced pharmacovigilance activities and database (EudraVigilance) monitoring for signals by all European Economic Area (EEA)-authorised MAHs, were introduced in the EU, effective from 12 February 2026 or sooner for some new obligations [116]. While a pharmacovigilance system is logically fundamental for the industry to collect, handle and curate reports of ADRs, explicit legal mandates for system standards may consider various factors, including the industry’s maturity and readiness to undertake pharmacovigilance activities. In certain jurisdictions, such as South Africa, applicants/holders of certificates of registration for medicines are mandated to report serious ADRs, i.e., they must report serious ADRs. However, relevant guidelines recommend that they should establish and operate a pharmacovigilance system to monitor their products, potentially indicating variability in the level of mandated requirements [65]. Given that pharmacovigilance systems for NHPs are less established than those for conventional pharmaceutical medicines in many jurisdictions, particularly regarding industry reporting obligations, it may be more practical and realistic to begin with minimal requirements, such as the reporting of serious ADRs for NHPs within 15 calendar days, allowing time for stakeholders to build capability and capacity, before moving on to requiring reports for all ADRs and other safety monitoring methods. That said, ensuring reporting quality is arguably as important as the requirement to report, as incomplete or poorly documented spontaneous reports can substantially hinder meaningful signal detection. Providing clear guidance on achieving these reporting requirements, through industry engagement, guidelines and government support (e.g. training and capacity building activities, and/or expertise support/consultation sessions), potentially could facilitate compliance and enhance pharmacovigilance practices.

The variations in the regulatory classification of NHPs across and within jurisdictions will have implications for the detection of safety signals at both the national and international levels. At the national level, because of a lack of reporting requirements, consumer reports of suspected ADRs associated with ‘food-type’ NHPs submitted to the industry may remain outside formal regulatory surveillance. Furthermore, as the responsibility for assessing and responding to emerging safety concerns (such as initiating a voluntary product recall) rests with the industry, in the absence of clearly defined requirements (e.g., specified timeline for key safety response actions), there is a risk of delayed or inconsistent response, as highlighted by the red yeast rice public health incident in Japan, involving contaminated food supplement products and multiple cases of serious renal injuries [10, 117]. Where there is routine capture of suspected ADR reports for ‘food-type’ NHPs or products regulated as ‘dietary supplements’ by national surveillance systems, these reports may be curated in a system separate to that used for conventional medicines and NHPs regulated in other ways. For example, in the USA, manufacturers, packers, or distributors of dietary supplements are required to submit reports of serious AEs associated with their products to the CAERS (now known as HFCS) [118], which is separate from the FDA’s Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) [119] used for reports of AEs for conventional pharmaceutical medicines. Both systems will contain reports concerning dietary supplement products, since some reports of AEs associated with conventional medicines submitted to FAERS will contain details of concomitant products, including NHPs. Such parallel, dual systems can potentially complicate signal detection and delay consolidated safety analysis at a national level. At the international level, differences in the regulatory classification of NHPs can impact whether or not such reports are submitted by national regulatory authorities to VigiBase, the WHO’s global database of ICSRs of suspected ADRs, maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre on behalf of the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring. Reports for ‘food-type’ NHPs, or products regulated as ‘dietary supplements’, are not necessarily submitted by national regulatory authorities to the VigiBase database. For example, the USA CAERS/HFCS (described above) does not submit its reports to VigiBase. Ultimately, this may have implications for early detection of safety signals associated with NHPs and their individual ingredients at the international level.

Fundamentally, pharmacovigilance and, in-turn, regulations seek to ensure products have an appropriate benefit-harm balance and recognise that this can change throughout the product lifecycle. Effective regulation of medical products should both safeguard public health and enable timely access to quality products. Across a product’s life cycle, pharmacovigilance benefit–risk assessments provide essential input to regulatory decisions regarding continued market access [120]. The relationship between pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements and pharmacovigilance activities is therefore inherently interdependent. In circumstances or environments where NHPs are perceived (and regulated) as low risk, these products could be subject to less stringent regulatory and pharmacovigilance requirements. However, limited safety monitoring and underreporting of ADRs can contribute to the lack of comprehensive safety evidence, reinforcing the perception that these products are low risk. Such dynamics could potentially lead to a self-reinforcing cycle, where regulatory leniency and (safety) evidence gaps perpetuate each other. Compared with regulating conventional pharmaceuticals, NHPs present additional challenges in achieving an appropriate benefit–harm balance, as high-quality evidence supporting the health benefits of these products is limited or lacking in most cases for specific products. Consequently, safety signals detected through pharmacovigilance could shift the benefit–harm balance, potentially supporting more cautious interpretations. This review did not further assess the impact of varying regulatory requirements on industry practices and subsequent pharmacovigilance activities. Further research could examine industry reporting practices and compliance, as well as associated regulatory and public health outcomes at the national level, through analyses of national pharmacovigilance databases and in-depth stakeholder engagement activities such as industry and public (consumer) surveys. The dynamics between consumer perceptions of safety, NHPs purchasing, and subsequent ADR reporting behaviours also warrant further exploration through theory-informed qualitative research and population-based surveys. Specifically, investigating consumer-industry-regulator ADR reporting patterns could provide further insights into the current and potential roles of industry in pharmacovigilance for NHPs. At the international level, a formal and systematic assessment of pharmacovigilance systems for surveillance of all types of NHPs (including ‘food-type’ or ‘dietary supplements’) through international stakeholder collaboration (e.g., among regulators, pharmacovigilance centres, industry) could provide a more comprehensive outlook on global NHPs pharmacovigilance practices and gaps.

Considerations Towards Harmonisation of Regulatory Requirements for Pharmacovigilance for NHPs

Although regulations generally differ across countries/regions, some similarities were observed. For example, countries/regions that impose regulatory requirements on the NHPs industry all impose a 15-day reporting timeline for reporting of serious AEs/ADRs by the NHPs industry in the USA [72], EU [71], UK [70], Canada [69], Australia [68], South Africa [65] and the Republic of Korea [46]. Such similarities potentially indicate alignment or efforts towards convergence or harmonisation of regulatory requirements, mirroring the path undertaken by the pharmaceutical industry for conventional medicines. International harmonisation for medicines began in the 1980s, pioneered by the EC, followed by the setting up of the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) through joint efforts by the regulatory authorities and pharmaceutical industry in 1990 [121]. In the face of globalisation, aligning international pharmaceutical regulatory requirements will likely benefit the industry by allowing smoother transitions of therapeutic products through the global market, avoiding repetitive testing, and reducing costs. Consequently, international harmonisation should improve patient access to safe and effective medicines [121, 122]. Specifically for pharmacovigilance, ICH provides guidance through the ICH E2 (2A-2F) series of guidelines for pharmacovigilance, which could be further adapted by individual countries [123]. ICH also conducts training for regulators, industry, and others from individual countries on these guidance documents [124, 125]. In addition to ICH pharmacovigilance guidelines, further work on developing an international benchmarking tool specific to pharmacovigilance for NHPs (such as extending or adopting the WHO global benchmarking tool [115]) could be particularly useful.

Beyond aligning technical requirements, international harmonisation (for conventional pharmaceutical medicines) also extends to joint or mutually recognised benefit-risk evaluations to develop regulatory capacities and improve efficiency. An example is the “Initiative of the National Regulatory Authorities for the joint evaluation of periodic safety update reports (PSURs), risk management plans (RMPs), and periodic benefit-risk evaluation reports (PBRERs)” by health authorities of Brazil, Chile and Canada [126]. An initiative in joint regulation was proposed by Australia and New Zealand in 2003, where both countries proposed to form an Australia New Zealand Therapeutic Products Agency (ANZTPA). ANZTPA would have sought to harmonise and jointly regulate therapeutic products, including complementary medicines. However, in 2011, it was announced that New Zealand would regulate NHPs separately [127], and the ANZTPA proposal did not come to fruition and eventually lapsed for several reasons [128]. There are currently limited (known or published) international efforts towards joint regulations specific to NHPs.

Considerable progress has been made in harmonising pharmacovigilance activities in general, including through requirements to undertake standardised coding of AEs using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA)—developed by ICH—in pharmacovigilance databases [129]. Specifically for herbal medicines, coding of herbal substances listed in spontaneous reports of ADRs was aided by the creation of a harmonised nomenclature for herbal medicinal products in the WHODrug Global® dictionary, the Herbal-Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (HATC) classification system. The HATC classification system links herbal ingredients to synonyms and vernacular names via the substance register of the WHODrug Global® dictionary, and is based on botanical science, traditional use and literature [130]. However, there are several limitations to this system, such as issues with coding multi-ingredient products, material used in raw/crude preparations, and the HATC classification system is not universally used [130].

Beyond variation in regulatory requirements between jurisdictions that have established frameworks for NHPs (such as those included in this review), the absence of, or minimal regulations for these products in other countries (not included in this review) can present additional barriers to international regulatory harmonisation. For example, in New Zealand, most NHPs are currently captured by the Dietary Supplements Regulations 1985 [131], which is (now) under the Food Act 2014 [132]. Manufacturers of NHPs, apart from a few products that are approved as ‘medicines’ [133–135] in New Zealand, are not required to undergo pre-market evaluation of quality, efficacy and safety, nor fulfil pharmacovigilance obligations for their products [128, 136]. Although this review does not examine countries with limited or no NHP regulation, the opportunities and challenges of regulatory development for NHPs in these countries remain important areas for future research.

Finally, because the use of NHPs is, to some extent (and in some communities commonly), linked to local and traditional practices, national policies for NHPs need to take into account local community histories, traditions, practices and cultural beliefs. These contextual factors should also be considered in efforts toward international alignment and regulatory harmonisation. For instance, when planning new regulations in Aotearoa New Zealand, the government’s obligations to Māori (indigenous people of the land) under Te Tiriti o Waitangi (the Māori language version of the Treaty of Waitangi: the document setting out the founding treaty partnership between Māori and the Crown) must be honoured. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Māori medicine (rongoā Māori) is the Indigenous medicine system and is considered a taonga (treasure) among Māori; Māori have the right to exercise rangatiratanga and mana motuhake (i.e., sovereignty, leadership, autonomy to make decisions and self-determination) over rongoā and, in this context, rongoā rākau (native New Zealand botanicals), which could be considered NHPs [3, 137]. In addition to specific cultural considerations, the degree of establishment of the traditional medicine system can influence the implementation of pharmacovigilance regulations. For example, in China, TCM injections are commonly used in hospital settings, supported by established standards for medicine quality and practitioner qualifications [138, 139]. These factors create a conducive environment for effective pharmacovigilance and the potential implementation of related regulations. Conversely, public sentiment that associates the long history of NHPs use with inherent safety may hinder the introduction or strengthening of pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements (in any jurisdiction). Public perception and opinions can influence policies and regulatory requirements [140, 141], and factors that facilitate, or reduce barriers to implementing pharmacovigilance regulations should be further explored.

Considering factors discussed above, international harmonisation of pharmacovigilance regulations for NHPs will therefore remain challenging, unless strong drivers exist to encourage and achieve alignment. Beyond crises (e.g., significant public health concerns associated with NHPs use), one potential driver could be substantial financial interest from industry stakeholders with significant market influence, such as large multinational companies. The potential for cost savings and easier compliance across multiple countries may motivate these companies to support harmonisation, most likely by adopting the standards of a major market. Drivers for and barriers to harmonising international pharmacovigilance requirements for NHPs from an industry perspective have not been well explored and warrant further examination via high-level stakeholder surveys and in-depth interviews/discussions. In addition to regulatory discrepancies, the focus of this review, other barriers within the industry, such as cost considerations, availability of resources, technical expertise and knowledge, and organisational or sectoral culture, may also influence attitudes towards harmonisation.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first review to provide a comprehensive overview of regulations regarding pharmacovigilance requirements for the NHPs industry. Despite the inherent methodological challenges in grey literature reviews, this review applied, to the extent possible, a systematic and structured approach in identifying and including relevant information from various sources, combined with a standardised data extraction process, which enabled meaningful comparison of pharmacovigilance requirements across jurisdictions.

This review is limited by its inherent structural selection bias and English language restrictions. It was beyond the scope of this review to include every nation globally due to document accessibility and language barriers. Although this approach was selected for its feasibility in enabling meaningful data interpretation across countries with high health expenditure, the findings are not representative of lower- and middle-income countries (LMICs), many of which may make substantial use of traditional medicines and other NHPs in healthcare. Furthermore, language restrictions limited this review’s ability to interpret and draw in-depth insights from certain non-English-dominant countries with high prevalence of use of NHPs, particularly China. Nevertheless, insights from the included jurisdictions in this review still provide valuable reference points for the strengthening of pharmacovigilance systems for NHPs, while acknowledging the need for country-specific adaptations and economic priorities.

Another limitation of this review includes the use of a single (yet experienced) investigator for literature searching, screening, and data extraction, which may have introduced investigator bias and human error. However, the interpretation of findings was discussed among all investigators, and clarifications were sought and incorporated from experts for specific countries. In many instances, supportive guidelines or clarification notes issued by the relevant regulators provide additional details on pharmacovigilance regulations. It is possible that some information may have been missed, especially when not explicitly stated in the regulatory instruments included here, or when supportive guidance documents/clarification notes are not readily available in English (despite English versions of the main regulations being accessible). Regardless of the accuracy and comprehensiveness of document inclusion, regulatory provisions can still be interpreted in different ways by stakeholders within the same jurisdiction. It is beyond the scope of this review to reflect, assess and compare such interpretations, nor is it intended to serve as a legal reference.

Lastly, this review has examined pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements in isolation: ideally, pharmacovigilance requirements should be interpreted in conjunction with other aspects of regulations that impact safety, including product quality requirements, manufacturing standards, permitted ingredient lists, permitted health claims and, not least, whether there is pre-market assessment of quality, safety and evidence for benefit claims. Nonetheless, pharmacovigilance remains essential for all marketed products, even those already subject to comprehensive pre-market evaluation of quality, efficacy, and safety. It is within this context that the present review has been undertaken.

Conclusions

Regulatory requirements relating to pharmacovigilance for the NHPs industry vary between and within countries/regions. Of the countries/regions reviewed, most of those with existing regulations for NHPs require the NHPs industry to report serious ADRs within 15 days. Differences in defining and categorising NHPs across jurisdictions could pose challenges towards international harmonisation of pharmacovigilance and subsequent benefit–risk assessments for these products. Further research should examine the impact of differences in pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements on industry practices and for pharmacovigilance for NHPs, as well as broadening the scope to countries not included in this review. To support effective evaluation and safeguard public health, future policy-oriented efforts are warranted to explore the feasibility of, pathways towards, and challenges associated with establishing minimum pharmacovigilance regulatory requirements for the NHPs industry (e.g., mandatory reporting of serious ADRs within 15 days) as potential benchmarks for harmonisation across all NHP categories.
