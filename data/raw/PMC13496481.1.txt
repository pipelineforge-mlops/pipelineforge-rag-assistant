==============================
Inferior vena cava (IVC) agenesis is a rare congenital anomaly associated with deep vein thrombosis (DVT), particularly in young adults without identifiable risk factors. We report the case of a 27-year-old male presenting with right lower extremity swelling and hip pain. Imaging revealed extensive thrombosis from the popliteal vein to the right common iliac vein. Diagnostic venography confirmed congenital absence of the IVC, with venous drainage via collateral lumbar pathways. The patient underwent catheter-directed thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy. Given the extensive thrombus burden and risk of pulmonary embolism, lifelong anticoagulation with a direct oral anticoagulant was initiated. This case highlights that in young patients with extensive iliofemoral DVT and no identifiable risk factors, congenital IVC agenesis should be suspected. Timely venographic confirmation is crucial, as management requires both endovascular intervention and lifelong anticoagulation due to impaired venous return.

1. Introduction

Inferior vena cava (IVC) atresia, or agenesis, is a rare congenital vascular anomaly implicated in approximately 5 % of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) cases.1,2 The most commonly postulated etiology is embryological dysgenesis, although intrauterine or perinatal thrombosis has also been proposed as a secondary process contributing to IVC agenesis.3,4 Extensive collateral circulation—primarily via the azygos, hemiazygos, paraspinal, and abdominal wall veins—compensates for IVC agenesis.1 Although often asymptomatic, IVC agenesis may present with symptoms secondary to an increased risk of chronic venous insufficiency and deep vein thrombosis, particularly in young individuals, making it a clinically relevant anomaly for vascular surgeons.5 We present the case of a 27-year-old male who presented with right lower extremity swelling and hip pain.

2. Case-presentation

A 27-year-old male with no significant past medical history, no intravenous drug use, and no known family history of clotting disorders presented with right lower extremity swelling and right hip pain for several days. On initial evaluation, the differential diagnosis included catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), protein C or S deficiency, antithrombin III deficiency, Factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin gene mutation, and JAK2 mutation. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered unlikely due to no prior heparin exposure, and vaccine-induced thrombosis (VIT) was ruled out given no recent vaccination. Laboratory Parameters of Coagulation Panel and Thrombophilia Screening are presented in Table 1.

Initial Doppler venous ultrasound showed deep venous thrombosis from right popliteal vein to right common femoral vein. A CT abdomen and pelvis revealed extensive thrombosis extending from the right popliteal vein to the common femoral, external iliac, internal iliac, right common iliac vein. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed a moderately dilated right ventricle with an estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) of 36 mmHg and preserved left ventricular function (Table 2).

The patient was evaluated by the vascular surgery team and underwent a diagnostic venogram, followed by placement of a lysis catheter in the right common iliac vein, femoral vein, and popliteal vein. Intraoperatively, a thrombus was noted extending from the femoral vein to the right common iliac vein. Notably, there was a congenital absence of the IVC, with the right common iliac vein draining into the lumbar venous plexus. The patient subsequently returned to the operating room for venogram and thrombectomy. Given the congenital absence of the IVC and the extensive, life-threatening DVT and risk of pulmonary embolism (PE), the patient was started on long-term anticoagulation. He will require lifelong therapy with a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), such as apixaban or rivaroxaban.

3. Literature-review

We also present a systematic review of cases of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in young individuals with underlying inferior vena cava (IVC) agenesis, a condition typically characterized by extensive involvement of the lower extremities. The inclusion criteria described as: (1) congenital IVC agenesis as the underlying cause, (2) presentation with DVT or bilateral DVT, and (3) patient age <45 years, irrespective of gender. Duplex ultrasound was primarily employed as the diagnostic modality. Anticoagulation therapy, predominantly with low molecular weight heparin and direct oral anticoagulants, represented the standard treatment approach, while interventional therapies were reserved for more severe cases (Table 3).

4. Discussion

Agenesis or atresia of the inferior vena cava (AIVC/IVC atresia) is a rare congenital anomaly, with a reported prevalence of 0.3–0.6 % in the general population.1 Although frequently asymptomatic due to well-developed collateral venous circulation, it may present with deep vein thrombosis (DVT), particularly in young males, and often in the absence of common risk factors.1,3,6 A consistent male predominance (2:1) has been noted, although this may reverse in cases associated with congenital anomalies.3

Isolated IVC atresia most commonly presents with extensive bilateral lower extremity DVT in patients under 40 years of age.1–3 In such cases, the absence of risk factors such as surgery, immobilization, or known thrombophilia should prompt evaluation for anatomical abnormalities such as AIVC.3,6 Conventional duplex ultrasonography often fails to identify the atretic segment due to its operator dependence, necessitating advanced imaging. Cross-sectional contrast-enhanced CT or MR venography— especially magnetic resonance angiography —is the preferred diagnostic modality due to superior visualization of the venous system.1,3 Several studies have demonstrated a strong association between IVC atresia and inherited thrombophilic conditions, particularly Factor VLeiden mutation (16.4%in AIVC vs. 4.8 % in controls), as well as MTHFR mutations in conjunction with hyperhomocysteinemia.6 While prothrombin G20210A mutation and lupus anticoagulant appear to play no significant role, these findings suggest a multifactorial thrombotic predisposition and raise the possibility of a hereditary component, supported by familial clustering in some reported cases.6 Age and male gender are significant positive predictors of inferior vena cava agenesis (IVCA), as reported by Atilla et al. (2015), who found a relative risk of 3.274 (95%CI: 2.064–5.192; p < 0.0001) for male gender, and a multivariate-adjusted relative risk of 5.789 (95%CI: 3.131–10.701; p < 0.0001) for those with hereditary coagulation defects and anatomic malformations.7

Although IVC atresia is rarely life-threatening when isolated, a small number of patients may present with complications such as IVC aneurysm, which have been managed successfully with surgery and without mortality.3 In contrast, cases associated with syndromic congenital anomalies may carry higher morbidity and mortality unrelated to the IVC defect itself.3 Most patients are managed conservatively with anticoagulation, compression therapy, and thrombolysis when indicated. There is no consensus on the duration of anticoagulation; however, long-term or indefinite therapy was administered in over 50 % of cases reviewed.1,3 The 2021 Clinical Practice Guidelines by the European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) recommend acute thrombus removal in such cases; however, there is still no consensus on the definitive long-term management. 8,9 Recurrence or post-procedural status typically justifies lifelong anticoagulation.1,3 Surgical or endovascular recanalization may be considered in patients with severe chronic venous insufficiency unresponsive to conservative treatment. While symptom relief is achieved in ~80 % of cases, the 1-year failure rate approaches 40 %, and long-term durability beyond 2–3 years remains uncertain 1,3. Given these limitations, an endovascular-first approach is typically favored, with patient selection based on anatomical feasibility, physician expertise, and institutional capability.1,3

In conclusion, IVC atresia should be considered in young patients presenting with unprovoked or recurrent DVT, particularly if bilateral or right-sided. Early recognition is key to avoiding misdiagnosis, unwarranted investigations, and complications such as chronic venous insufficiency. A tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approach, including long-term follow-up, is essential for optimal outcomes.1–3,6

5. Case-summary

A 27-year-old male presented with right lower extremity swelling and hip pain. Imaging revealed extensive iliofemoral thrombosis. Intraoperative venography confirmed congenital absence of the IVC with collateral drainage. The patient underwent thrombectomy and was started on lifelong anticoagulation.

6. Key clinical message

In young patients with extensive iliofemoral DVT and no identifiable risk factors, congenital IVC agenesis should be suspected. Timely venographic confirmation is crucial, as management requires both endovascular intervention and lifelong anticoagulation due to impaired venous return.
