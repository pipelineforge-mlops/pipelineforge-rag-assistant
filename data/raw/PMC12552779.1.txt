INTRODUCTION

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, inflammatory skin disease characterized by skin barrier dysfunction. The pathogenesis of AD is considered to be multifactorial and complex with many environmental exposures now linked with AD1. Atopy and AD can be seen to “run in families”. While epigenetic changes, such as DNA methylation, modification of histones, and RNA processing may explain familial clustering, no consistent genetic sequence mechanisms for pathogenesis have been found2. In the last 5 decades, the sharp increase in rates of AD in developed countries compared to less developing areas3,4, cannot be explained by genetic drift alone. Meanwhile, increasing evidence connecting the environment to AD risk and severity to outdoor pollutants such as diesel exhaust, factory exhaust, and wildfires1.

The epithelial barrier protects skin from external exposures and the “epithelial barrier hypothesis” asserts that damage to this barrier exposes the gut or skin to noxious external stimuli and correlates with many inflammatory skin disorders including AD5. The external exposome includes air pollutants, allergens, chemical toxins, and microbes as well as internal exposures such as diet and antibiotic use, which impact barrier integrity contributing to AD pathogenesis6.

General air pollutants

The best studied outdoor pollutants associated with AD and other atopic disorders include traffic-related air pollutants (TRAPs) such as particulate matter less than 2.5 microns in diameter (PM2.5), PM10, volatile organic compounds, and carbon dioxide7. There is strong evidence to support increased risk of AD in children whose mothers lived near major roads while pregnant8 and exposure to factory exhaust with increased visits AD clinic visits7,9,10. Although smoke exposure has long been known to exacerbate asthma11, wildfires have also been associated with AD flares10,12. Wildfires such as those in southern California were associated with an acute increase in clinic visits for AD as well as online searches for AD-related terms such as “itch”10,12.

The pollutants included in the air quality index (AQI) include carbon monoxide, ozone, PM2.5, PM10, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxide also affect the indoor air and has been associated with health problems13. While the evidence connecting AQI with AD susceptibility in inconsistent, there does seem to be a role of NO2 in increased trans epidermal water loss (TEWL)14. NO2 has also been linked to microbiome dysbiosis, showing that exposure impairs growth and alters biofilm of skin commensals15. In addition, PM10 has been shown to increase transcription of AD-related markers16 while PM2.5 erodes epithelial barriers through oxidative and proinflammatory mechanisms17.

Day-to-day activities of life contribute to additional chemical additives/exposures with AD-relevance such as organophosphates, phthalate plasticizers, microplastics, fluorinated surfactants, and flame retardants18; such exposures may be dermally contacted but are also inhaled19. Translocation into systemic compartment, and subsequent detection in the blood and urine is possible, which may facilitate experimental detection18.

BTEX, VOCs, and isocyanates

Volatile organic compounds (carbon-based atmospheric chemicals, VOCs) such as benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene (BTEX) and isocyanates are each linked to an increased risk of AD9,20. BTEX and isocyanates became part of automobile exhaust around the time that AD rates began to rise in the US (circa 1970), after that transition away from leaded gasoline and onto catalytic converters. Of note, VOCs tend to concentrate indoors. This paradoxically means indoor spaces may present greater risk than outdoor unless homes/schools are properly ventilated1. However, VOCs may be emitted by indoor sources including home renovation products, skincare products, furniture, tobacco smoke, wallpaper glue, and synthetic fabrics21–24.

The strongest link with AD may be with xylene and the most common isocyanate, Toluene diisocyanate (TDI). These chemicals have been geographically linked to AD and experimentally shown to disrupt skin barrier function, lipid-biology in skin commensals, and directly activate the itch receptor transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1)9,20,25.

Exposures relevant to gut and skin

Various topical products have been associated with AD such as certain skincare products, cleansers, and synthetic fabrics26,27. There have been in vitro studies to connect AD-relevant dysbiosis with certain chemicals common in home goods such as parabens, benzyl alcohol, formaldehyde, and lanolin27. Another skin barrier damaging chemical found in many home products is sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), found in soaps, shampoos, laundry detergents, dishwashing detergents, and toothpaste5,28. One study, in mice, showed skin barrier dysfunction through TEWL measurements four hours after being treated with SLS containing laundry detergents28. Similarly, rinse aid residue found after professional dishwashing was sufficient to cause cytotoxicity and epithelial barrier damage29. In vitro studies have also implicated dietary emulsifiers in the induction of epithelial barrier dysfunction and inflammation30.

Heavy metals such as lead and arsenic may present risk through contamination in water used for drinking or bathing. Both serum lead levels and prenatal arsenic exposures were each positively correlate with AD31,32. Exposure to per-and poly-fluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) in the diet or inhalation from factory exhaust was shown to aggravate IgE-mediated symptoms33. However, the evidence linking PFAS to atopy is still conflicting and limited.

Although this report focuses on the role of aerosolized exposures, one should not overlook the increasing evidence with relationship between the gut microbiome and AD development through dietary exposures. While the primary example of iatrogenic dysbiosis, perinatal antibiotic exposure, has also been linked to AD, stronger evidence is needed34,35. Diets low in fiber and prebiotics but high in refined saturated fats, refined sugars, and process ingredients have been strongly associated with AD36,37. A recent systematic review highlights exposure to fructose, sugar sweetened beverages, ultra-processed foods, and advanced glycated end products to be associated with increased allergic diseases38. Another study showed that a monosodium glutamate (MSG)-restricted diet for just one week yielded improved SCORAD scores in AD patients39. One study identified a protective association against atopy for perinatal consumption of fruits, vegetables, and fermented foods40. However, the efficacy of dietary modifications to improve AD symptoms remains controversial and under studied. Given the attention dietary modifications receive within the chat communities for patients and caregivers with AD27,41, the field would greatly benefit from an objective study elaborating which dietary practices offer genuine benefit to patients.

Shared mechanisms

While the mechanisms of atopic pathogenesis remain to be fully elucidated, exposures tend to impact one of three pathways: (1) direct erosion of barrier function, (2) induction of dysbiosis through altered commensal survival or metabolism, and (3) induction of inflammation.

While the role of barrier proteins, such as filaggrin, have been the most studied recent data has indicated that barrier-lipids are the most consistent predictors of AD risk42–45. Four separate studies, in four nations, with a total of three different modalities each identified ceramide-sphingolipid defects in the first weeks of life as predictive of subsequent development of AD42–45. Ceramides and sphingolipids help with optimal skin barrier function and regulate inflammatory responses46. Damage to the epithelial barrier allows toxins and microbial products to enter the bloodstream through the skin or gut47–49. Although translation of allergens through the skin is the current paradigm for connecting AD to food allergy risk, additional complexity may be present in the form of gut-remodeling induced by neuroimmune modulators50.

One important source of ceramide-sphingolipid barrier lipids are the bacterial commensals on the skin9,20,51. Loss of normal commensal bacteria like Staphylococcus epidermidis and Roseomonas mucosa may lead to reduced production of ceramides; S. epidermidis produces sphingomyelinases which help boost host-derived ceramides52 while R. mucosa directly produces ceramides9,20,51. Although causal evidence for the loss or gain of any specific organism in AD is lacking, AD has been associated with reductions in Lactobacilli and Prevotella with an increase in the Firmicute to Bacteroides ratio43,53.

Induction of inflammation may be mediated by several different inflammatory mediators. The most cited is the induction of allergic cytokines such as interleukin (IL-) 4, IL-13, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP)1. However, mechanisms of inflammation may also include direct activation of skin itch and thermo-receptors such as TRPA1 and TRPV125,54–58, generation of oxidative stress, and direct activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and Toll-like receptors (TLR-) 4 and TLR51,59.

Mitigation strategies and clinical implications

Given the strong evidence of environmental factors on AD development and severity, current management strategies of AD should move beyond medical management exclusively and focus on preventative and non-prescription strategies. A mnemonic “HELPSS AD” was proposed to suggest being mindful of toxins in home environment, use of emollients, detergent chemicals, role of probiotics, daily soaks to assist with TEWL, strong social supports, being mindful of air quality effects, and avoiding ultra processed diets could impact AD management60 (Figure 1).

Updated AD practice parameters suggest “bland fragrance-free” emollients; however, there are no recommendations for specific brands61. While thicker ointments can offer a better skin barrier, creams and lotions are often more user-friendly with improved adherence62. Overall, there is not robust evidence to suggest any emollient over another, apart from the risk of specific ingredients linked with dysbiosis or risk of contact dermatitis26. Similarly, the evidence for the use of early-life emollients as prevention for AD has been debated; currently, the evidence suggests there may be some benefit in high-risk patients with strong family history of atopy63–65 but further investigations will be needed to elucidate differing effects of emollient formulations.

There is limited evidence to suggest the utility of air filters in the mitigation of indoor pollutant-related AD exacerbation. One small study done found some improvement in SCORAD and reduction in TEWL in AD group after installation of air purifiers in the home66. Another study found improvements in patients with allergic rhinitis with six weeks of air purifier use in the home67; whereas results from a metanalysis of air purification for asthma yielded mixed results68. Systematic ventilation designed to draw filtered air into indoor spaces for circulation can greatly improve overall air quality69,70. However, air purifiers are traditionally rated based on their ability to remove dust or PM10 from the air; meaning that efficacy against specific chemicals is often unknown1.

Overall, a better awareness of AD triggers is essential for providers wishing to best care for their patients. Online, patients and caregivers frequently discuss the identification and avoidance of specific triggers27,41. Although many of these discussions are anecdotal rather than scientific, providers who do not discuss their patients’ perceived concerns may risk being seen as only interested in dispensing medication. Given the time constrains for a given clinic visit, providers may consider providing handout materials or use patient questionnaires to focus the conversations.

Implications for the future

The impact of climate change on allergic diseases has recently been reviewed71. Among the many concerns related to global climate changes, specific concerns include severe weather leading to dramatic shifts in humidity and temperature. While such shifts have more obvious implications for allergic rhinitis due to changing growth patterns of allergenic plants, increased dust storms, temperature changes, and humidity shifts all may impact AD as well1,20,72. Shifting wind patterns will alter the reach and distribution of aerosolized chemicals. Ground water evaporation will cause water-based chemicals to aerosolize more frequently, spread further, while also concentrating the chemicals in the water remaining on land. Thus, exposures inducing allergic skin disease will become more common due to the effects of climate change.

SUMMARY

While there is a building level of evidence that various pollutants and environmental triggers contribute to development of and worsening severity of AD, we still have limited understanding to explain patient heterogeneity. It is also difficult to ethically conduct exposure and provocation studies involving human subjects. Many of the pollutant-related exposures may be best addressed at the government level, considering principles of environmental justice and the systemic inequalities that disproportionately expose low-income and minority communities to these toxins. Novel therapies such as biologics and JAK inhibitors have become staples of therapy in the high-income countries which has further increased the gap and disparity as these therapies are not available in most middle- and low-income nations. Thus, a focus on preventative and mitigation strategies is paramount in the future.

Key Points

Air pollution and dietary exposures show greater connection to atopic dermatitis than innate factors.

Specific air pollutants presenting the greatest concern include: volatile organic compounds (VOC); benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene (BTEX) family chemicals; and isocyanate containing compounds.

Mechanisms of pathology are related to epithelial barrier erosion, generation of dysbiosis, and induction of inflammation.

Patients and providers should be aware of the specific mitigation strategies that are now possible.

Synopsis

Mounting evidence suggests atopic dermatitis (AD) is primarily mediated by environmental exposures. This article focuses on specific air pollutants such as isocyanates and those in the benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene (BTEX) family. We discuss the current strength and limitations of the data linking air pollution to AD. Brief mention is given to dietary and water-borne factors of concern. The proposed mechanisms of pathology for these chemicals are detailed. Finally, mitigation strategies are discussed with a focus on facilitating discussion between providers and patients related to the ways of improving AD without a prescription.

CLINICS CARE POINTS

Providers and patients with atopy should be informed of the established connections between environmental exposures and allergic disease.

Providers seeing patients for atopic diseases should inquire about the indoor and outdoor air quality risks.

Use of informational handouts or targeted questionnaires may be useful for providers wishing into engage their patients about perceived AD triggers.

Patients with atopy should be provided with information about home products and dietary factors that may worsen their symptoms.

Long-term solutions will require mass scale collective action.

Immediate mitigation strategies may be summarized by the pneumonic “HELPSS AD” Home Décor – avoid products containing BTEX chemicals and isocyanates

Emollients – consider fragrance free emollients containing ceramides and free of preservatives

Laundering – when possible, avoid detergents containing parabens, formaldehyde, lanolin, alcohol ethoxylate, or SLS

Probiotics – consider probiotics

Soaks – daily baths to hydrate dry skin

Social supports – seek out family and local supports

Air quality – consider staying in doors on poor air quality days and use of air purifier

Diet – focus on clean eating of fresh fruits and vegetables with limits on processed foods containing additives and emulsifiers
