Abstract

Introduction

Toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS) is a rare, sterile, acute inflammatory reaction of the anterior segment following intraocular surgery. Although it is most commonly associated with cataract surgery, it has been reported after a variety of ophthalmic procedures. To our knowledge, no case of TASS following XEN Gel Stent Implantation has been previously described in the literature.

Case Presentation

An 85-year-old pseudophakic woman with primary open-angle glaucoma developed TASS after uneventful XEN63 implantation. Within 24 h postoperatively, the patient presented with diffuse limbus-to-limbus corneal edema, anterior chamber inflammation, and absence of pain or posterior segment involvement. Despite prompt and intensive topical corticosteroid therapy, she developed irreversible corneal decompensation, iris atrophy, and fixed nonreactive mydriasis. No additional cases were identified in the same surgical setting, suggesting a patient- or procedure-specific trigger. Potential causative factors included intracameral agents (lidocaine, acetylcholine, cefuroxime, viscoelastic material), possible migration of mitomycin C through the XEN stent lumen, or an inflammatory response to the gelatin-based device itself.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that TASS can occur following minimally invasive glaucoma surgery, including XEN Gel Stent implantation, and may result in severe and irreversible ocular sequelae. It underscores the importance of heightened postoperative vigilance and strict preventive protocols even in procedures perceived as low risk.

Plain Language Summary

Eye surgery is generally considered safe, but in rare cases, certain complications can occur even in routine procedures. One such complication is toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS), a noninfectious inflammation of the front part of the eye that typically develops within 24 h after surgery. TASS is caused by a reaction to substances that enter the eye during the operation, such as medications, fluids, or residues from surgical instruments. Although TASS has been described mainly after cataract surgery, it can occur following other types of eye surgery as well. In this report, we describe the first known case of TASS occurring after a glaucoma surgery called XEN63 Gel Stent implantation. Glaucoma is a disease that damages the optic nerve, often due to elevated pressure inside the eye. The XEN Gel Stent is a tiny tube inserted into the eye to help lower the pressure. The procedure is widely used. Our patient was an 85-year-old woman who developed severe eye inflammation the day after her surgery. Despite prompt treatment with anti-inflammatory eye drops, she suffered permanent damage to the cornea (the transparent front surface of the eye), resulting in significant visual loss. This case highlights that even procedures considered low-risk can, on rare occasions, lead to serious complications. Eye care professionals should be aware of TASS as a possible complication following this type of glaucoma surgery, so that it can be recognized and treated as early as possible.

Introduction

Toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS) is a rare entity characterized by a sterile, acute inflammatory reaction of the anterior segment, typically triggered by the entry of a noninfectious agent into the anterior chamber. Although it is most commonly associated with cataract surgery, it has also been reported following corneal transplantation, vitreoretinal surgery, and, less frequently, glaucoma procedures [1, 2]. In this report, we present the first documented case, to our knowledge, of TASS following implantation of the XEN Gel Stent, highlighting its clinical presentation, diagnostic considerations, and management. This report aims to contribute to the understanding of this rare but significant complication in minimally invasive glaucoma surgery. The CARE Checklist has been completed by the authors for this case report, attached as online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553653).

Case Presentation

An 85-year-old pseudophakic woman with type II diabetes mellitus presented for surgical management of progressive moderate primary open-angle glaucoma in her left eye. Preoperatively, best-corrected visual acuity was 20/25 in both eyes, and IOP in the left eye measured 20 mm Hg by Goldmann applanation tonometry despite topical treatment with latanoprost and a dorzolamide/timolol fixed combination. Slit-lamp examination confirmed correct pseudophakia bilaterally, and fundus examination revealed a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.8 in the left eye with no other pathological findings. Corneal topography was within normal limits, and endothelial cell density (ECD) was 1,855 cells/mm2.

The patient underwent uneventful XEN63 Gel Stent implantation via an ab interno approach in the superonasal quadrant of the left eye. The procedure was performed under sterile conditions (periocular skin preparation with 10% povidone-iodine and instillation of 5% povidone-iodine onto the ocular surface). Anesthesia consisted of topical agents (0.1% tetracaine hydrochloride and 0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride) combined with intracameral injection of 0.3 mL of 1% lidocaine hydrochloride. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 1% acetylcholine was administered intracamerally, and a cohesive viscoelastic agent (2% sodium hyaluronate) was used to fill the anterior chamber prior to stent deployment. At the conclusion of surgery, 0.1 mL of intracameral 1% cefuroxime was injected as routine prophylaxis, and 0.1 mL of 0.01% mitomycin C (MMC) was administered subconjunctivally at a site distant from the intended bleb.

On postoperative day 1 (shown in Fig. 1), slit-lamp examination revealed diffuse limbus-to-limbus epithelial and stromal corneal edema with a mild anterior chamber inflammatory reaction (+2 cells) in the absence of fibrin, hypopyon, or posterior segment involvement. Notably, the patient reported no ocular pain. IOP measured 29 mm Hg, and a shallow bleb with no evidence of leakage was observed. This constellation of findings raised a strong clinical suspicion of TASS, prompting early initiation of corticosteroid therapy. Infectious endophthalmitis was considered but deemed unlikely given the early onset within 24 h of surgery, the painless presentation, and the absence of posterior segment inflammation. Treatment was initiated with topical tobramycin/dexamethasone six times daily and brimonidine/timolol fixed combination twice daily.

Fig. 1. Postoperative day 1 – a Shallow filtering bleb. b Conjunctival hyperemia, limbal-to-limbal corneal edema, and anterior chamber inflammatory reaction.

Slit-lamp photographs of the left eye on postoperative day one. Panel a shows marked diffuse conjunctival and episcleral hyperemia with an elevated dome-shaped subconjunctival filtering bleb in the superior nasal quadrant. Panel b shows a close-up view of the anterior segment with diffuse corneal stromal edema reducing transparency from limbus to limbus, prominent conjunctival injection, and inflammatory cells in the anterior chamber.

At postoperative week 1, IOP had decreased to 16 mm Hg with a diffuse bleb. However, corneal edema persisted with marked Descemet’s membrane folds, and hypertonic sodium chloride 5% solution three times daily was added to the treatment regimen.

At postoperative month 1 (shown in Fig. 2), anterior chamber inflammation had resolved, and a partial reduction in corneal edema was observed. IOP remained stable at 16 mm Hg with a morphologically functional filtering bleb, as assessed on slit-lamp examination and anterior segment optical coherence tomography. Treatment was adjusted to topical dexamethasone six times daily, timolol twice daily, and hypertonic saline drops three times daily.

Fig. 2. a Two weeks postoperative. Marked diffuse stromal edema with Descemet folds. b One month postoperative. Notice partial resolution of corneal edema with no inflammatory reaction in the anterior chamber.

Slit-lamp photographs of the left eye at two postoperative timepoints. Panel a at 2 weeks shows a severely stromal corneal edema with loss of transparency and multiple Descemet folds. Panel b at 1 month shows substantially improved corneal clarity with mild residual haze, a clearly visible iris and pupil, and a quiet anterior chamber.

At postoperative month 3 (shown in Fig. 3), stromal edema had worsened, and endothelial pigment deposition with a recurrent mild anterior chamber inflammatory reaction (+2 cells) was observed. An iridocorneal synechia at the implantation site was identified, and the pupil was found to be fixed and dilated with no light response. IOP was 20 mm Hg. Anterior segment optical coherence tomography confirmed correct positioning of the XEN63 stent at the subconjunctival level with a functioning bleb. The treatment regimen was maintained.

Fig. 3. Three-month follow-up. a Persistent stromal edema, endothelial pigment dispersion, fixed and dilated pupil, and iridocorneal synechia at the XEN63 implantation site (yellow arrow). b Anterior segment OCT image showing the subconjunctival filtration bleb (red arrows) and the XEN Gel Stent pathway (blue arrow).

Figure 3 – Panel a is a slit-lamp photograph at 3 months showing persistent stromal corneal edema, brown endothelial pigment deposits, a fixed and widely dilated pupil, and a focal iridocorneal adhesion in the superonasal quadrant at the stent implantation site. Panel b is an anterior segment optical coherence tomography image showing an elevated subconjunctival bleb and the intrascleral channel of the XEN Gel Stent.

By postoperative month 5, anterior chamber inflammation had completely resolved. Nevertheless, irreversible corneal decompensation, iridocorneal synechia, endothelial pigment dispersion, and fixed nonreactive mydriasis persisted, with a best-corrected visual acuity of 20/200. The XEN63 stent remained well-positioned, with a functional bleb and an IOP of 16 mm Hg. Treatment was tapered to dexamethasone drops three times daily, timolol twice daily, and hypertonic sodium chloride 5% ointment at bedtime. The patient was referred for corneal evaluation to assess candidacy for endothelial keratoplasty.

Discussion

The inflammatory cascade of TASS is triggered by intraocular exposure to foreign materials or solutions during surgery, including toxins, medications, contaminants, preservatives, and residues [1]. Identifying the specific cause of TASS is challenging due to the broad range of potential triggers; inadequate cleaning of surgical instruments is the most commonly recognized cause of TASS [2].

Outbreaks of TASS have been described in the literature, often affecting multiple patients at the same surgical center and within a specific time frame. In the presented case, following a detailed investigation prompted by the report of TASS, no additional cases were identified in the same surgical session or within a 6-month period before and after the event. This isolated occurrence strongly suggests an isolated-patient or procedure-specific factor. Potential causative factors in this case include the intracameral administration of multiple agents, namely lidocaine, acetylcholine, viscoelastic material, and cefuroxime. Although TASS associated with intracameral cefuroxime has been described in the literature, the available evidence suggests that toxicity is dose-dependent and predominantly reported in the context of dilution errors leading to inadvertent overdose [3, 4]. Another cause may involve the use of shared or improperly flushed syringes or cannulas for different surgical solutions, leading to unintended interactions or contamination. Regarding the possible role of MMC, the XEN63 Gel Stent creates a communication between the anterior chamber and the subconjunctival space [5]. Although under physiological conditions flow proceeds from the anterior chamber outward, flow direction through any passive valveless conduit is determined by the pressure gradient across it: the subconjunctival injection of MMC could have transiently generated sufficient pressure to drive retrograde flow into the anterior chamber. No pharmacokinetic study has directly quantified MMC concentrations in the anterior chamber following subconjunctival injection in the context of XEN implantation. Moreover, a device-related inflammatory response related to the XEN implant itself, composed of cross-linked porcine-derived gelatin, may represent an additional contributing mechanism.

Clinically, TASS typically develops within the immediate postoperative period (12–24 h after surgery). Patients commonly present with blurred vision, ocular discomfort, conjunctival hyperemia, anterior chamber inflammatory reaction, and the hallmark sign of limbus-to-limbus corneal edema [6]. In our patient, these features were clearly evident on the first postoperative day, with no posterior segment involvement at any stage. The principal differential diagnosis, infectious endophthalmitis, was excluded based on the early onset within 24 h rather than the typical 4–7 days for endophthalmitis, the absence of pain, the characteristic pattern of corneal edema, and the absence of posterior segment signs. Notably, the absence of fibrin in this case does not argue against the diagnosis of TASS, as it is not a mandatory feature [1]. Postoperative anterior uveitis was also considered in the differential diagnosis; however, this entity does not characteristically produce the acute limbus-to-limbus corneal edema observed from postoperative day 1. Moreover, the subsequent development of irreversible corneal endothelial decompensation and Urrets-Zavalia syndrome is more consistent with a toxic insult than with an immune-mediated process. It must also be acknowledged that no aqueous humor tap for microbiological or cytological analysis was performed. The clinical decision to forgo aqueous sampling was based on the strong temporal and clinical profile favoring TASS, which made an infectious etiology highly unlikely. This represents a limitation of our case.

Long-term sequelae of TASS include irreversible corneal decompensation, secondary IOP elevation due to synechiae formation or trabecular meshwork damage, cystoid macular edema, and iris ischemia [1]. Despite intensive corticosteroid treatment, which led to the eventual resolution of anterior chamber inflammation – albeit with a recurrence at 3 months – our patient developed irreversible corneal endothelial failure and iris ischemia, resulting in iris atrophy and a fixed, nonreactive pupil, a condition described as Urrets-Zavalia syndrome [6].

Management of TASS requires early and aggressive topical corticosteroid therapy, typically prednisolone acetate 1% or dexamethasone 0.1%, administered 4–8 times daily for extended periods. In refractory cases, escalation to hourly dosing, as well as subconjunctival and oral corticosteroids, can be considered [2]. When inflammation fails to resolve, surgical options such as anterior chamber irrigation, removal of intraocular lenses or devices, or even pars plana vitrectomy have been proposed [1]. IOP control should rely on topical hypotensive agents, avoiding prostaglandin analogues. However, in cases with significant synechiae or extensive trabecular meshwork damage, topical therapy may be ineffective, requiring surgical management [2]. To address visually significant corneal decompensation, endothelial keratoplasty techniques such as Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty or Descemet-stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty have demonstrated the best outcomes [1].

The patient’s type II diabetes mellitus deserves specific mention as a contributing factor to the severity of the clinical course. This condition is known to reduce corneal ECD, morphology, and function [7] and has also been suggested as a potential risk factor for TASS following intraocular surgery, possibly through disruption of the blood-aqueous barrier and a pro-inflammatory systemic state [8]. Combined with a preoperative ECD of 1,855 cells/mm2 – already at the lower limit for a patient of this age – these factors likely contributed both to a reduced endothelial reserve and to a heightened susceptibility to toxic and inflammatory injury. This comorbidity profile also guided the decision not to escalate to oral corticosteroids when inflammation recurred at 3 months, given the risk of systemic complications in an elderly insulin-dependent patient in whom topical therapy had previously achieved resolution.

Prevention remains the most effective strategy for TASS. Rigorous attention to instrument sterilization and reprocessing protocols, avoidance of detergent or preservative contamination of irrigation solutions, and careful selection and preparation of intracameral medications are essential preventive measures [2]. In the context of glaucoma surgery with adjunctive agents such as MMC, meticulous technique to minimize inadvertent anterior chamber exposure is warranted.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that TASS may also occur, albeit rarely, following minimally invasive glaucoma surgery. While anterior chamber inflammation may respond to corticosteroid therapy, as in our patient, irreversible sequelae such as corneal decompensation and iris ischemia may persist and cause significant visual morbidity. This first reported case of TASS following XEN Gel Stent implantation underscores the importance of strict preventive measures, meticulous surgical protocols, and heightened postoperative vigilance.
