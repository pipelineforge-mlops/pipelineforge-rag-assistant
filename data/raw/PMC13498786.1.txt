Introduction

Digital tools, including WhatsApp, are widely used across Panama. These tools may serve as mobile health (mHealth) interventions to maintain patient contact, especially among individuals who have difficulty attending appointments. However, prior use and comfort with WhatsApp-based health communication among People Living with HIV (PLHIV) have not been previously examined in Panama.

Methods

During August-October 2024, this cross-sectional study recruited participants from two urban antiretroviral (ART) clinics and three community-based organizations in and near Panama City and Colón. A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect information on prior use and comfort with WhatsApp-based communication with providers and for receiving health information. Descriptive statistics and multivariable regression described associations with sociodemographic, HIV care engagement, substance use, and psychosocial factors.

Results

Among 277 participants (median age 37 years, interquartile range 28-45 years), 33.6% had communicated with providers and 47.7% received health information via WhatsApp. Over 80% reported comfort using WhatsApp with a provider and for receiving health information. Discrimination was associated with prior provider communication (AOR=2.129, 90%CI:1.12-4.047) and comfort (AOR=2.324, 90%CI:1.202-4.492). Missed appointments were associated with lower odds of provider communication (AOR=0.567, 90%CI:0.311-1.034). Recent drug use was associated with having received health information (AOR=2.16, 90%CI:1.054-4.465); not yet engaging in care was associated with lower odds of prior use (AOR=0.374, 90%CI:0.145-0.963).

Conclusions

Prior use of WhatsApp for health communication was modest, but comfort was high. Associations with discrimination, drug use, and retention in care may indicate that WhatsApp could be relevant for individuals experiencing psychosocial and behavioural vulnerabilities.

Introduction

Over the past decades, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-related expenditures in Panama have increased, including international funding; however, the country continues to fall short of global targets.1,2 In 2025, abrupt international funding freezes (for instance, those by U.S. Agency for International Development [USAID]) caused immediate program delays and service disruptions, underscoring the need for modifiable, low-cost models. 3 According to Panama´s Ministry of Health, 96% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) know their status, 69% are on antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 84% of those are virally suppressed (i.e. 58% of all PLHIV are virally suppressed). 4 Persistent gaps in HIV outcomes remain in Panama, with higher prevalence and incidence among men who have sex with men and transgender women, in addition to among those who experience mental health challenges and economic hardship. These factors, together with uneven access to services across the country, continue to affect how PLHIV engage with HIV care.5,6 Therefore, interventions are needed to maintain communication with patients and support care continuity.

One intervention option is mobile health interventions (mHealth). 7 mHealth interventions can reach large numbers of individuals and tend to have low operating costs.3,8 Panama mirrors global trends regarding access to digital means. The country has extremely high market penetration, widespread (>85%) access to 3G-5G networks and use of WhatsApp; most buy unlimited data plans on day, week or month basis.9,10 Despite this access across the country, Panama faces pronounced socioeconomic inequality, 11 which shapes access to healthcare and HIV-related services. Access to HIV services in Panama is not uniform.5,6 Some areas have long travel distances, limited transportation, and shortages of health services, which can interrupt regular care. 5 Experiences of stigma, discrimination, and mental health disorders can discourage PLHIV from staying in care.5,6 Furthermore, disruptions in international funding or pandemic-related barriers may upend access altogether. 8 mHealth may help PLHIV overcome these barriers by providing an accessible, low-cost tool that supports communication and continuity of care throughout diverse settings.

A growing body of research indicates that mHealth interventions can support HIV-related outcomes, including ART adherence reminders, appointment coordination, retention in care, and motivational support. 12 However, their effectiveness may vary and much depends on intervention design and acceptability.13,14 Importantly, mHealth interventions must be acceptable to both patients and providers to be integrated into routine care, especially in contexts with significant social and institutional barriers.15,16

Evidence from Latin American and Caribbean (LAC) countries shows promise for message-based interventions for PLHIV. For example, a randomized trial in Mexico showed increased adherence and lower viral loads using SMS text messages. 17 Meanwhile, studies in Brazil showed improved adherence to ART 18 and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). 19 A WhatsApp and SMS-based study in Peru demonstrated the method’s multidomain utility by encouraging interactions between healthcare providers and PLHIV. 20 In contrast to emerging regional evidence, Panama is mainly absent from the literature, despite documented barriers to adherence and retention in care and widespread use of mobile platforms.5,6

Within the wide mHealth landscape, WhatsApp is a particularly relevant platform as it is used widely for everyday communication and therefore may be practical for message-based health communication. WhatsApp is one of the dominant mobile communication tools worldwide, especially in Latin America, and is used daily for personal, commercial, community, and institutional communication.21,22 WhatsApp is particularly relevant in Panama for daily communication and interventions.23,24 Despite WhatsApp’s ubiquity, no studies have assessed how PLHIV in Panama perceive or use the platform for health-related communication. Understanding patterns of potential comfort and prior engagement with health communication is essential to lay the groundwork for the development of safe and accessible digital interventions for PLHIV, especially during a period of fluctuating resources driven by reductions in international support.

Little is known about how PLHIV in Panama engage with mHealth tools and how comfortable they feel using them, which makes it difficult for programs to tailor digital approaches to local needs. Experiences with stigma, discrimination, mental health concerns, and inconsistent care may influence how people use mHealth tools by increasing interest in remote communication that reduces face-to-face exposure or by creating privacy and confidentiality concerns around digital use.25–27 Therefore, these experiences must be considered when planning interventions. WhatsApp is already used informally in many health settings and is nearly universally used across Panama. 28 Given the high penetration of WhatsApp and its growing use in health settings, understanding who uses WhatsApp for health communication and who would feel comfortable doing so can inform intervention development for those who find it difficult to attend in-person clinic visits. The study aims to describe the prevalence and correlates of prior WhatsApp-based health communication and comfort with use among PLHIV in Panama. The findings can help guide the development of interventions that complement clinic-based services.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study among adults (≥18 years) living with HIV who attended three community organizations or two Ministry of Health-run ART clinics in Panama City and Colón City between August and October 2024. A cross-sectional design was used to examine at one point in time the prevalence and correlates of prior WhatsApp-based health communication and comfort with WhatsApp use among PLHIV who attend one of two urban clinics in Panama. The cross-sectional elements included adults living with HIV and collection of self-reported questionnaire data occurred between August and October 2024. Descriptive and regression analyses were used to examine prior use, comfort and associated sociodemographic, HIV care engagement, substance use and psychosocial factors. The subsample from the community organization included transgender women who were living with HIV and were recruited through the parent study’s community-based recruitment component. These participants met the same eligibility criteria as those who were recruited from the clinics, completed the same questionnaire and were part of the same parent study database. Although the subsample was small, the participants were retained in the pooled analysis to not exclude this population. However, the sample was not large enough to support separate subgroup analyses by recruitment site. Potential participants were approached by one member of study team during regularly scheduled HIV care appointments at each clinic (Monday-Friday) or during meetings at the community-based organizations for psychosocial support for transgender women. Study staff approached potential participants and invited them to participate. Non-participation was discussed previously. 29 All potential participants were informed of the study objectives; those who agreed to learn more were offered additional information in a one-on-one meeting with study staff. Participants reviewed the written consent form in Spanish, with a witness assisting if needed due to literacy or signature limitations. Participants received a US$2 phone card as compensation. If the individual chose to participate, they signed the consent form. However, if they were unable to sign their name, they used a fingerprint, and a witness signed their name.

The sample size (N=149 for Panama City and N=106 for Colón) was based on estimating the prevalence of severe depression (main study outcome) of 12% among PLHIV in the two ART clinics (total clinic population of Panama City clinic of 1750 and 300 in Colón).

Data were collected at the time of recruitment, using a self-administered Spanish-language questionnaire on a tablet computer or a personal cellphone via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), hosted at Florida State University.30,31 Study staff were available to assist participants with any questions or difficulties in administering questionnaires.

Measures

mHealth measures

The following questions assessed prior use of mobile WhatsApp for health communication: In the past year: (a) Have you communicated with medical providers through WhatsApp? (no/yes) (b) Have you received health information via WhatsApp? (no/yes). Anticipated comfort in use of mHealth was assessed by a) Would you feel comfortable using WhatsApp to communicate with a medical provider? (totally agree/agree/neutral/disagree/strongly disagree), and b) Would you feel comfortable receiving health information via WhatsApp? (totally agree/agree/neutral/disagree/strongly disagree). Prior experiences were dichotomized as no/yes. Perceived comfort responses were dichotomized as agree vs did not agree, with “agree” and “totally agree” coded as agreement with comfort, and neutral, disagree and strongly disagree responses coded as did not agree. We used this approach to identify participants who endorsed comfort with WhatsApp-based health communication instead of those who were neutral or uncertain. Because of this, the “did not agree” category should not be interpreted as “discomfort”.

Independent variables

Variables included sociodemographic factors (age, gender [cisgender woman/Cisgender man/Transgender/Non-binary], country of birth [Panama/another country], ethnicity [AfroPanamanian, AfroDescendent non Panamanian, Black/Indigenous/Asian/Another group], education completed [No formal education, primary-middle school only/Secondary/University or above], employment [Not employed/Employed], biweekly income [no income,≤$400, >$400], and resource sufficiency for day to day living [Never insufficient resources/Ever has insufficient resources]); comorbidity diagnoses [None/1 or more CVD or metabolic diagnoses], sexually transmitted infections [None/1 or more STI diagnoses]); history of substance use (alcohol [monthly or less/2 or more times a month], smoking [Never/Past/Current], and drug use in past 3 months [Never used/Used]; HIV-related experiences: years living with HIV, ART status [interrupted ART for a month or more, never interrupted], care retention in past 12 months [Never missed appointments/Missed ≥1 appointments/Has not had an appointment]; intimate partner violence experiences with current or most recent partner [Never/Ever]; discrimination, Everyday Discrimination Scale (likart scale from almost every day to never), 32 HIV-related discrimination [Never/Ever], gender identity-related discrimination [Never/Ever]; depression assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] 33 [None or mild scored 0-10/Moderate or severe scored 11-27] and anxiety assessed by the 2-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale [No, scored 0-2/Yes scored 3 or above]. 34

Statistical analyses

We conducted the analyses using R 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025). Participants’ characteristics were first summarized as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) or means ± standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Participant observations with missing primary outcomes or>20% missingness in the independent variables were excluded from the analyses. Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were used to examine bivariate associations between each independent variable and each outcome, with recruitment settings (ART Clinic A, ART Clinic B, and community organizations) included as random effects to adjust for differences at the recruitment setting level. mHealth experience variables were included as covariates in the corresponding models assessing perceived comfort outcomes. Additional analyses were conducted, adjusting for age and education, to account for independent variables other than sociodemographic factors. Independent variables that showed significant associations in the bivariate analyses were subsequently entered into multivariable GLMMs. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation (MI), implemented with the mice package 35 in R, with 5 imputed datasets and 100 iterations. All models were rerun in a sensitivity analysis using listwise deletion for missing data and logistic regression with recruitment settings as a fixed effect. Given the exploratory nature of the study, a significance level of 0.1 was set, and both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted p-values were reported.

Ethical procedures

The protocol received IRB approval from the University of Panama Bioethics Committee (Number CBUP/231/2024) and Florida State University (Number 00005444).

In terms of substance use history, most participants reported never having used drugs (82.3%). The median years participants had lived with HIV was six years (IQR = 2-12 years). About one-third had experienced an interruption in ART for a month or longer, with more than half of these interruptions occurring over a year ago. Approximately 90% of participants had already had a care appointment (10% were assisting with their first care appointment at the time of recruitment), and two-thirds of those who had already had an appointment had never missed any appointments. Additionally, 32.9% of participants reported experiencing intimate partner violence with their current or most recent partner. Approximately half of the participants reported experiencing everyday discrimination, with 19.1% and 3.2% attributing it to HIV status and gender identity, respectively. Moreover, 16.2% of participants screened positive for depression and 19.5% for anxiety.

Regarding mHealth, 33.6% of the participants had prior experience communicating with healthcare providers via WhatsApp, while 47.7% had received health information through the platform. More than 80% of participants reported feeling comfortable using WhatsApp for both communication and receiving health information.

Comfort of using mHealth

Age was inversely associated with comfort in communicating with providers via WhatsApp (Table 2; OR=0.966,90%CI:0.938-0.996), indicating that older participants, despite having greater prior mHealth use, reported lower comfort with this form of communication. In the complete-case analysis, age was also associated with lower odds of feeling comfortable receiving health information via WhatsApp (Table S1; OR=0.973,90%CI:0.948-0.999). With or without adjustment for age and education, only everyday discrimination was significantly associated with comfort using mHealth among all participants. Specifically, participants who had experienced everyday discrimination reported being more comfortable communicating with providers (OR= 2.324,90%CI:1.202-4.492) and receiving health information (OR=1.713,90%CI:0.910-3.225, p-value=0.096) via WhatsApp. In the complete-case analysis, ART status was also found to be associated with comfort in receiving health information through WhatsApp (Tables S2 and S3), such that participants who had interrupted ART for a month or more were more comfortable receiving health information via WhatsApp (OR=2.126,90%CI:1.072-4.215) compared to those who had never interrupted treatment.

Discussion

This study described prior use and comfort with WhatsApp-based health communication among PLHIV who attend urban ART clinics or community-based organizations in urban Panama. Approximately one-third of participants had experience with health communication via WhatsApp (either with a provider or by receiving health information); more than 80% reported they would feel comfortable receiving health communication via WhatsApp. Although prior use was limited, participants reported high comfort. Our findings are congruent with other research that has shown mHealth tools are acceptable to many PLHIV but not integrated equally across routine HIV care.36,37 Our findings also point to a foundation for the future integration of WhatsApp-based mHealth into clinical HIV care in Panama. 38

Participants who reported more everyday discrimination were more likely to indicate greater comfort outcomes. Several studies have described a similar dynamic in which digital contact can help some people avoid stigma and discrimination in clinical settings.25,26 At the same time, stigma has also been identified as a barrier to mHealth use when privacy on the device cannot be ensured, especially when cellphones are shared or when digital communication could risk involuntary serostatus outing. 27 WhatsApp is a digital tool that was not designed for healthcare communication, and its use in clinical settings occurs in the absence of institutional guidance, regulator standards, formal consent protocols, provider boundaries and data protection. 39 This gap in ethics and regulation is important in HIV care since inadvertent disclosure of HIV status, shared phones, visible messages and notifications and lack of clarity about who answers messages may create risks or friction with providers and patients. Despite these important concerns, WhatsApp is used widely for healthcare communication. Therefore, any WhatsApp-based intervention needs to be accompanied by procedures for informed consent, message content, privacy safeguards and referral pathways for urgent or safety needs.

Older participants had higher odds of communicating with providers or receiving health information via WhatsApp. Other research has similarly found high digital engagement among older adults. 40 However, we also found that older age was inversely associated with comfort. Surprisingly, participants with university-level education were less likely to have used WhatsApp for health communication. As this study did not assess reasons for participants using or not using WhatsApp, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. One hypothesis is that those with higher education may have greater concerns about data security and privacy when discussing health information in WhatsApp. Another hypothesis is that these individuals may have greater access to formal clinic-based communication. Future qualitative work could explore these hypotheses in the future.

We also found several psychosocial factors associated with prior WhatsApp use in unadjusted models. After adjustment, only drug use and retention in care remained significant correlates. Our findings suggest that patients with psychosocial challenges may already use WhatsApp to stay in touch with providers. WhatsApp communication has been used informally in Panama’s HIV services (through USAID funding), though activities were paused in early 2025. 41 Our findings are similar to observations from other settings, where mHealth may support PLHIV managing psychosocial and substance use. 42 Additionally, complete-case analyses revealed that participants with long-term ART interruptions had lower odds of having received information, consistent with global findings that the most disengaged patients could face additional barriers to using digital tools.43,44

HIV-related discrimination was associated with higher odds of provider communication through WhatsApp. Participants who had missed appointments had lower odds of prior WhatsApp communication with providers compared to those who had not yet attended an appointment. Digital communication creates social distance; one possible explanation is that people who have had negative experiences use these means to avoid in-person contact. These findings point to similarities between messaging-based communication and HIV-related discrimination as well as broader patterns of engagement with HIV care. 45 The results raise questions about how WhatsApp-based interventions could support individuals who experience discrimination or are inconsistent with care. Additionally, the results point to the need for further research into the barriers and facilitators.

Whilst this study did not directly assess implementation outcomes for a WhatsApp-based mHealth intervention, the combination of high comfort and modest levels of past WhatsApp use for health communication resembles patterns reported in other regions that have incorporated digital health interventions into HIV programs. 46 Clinics sometimes rely on mobile apps, including WhatsApp, to stay in touch with patients. In Panama, WhatsApp use is widespread and free, though it relies on data use. Because of these factors, WhatsApp is a realistic option to maintain communication with patients, particularly those who face stigma or other challenges that are able to disrupt routine care. Implementation studies in other settings have shown that messaging tools if used weekly, could support getting and reminding patients of appointments and aid in ART adherence.47–49 Safeguards for confidentiality, data security, and equitable access need to be included if these tools were introduced into care. Additionally, any intervention and should be accompanied by ongoing monitoring to assess their reach, safety, and acceptability.39,50

Policy and program implications

Our findings may inform health programs that seek to adapt their services to digital and mHealth platforms. First, programs should develop guidance on consent, confidentiality, data protection, and adequate boundaries for WhatsApp-based communication, particularly given concerns with respect to privacy and the use of shared devices.39,51 Second, providers need training to use digital communication in stigma-informed ways, as individuals who experienced discrimination demonstrated greater comfort with WhatsApp contact.52,53 Third, there should not be an assumption that WhatsApp communication will automatically reach patients with poorer retention. In our study, participants who missed appointments had lower odds of prior WhatsApp communication with providers. Therefore, programs that are considering WhatsApp-based communication should proactively assess if patients who are at risk of missed visits are able and willing to use WhatsApp, and able to do so safely and confidentially. Lastly, all mHealth intervention programs should monitor using paradata (e.g., read receipts) to track who is reached and who engages (and who doesn’t), and assess whether the application is acceptable across age groups, educational levels, and connectivity levels.14,54 These steps, when taken together, could help position WhatsApp as a tool that complements health services. Additionally, it could help sustain patient engagement when face-to-face appointments are irregular, or if resources are limited or fluctuating. 55

Limitations

This study used a cross-sectional design; therefore, we are unable to make causal conclusions from our data. All measures were self-reported; consequently, they were subject to recall or social desirability bias. Additionally, outcomes that described comfort were dichotomized to distinguish participants who affirmatively agreed with comfort using WhatsApp from those who were neutral or disagreed. Therefore, the approach may obscure meaningful differences between uncertainty and discomfort; therefore, this should be considered when interpreting the comfort outcome. The study population attended ART clinics in urban areas of Panama City and Colón, and in community organizations, therefore the population may not be generalizable across Panama or LAC. Additionally, nine participants were recruited from community organizations. Therefore, findings from this subgroup should not be interpreted separately; furthermore, the study was not powered to compare where participants were recruited from. Lastly, after applying FDR correction to control for multiple testing, no associations remained significant after FDR correction. The sample size was calculated for the parent study’s primary outcome of depression; therefore, the study may have had limited statistical power to detect associations with outcomes of prior WhatsApp use and comfort outcomes. The lack of FDR-adjusted significance likely reflects the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of the analyses, rather than the absence of true associations.

Furthermore, the unadjusted associations we identified can be interpreted as preliminary signals highlighting specific social-behavioral factors and are important to prioritize in future research. Despite these limitations, this study offers an initial description of mHealth use and comfort in a Central American country, where such data are scarce. We were able to include participants of diverse social and clinical characteristics. Therefore, this research describes how digital communication behaviors intersect with stigma, mental health, and care.

Conclusions

Experiences of discrimination and several behavioral factors were associated with prior WhatsApp-based health communication among PLHIV in Panama. These include recent drug use, HIV-related discrimination, and missed appointments. Everyday discrimination was associated with greater comfort using WhatsApp for health communication. Our results describe WhatsApp could be a cost-effective platform for interventions designed to keep patients connected to clinics, especially those who experience discrimination or have had breaks in care. Future research is recommended to better understand how WhatsApp can overcome barriers to care and to guide the safe and equitable integration of mHealth tools into HIV care in Panama.
