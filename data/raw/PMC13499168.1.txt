Oropharyngeal impalement injuries in children are uncommon but potentially life-threatening, due to the proximity of vital neurovascular structures and the risk of airway obstruction. We report the case of a child who sustained an impalement injury from a reinforcement bar that penetrated the oropharynx, traversed the parapharyngeal space and exited near the preauricular region. Given the potential for disastrous complications, such as carotid artery blowout or need for surgical airway, the patient was managed by a multidisciplinary team including experienced anaesthetists, ENT, and vascular surgeons. Awake fibreoptic nasotracheal intubation was performed, followed by surgical removal of the object and primary repair of the oropharyngeal laceration under general anaesthesia. Despite the fact that the patient developed a transient grade III facial nerve palsy, which resolved over six months, no major structures were damaged. This case highlights the importance of early multidisciplinary involvement, advanced airway planning and awareness of neurovascular complications when approaching oropharyngeal trauma.

Introduction

Injuries to the oral cavity and oropharynx are relatively common in young children, typically as a consequence of accidental falls while carrying objects in the mouth. Most cases involve superficial trauma to the soft palate or oral mucosa and resolve without complication. Impalement injuries with deeper trajectories, however, pose a risk to critical neurovascular structures, including the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, cranial nerves IX–XII, the facial and mandibular nerves, and the temporomandibular joint, and present a significant management challenge.

To the best of our knowledge, no previously published case has described a transoral impalement injury with the exact trajectory and anatomical extent of the present case, successfully managed and without major complications. Case presentation

A previously healthy boy in his early school years was brought to the Emergency Department after sustaining a transoral impalement injury while playing unsupervised at a construction site near his home. According to his caregiver, he had fallen onto an upright steel reinforcement bar. Emergency medical services were not contacted; the child was transported directly to hospital by a family member.

On arrival, the patient was alert, his oxygen saturation was normal on room air, and haemodynamically stable. There was no stridor or evidence of airway obstruction, although he was hyperventilating and visibly distressed. He was conscious but unable to communicate. Clinical examination revealed a steel bar approximately 3 cm in diameter penetrating the right lateral oropharyngeal wall, without active bleeding. The distal end of the bar was visible externally, protruding in the right preauricular region, just anterior to the tragus, with no associated swelling, pulsatility or signs of haematoma (Fig. 2).Fig. 2 Left: Detail of the preauricular exit wound of the penetrating object. Right: Appearance following surgical debridement and placement of a preauricular drain.

Neurological examination revealed grade IV peripheral facial nerve paralysis on the right side, according to the House–Brackmann scale. There was no cervical subcutaneous emphysema, and no tenderness was elicited on palpation of the neck. The patient was unable to perform any mandibular movements.

The child had no previous medical conditions, was fully vaccinated (including tetanus), and had no relevant surgical or trauma history. There was no family history of bleeding or clotting disorders.

The trajectory of the foreign body, combined with the anatomical location of the entry and exit points, raised immediate concern for possible injury to the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, facial nerve, retromandibular vein, and temporomandibular joint. Despite the absence of active bleeding or additional neurological signs beyond the facial nerve palsy, the depth and lateral direction of the injury placed these structures at significant risk (Fig. 3).Fig. 3 Axial computed tomography (CT) image of the neck demonstrating soft tissue disruption in the right parapharyngeal space (PPS) and masticator space (MS). A passive surgical drain is visible in the right preauricular region (Drain). No secondary signs of vascular injury are observed.

Fig. 3

Given the precarious position of the foreign object and the high risk of airway compromise if it were displaced or manipulated, no further intraoral examination or bedside imaging was attempted. The immediate priority was to secure the airway under controlled conditions, and the otolaryngology, anesthesiology and surgical teams were assembled promptly to facilitate coordinated operative management.

Investigations

Upon arrival, the patient underwent basic laboratory investigations to establish baseline status and anaesthetic fitness. No radiological imaging—such as plain radiographs, computed tomography (CT), or CT angiography—was performed prior to surgery. This decision was based on several clinical factors: the patient was haemodynamically stable, showed no evidence of active bleeding, and there were no signs of neurological deterioration apart from the facial nerve palsy. More importantly, metal objects create significant artifacts in CT imaging making it difficult to interpret the images. Also, position and trajectory of the impaled object raised concerns about possible displacement during imaging, which could have precipitated severe airway compromise or vascular injury.

The decision to proceed directly to the operating theatre for airway management and surgical exploration was taken by consensus within the multidisciplinary team. Postoperatively, a tomography (CT) scan of the head and neck was obtained to evaluate the extent of soft tissue injury and to exclude delayed vascular complications (Fig. 4).Fig. 4 Coronal computed tomography (CT) image of the neck showing the trajectory of the penetrating injury, with soft tissue disruption in the right parapharyngeal space (PPS) and masticator space (MS). Differential diagnosis

The immediate diagnostic focus centred on evaluating potential damage to vascular, neural, articular, and glandular structures.

Vascular injury, particularly to the internal carotid artery or external carotid branches, was a primary concern. Although there were no signs of active hemorrhage or haematoma at presentation, the risk of hemorrhage during foreign body removal, of delayed arterial thrombosis or pseudoaneurysm justified postoperative imaging. CT confirmed no vascular events.

Cranial nerve injury was also suspected, particularly involving the facial nerve, given the exit site near the stylomastoid foramen and parotid region. This was clinically substantiated by a right-sided grade IV peripheral facial nerve palsy at presentation. Clinical signs suggesting involvement of the lower cranial nerves (IX–XII), or mandibular nerve, such as face hypoesthesia, dysphagia, dysphonia, or tongue deviation were excluded.

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) involvement was considered, as the patient's significant trismus and inability to perform mandibular movements were evident upon arrival. This was likely due to local oedema and involvement of the masticator space musculature, since bony injury was not evident on imaging. Functional recovery confirmed the absence of structural joint damage.

Parotid gland injury was presumed based on the anatomical route and later confirmed by postoperative CT, which showed disruption of the glandular tissue. No sialocele or salivary fistula developed, likely due to early drainage and appropriate surgical repair.

Infectious complications such as deep neck space infection or abscess formation were considered a potential delayed risk, especially with parapharyngeal involvement. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered perioperatively, and the absence of fever, swelling, or elevated inflammatory markers during follow-up excluded evolving infection.

The final diagnosis was established through intraoperative findings, postoperative imaging, and clinical progression, confirming a penetrating oropharyngeal injury with soft tissue involvement of the parapharyngeal and masticator spaces, associated with transient facial nerve palsy and preserved vascular and articular structures.

Management

Initial management focused on airway protection. Given the risk of displacement and potential vascular or airway compromise, the patient underwent awake fibreoptic nasotracheal intubation under topical anaesthesia and light sedation. General anaesthesia was then induced for surgical exploration by a multidisciplinary team combining ENT and vascular surgeons.

The steel bar was removed via its original transoral trajectory without the need for external incision. The oropharyngeal mucosal laceration was irrigated and closed primarily with absorbable sutures. A passive Penrose-type drain was placed at the preauricular exit site to monitor for salivary leakage or haematoma.

Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics were initiated with intravenous amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (50/5 mg/kg every 8 h) and continued for seven days. Analgesia was maintained with paracetamol and ibuprofen.

Due to the onset of grade IV peripheral facial nerve palsy, oral corticosteroid therapy was started on postoperative day one with prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) for seven days, followed by a tapering course over the subsequent week. The patient was referred for outpatient physiotherapy to support neuromuscular recovery.

Outcome and follow-up

The child remained clinically stable throughout the postoperative period. Enteral nutrition was initially withheld due to the risk of wound dehiscence and infection. A soft diet was introduced on postoperative day five, following confirmation of satisfactory mucosal healing. Tolerance was good, and the patient progressed to a normal diet by day eight. He was discharged home on day ten, afebrile, tolerating oral intake, and with no signs of infection, haematoma, or salivary leakage.

At discharge, right-sided grade III peripheral facial nerve palsy persisted, along with mild trismus. Oral corticosteroids were tapered, and physiotherapy was initiated. Mandibular mobility improved within the first postoperative week, and facial function began to recover progressively.

The patient was followed closely in outpatient consultations—weekly during the first month and then monthly. By the end of six months, facial nerve function had fully normalized, and mandibular range of motion was restored. The patient resumed all daily activities without limitations or long-term sequelae. No further imaging or intervention was required. He remains well at the time of final review.

Discussion

Oropharyngeal impalement injuries in children are relatively common [1], [2]. Even though most of this type of injuries are superficial and heal uneventfully when the trajectory extends laterally into the parapharyngeal or masticator spaces, the risk of damage to vital structures increases substantially [3], [4], [5].

Traumatic penetration of this area may result in vascular injury, haematoma, cranial neuropathies or even airway compromise. Although the incidence of stroke following such injuries is low (estimated at 0.2–0.3%) [6], delayed complications such as internal carotid artery thrombosis or dissection have been reported, often presenting hours to days after the initial trauma [7], [8].

In our case, despite the high-risk anatomical pathway of the impalement, no vascular injury occurred. This may be attributed to the foreign body displacing, rather than lacerating, critical structures. Clinical stability, the absence of neurological deterioration, and the fact that metal artifacts severely degrade CT image quality supported the decision to defer preoperative imaging and prioritise airway control, a decision echoed in prior reports [9], [10]. Although CT angiography is often recommended in deep penetrating oropharyngeal trauma, especially with lateral trajectories [11], its utility remains debated in haemodynamically stable, neurologically intact children without expanding haematoma or signs of vascular compromise [12].

The presence of a peripheral facial nerve palsy at presentation raised concern for direct neural compression or contusion, likely at the stylomastoid foramen or within the parotid gland. Similar cases are sparsely documented, with most facial nerve injuries in this context resulting from iatrogenic trauma or temporal bone fractures [13], [14]. Our case may be one of the few in the literature in which traumatic peripheral facial nerve palsy occurred following transoral impalement and recovered completely under conservative management, including corticosteroids and physiotherapy.

Additionally, the temporomandibular joint was functionally impaired at admission, likely due to local oedema and muscle laceration, as no fracture or dislocation was detected. Recovery of mandibular mobility occurred within one week, consistent with a soft tissue mechanism.

Multidisciplinary intervention was key to the favourable outcome. The decision to perform awake fibreoptic intubation avoided displacement of the foreign body and ensured controlled airway management—a strategy supported in similar high-risk cases [15], [16]. Surgical removal via the oropharynx avoided further dissection and reduced morbidity. Prophylactic antibiotics, surgical drainage and steroid therapy further contributed to the resolution of inflammation and prevention of secondary complications, such as salivary fistula or deep neck infection [17].

To the best of our knowledge, no previously published case has described a penetrating transoral impalement with this anatomical trajectory and degree of facial nerve involvement that was successfully managed without major surgical intervention or long-term sequelae. This case reinforces the importance of anatomical understanding, clinical vigilance, and timely, coordinated multidisciplinary care in managing pediatric oropharyngeal trauma.

Conclusion

Penetrating oropharyngeal trauma in children requires a high index of suspicion for injury to critical neurovascular structures, including the carotid artery, internal jugular vein, retromandibular vein, facial nerve, and temporomandibular joint.

Awake fibreoptic intubation is a key airway management strategy in anatomically complex oropharyngeal injuries, allowing controlled access while minimising the risk of foreign body displacement.

Facial nerve palsy may occur in penetrating lateral oropharyngeal trauma due to direct nerve compression or contusion. Conservative management, including corticosteroids and physiotherapy, can lead to full recovery.

A multidisciplinary approach—combining ENT, anaesthesiology, maxillofacial and vascular surgical teams—is critical to optimise outcomes and avoid morbidity in high-risk transoral impalement injuries.
