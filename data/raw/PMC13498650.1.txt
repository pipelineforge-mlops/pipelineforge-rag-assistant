Purpose

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is associated with elevated risks of osteoporosis and fractures, but the contribution of diabetic microvascular complications remains poorly defined—especially in Asian populations. Prior studies have suggested links between diabetes-related complications and skeletal fragility, but most were limited by small sample sizes, cross-sectional designs, or inadequate adjustment for key confounders such as medication use, fall history, and comorbidities.

Methods

Using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database, we conducted a large-scale, population-based cohort study of individuals newly diagnosed with T2DM (2008–2020) to assess associations between microvascular complications and the risk of new-onset osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models.

Results

In this East Asian cohort, patients with diabetic neuropathy (DN), retinopathy (DR), or kidney disease (DKD) exhibited a graded increase in the risk of both osteoporosis and major osteoporotic fractures, including those of the hip, spine, humerus, and radius/ulna. Compared to patients without microvascular disease, those with DR and DN had significantly higher risks of osteoporosis (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 1.14 [95% CI: 1.09–1.19] and 1.25 [1.22–1.28], respectively; p < 0.001). Furthermore, amongst the microvascular complications, DN showed the strongest and most consistent associations with osteoporosis and spine fractures.

Conclusions

This nationwide cohort focused exclusively on newly diagnosed T2DM patients and comprehensively adjusted for confounders. Our findings suggest that microvascular complications may serve as independent predictors of skeletal fragility and support their incorporation into fracture risk assessment models, particularly for Asian individuals with T2DM.

Introduction

Fracture risk in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasingly recognized as a clinically important yet underappreciated complication. Despite patients with T2DM often presenting with normal or even elevated bone mineral density (BMD), numerous epidemiologic studies have consistently shown a higher incidence of fragility fractures—particularly of the hip, vertebrae, and distal radius—compared to non-diabetic individuals [1–3]. This apparent paradox has drawn attention to alterations in bone quality, remodeling dynamics, and microarchitecture that are not fully captured by BMD alone.

Among the proposed contributors to skeletal fragility in T2DM, diabetic microvascular complications—such as nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy—have garnered growing interest [4]. These complications, which reflect chronic microvascular damage secondary to prolonged hyperglycemia, may influence bone health through several mechanisms [5]. Impaired renal handling of calcium and phosphate, reduced vascular supply to bone, chronic low-grade inflammation, and diminished neuromuscular coordination are all plausible pathways linking microvascular disease to osteoporosis and fractures [4, 5]. In addition, microvascular burden may serve as a marker of systemic end-organ dysfunction and frailty, yet its direct, independent impact on skeletal outcomes remains incompletely understood [4].

Previous studies examining the relationship between microvascular complications and fracture risk have been limited by cross-sectional designs, small sample sizes, or insufficient adjustment for relevant confounders such as fall history, antidiabetic medication use, and other comorbidities [3, 6–9]. Moreover, few studies have attempted to disentangle the relative contributions of individual microvascular complications or assess the potential dose-dependent-like relationship between cumulative vascular burden and skeletal outcomes. To address these gaps, we conducted a population-based cohort study using the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), a comprehensive longitudinal dataset encompassing healthcare data from over 99% of the Taiwanese population. We identified individuals with newly diagnosed T2DM and examined the association between microvascular complications—specifically diabetic kidney disease, retinopathy, and neuropathy—and subsequent development of osteoporosis and major osteoporotic fractures (MOF). By adjusting for a broad set of clinical covariates and matching for known fracture risk factors, we aimed to isolate the independent and cumulative skeletal effects of microvascular disease.

Diabetic microvascular complications are increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of bone fragility [5, 6]. In the field of osteoporosis research, microvascular complications in patients with diabetes have traditionally been considered a risk factor for osteoporosis [1, 4]. However, recent supporting data are lacking. Additionally, diabetes mellitus itself has been proposed as an independent risk factor for osteoporosis. Given the high prevalence of DM, an important clinical question arises: how can we effectively screen for osteoporosis among this large population? Utilizing microvascular complications as a clinical indicator may represent a high-risk approach for targeted screening. The present study aims to provide empirical evidence to support the use of microvascular complications as a rational clinical marker for osteoporosis risk stratification in patients with diabetes. Our findings have potential implications for refining fracture risk stratification in T2DM, guiding targeted screening protocols, and informing preventative strategies in endocrinology, primary care, and orthopedic practice.

Methods

Study population and data source

Taiwan initiated its National Health Insurance (NHI) program in 1995, reaching nearly universal coverage by 2000; it currently includes approximately 99% of the population (~ 23 million individuals). The National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) compiles comprehensive patient-level data from this program, encompassing demographic characteristics, diagnostic codes, treatment procedures, and hospitalizations, all classified according to ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM systems. The database is also cross-referenced with the National Death Registry to ensure accurate validation of death causes. For this analysis, we utilized anonymized and encrypted NHIRD data to maintain confidentiality of patients and healthcare providers [10]. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of China Medical University and Hospital (approval number CMUH110-REC1-038(CR-4)), which waived informed consent given the de-identified nature of the data.

Study procedures

This study identified and followed patients newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2020, tracking their clinical outcomes until December 31, 2021. T2DM diagnoses were determined based on ICD codes (Table S1), requiring either a minimum of three outpatient visits within one year or at least one inpatient admission, an approach previously validated in Taiwan with an accuracy of 93.3% [11]. To establish a clearly defined cohort, patients were excluded if they had incomplete data within one year after diagnosis, any pre-existing diabetic kidney disease (DKD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), or diabetic neuropathy (DN) prior to their T2DM diagnosis, an index date falling before 2008 or after 2020, type 1 diabetes, age younger than 50 years, occurrence of outcome events before the index date, or less than 180 days of follow-up after diagnosis. Patients with diagnosis of pathological fractures or trauma patients who suffered from traffic accidents were also excluded based on ICD codes (Table S1).

During the first year following T2DM diagnosis, patients were categorized based on the presence or absence of microvascular complications. Any events occurring beyond this initial year were excluded from the analysis. Microvascular complications identified within the first year were treated as the exposure factors to assess their potential influence on the subsequent risk of osteoporosis and fractures. Patients without complications formed the non-microvascular disease group, while those diagnosed with complications were further divided into subgroups: (1) patients with a single microvascular complication—DKD (including diabetic and hypertensive nephropathy, proteinuria, chronic kidney disease, dialysis, renal failure, and renal replacement therapy), DR (background and proliferative retinopathy, retinal edema and detachment, macular degeneration, vitreous hemorrhage, and vision impairment), or DN (mononeuropathy, polyneuropathy, neuralgia, cranial and peripheral nerve palsies, autonomic neuropathy, and Charcot’s disease); (2) patients with two microvascular complications; and (3) patients with all three microvascular complications. For consistency in follow-up periods and outcome analyses, the index date was standardized as the 366th day after T2DM diagnosis, ensuring a complete one-year window to evaluate microvascular complications prior to examining outcomes such as osteoporosis and major osteoporotic fractures.

Baseline characteristics and medications

This study considered numerous potential confounding factors at baseline, including demographic variables (gender, age), lifestyle factors (obesity, smoking status, and alcohol-related disorders), and significant comorbid conditions such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, peripheral arterial disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), liver cirrhosis, and connective tissue disorders. Other important conditions accounted for were history of falls, family history of osteoporosis, cancers, psychosis, depression, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, acromegaly, hypogonadism, and malabsorption, along with the overall health burden assessed by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [12]. Baseline medication usage was also documented, including oral antidiabetic drugs, thiazolidinediones, the number of oral antidiabetic drugs, insulin therapy, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), the type and number of hypertensive drugs, aspirin, statins, and corticosteroid treatments.

Main outcomes of interest

This study investigated the incidence of newly diagnosed osteoporosis, major osteoporotic fractures—defined as a composite of hip, vertebral (spinal), humeral, and radioulnar fractures—as well as the individual incidence rates of each fracture type. Outcomes were identified using ICD codes, requiring confirmation through either at least two outpatient diagnoses within one year or a single hospitalization, further validated by corresponding medication records (Table S1) [13–15]. Patients were monitored from the index date until the first occurrence of any of these specified outcomes, death, or the study’s conclusion on December 31, 2021, whichever occurred earliest.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate baseline characteristics and compare outcomes across study groups. Differences in continuous variables, such as mean age, were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while categorical variables comparing patients with and without microvascular complications were assessed via chi-squared tests. To control for confounding variables, a 1:1 propensity score matching procedure was performed, pairing individuals without microvascular complications to those with varying combinations of diabetic kidney disease, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic neuropathy (Tables S2–S5). To control for confounding, propensity scores were generated via a non-parsimonious multivariable logistic regression model incorporating all relevant covariates. Matched pairs were then created using the nearest-neighbor matching approach with a caliper of less than 0.01, achieving covariate balance across exposure groups. Matching considered enrolled year, baseline demographics, comorbid conditions, and medication use, aiming for a standardized mean difference of less than 0.1 to indicate balanced matched groups.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to estimate outcome risks, adjusting for critical factors including age, gender, obesity, smoking status, history of falls, pre-existing comorbidities, and medication usage. The proportional hazards assumption was validated through proportional hazards testing and examination of Schoenfeld residuals. Hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were calculated to measure risk differences. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were created to illustrate cumulative incidence patterns for osteoporosis and fracture outcomes among the groups. We conducted an additional analysis examining the interval between the diagnosis of microvascular complications and the occurrence of subsequent osteoporosis or fractures. We additionally presented the baseline characteristics of individuals who experienced outcomes prior to the index date alongside those of the final study cohort, to assess potential differences between these two populations and to strengthen the generalizability of our findings.

A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All statistical computations were performed using SAS version 9.4 and RStudio version 4.4.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Within the first year following a T2DM diagnosis, we assessed the prevalence of microvascular complications among patients. From 2008 to 2021, a total of 3,501,112 patients with newly diagnosed T2DM were identified. After applying exclusion criteria, we removed 213,893 patients lacking sufficient follow-up data within the first year after diagnosis, 609,064 with pre-existing diabetic kidney disease (DKD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), or diabetic neuropathy (DN), 92,524 diagnosed outside the study period (2008–2020), 124,695 patients diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 562,275 patients younger than 50 years old, 164,348 patients who experienced outcomes prior to the index date, and 44,831 patients who died or had a follow-up duration shorter than 180 days post-index. Following these exclusions, our final cohort consisted of 1,689,482 individuals, including 1,260,137 without any microvascular complications and 429,345 with at least one microvascular complication (Fig. S1).

To minimize confounding effects, we conducted a 1:1 propensity score matching based on demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and medication use. This resulted in 416,410 matched pairs between microvascular and non-microvascular groups (Fig. S1). Within the microvascular complication group, further matching produced 360,635 patients with one complication, 50,831 with two complications, and 4,944 with three complications (Fig. S1, Tables S2–S4). Before matching, patients with microvascular complications used more glucose-lowering agents as well as antihypertensive and other cardiovascular medications than those without microvascular complications (Tables S2–S4). They were also slightly older and had a higher prevalence of prior hypoglycemia (Table S3). After propensity score matching, baseline characteristics between these two groups were well balanced (SMD < 0.1) (Tables S2–S3). The only notable residual differences were observed among patients with three microvascular complications, who were younger, had lower prevalence of COPD and dementia, and were less likely to use metformin, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, and 2–3 oral antidiabetic agents, but were more likely to use 0–1 oral antidiabetic agents, insulin, and corticosteroids compared with those without microvascular complications (Table S4).The mean follow-up period for the matched cohort was approximately 7.88 years.

Main outcomes

Number of microvascular complications versus no microvascular complications

Our analysis revealed a dose-dependent-like association between the number of microvascular complications and the risk of osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures. Specifically, the adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) for developing osteoporosis increased progressively from 1.12 (95% CI: 1.10–1.14) in patients with one complication, to 1.31 (95% CI: 1.26–1.37) with two complications, and 1.37 (95% CI: 1.19–1.57) with three complications. Similarly, the risk of major osteoporotic fractures increased in a graded manner, from an aHR of 1.11 (95% CI: 1.10–1.13) with one complication to 1.29 (95% CI: 1.25–1.33) with two complications and 1.46 (95% CI: 1.31–1.62) with three complications (Table 1). Stratified analyses demonstrated consistent graded associations for hip, spine, humeral, and radioulnar fractures (Table 1). Kaplan-Meier plots demonstrated progressively higher cumulative incidence of osteoporosis and major osteoporotic fractures with increasing numbers of microvascular complications (Fig. 1, Fig. S2).

Type of microvascular complications versus no microvascular complications

To further examine specific microvascular diseases and their individual impacts on osteoporosis and fracture risks, additional 1:1 propensity matching was performed comparing individuals without complications to those with only DKD (N = 173,895), only DR (N = 43,951), or only DN (N = 149,406) (Tables S5–S7). Among these subgroups, diabetic neuropathy showed the highest aHRs for osteoporosis (1.25; 95% CI: 1.22–1.28), major osteoporotic fractures (1.14; 95% CI: 1.12–1.16), spine fractures (1.20; 95% CI: 1.16–1.23), humeral fractures (1.14; 95% CI: 1.09–1.19), and radioulnar fractures (1.08; 95% CI: 1.04–1.12) compared to those without any microvascular complications (Table 2). However, diabetic retinopathy was associated with the highest risk specifically for hip fractures (aHR 1.16; 95% CI: 1.09–1.22) compared to DKD and DN. Kaplan-Meier analyses confirmed that patients with only diabetic neuropathy had significantly higher cumulative incidence of osteoporosis, major osteoporotic fractures, spine, and radioulnar fractures (log-rank p < 0.001). In contrast, DKD did not significantly elevate the cumulative incidence of fractures except for hip fractures and was associated with lower cumulative incidence rates of osteoporosis and spine fractures compared to patients without microvascular complications (Fig. S3).

Additional analyses

Interval from microvascular complication diagnosis to osteoporosis/fracture

The mean (SD) intervals from the diagnosis of one, two, and three microvascular complications to the development of osteoporosis were 5.59 (3.28), 5.24 (3.25), and 5.16 (3.32) years, respectively. Correspondingly, the mean (SD) intervals from the diagnosis of one, two, and three microvascular complications to the onset of major osteoporotic fractures were 6.21 (3.40), 5.87 (3.39), and 5.45 (3.30) years, respectively. Notably, the intervals observed in patients with two or three microvascular complications were significantly shorter than those with a single complication (Tables S11).

Characteristics of patients with outcomes prior to the index date and those in the final study cohort

Compared with individuals in the final study cohort, patients who experienced outcomes prior to the index date had a higher proportion of females, were older, and had a greater burden of comorbidities (Table S12). They were less likely to use metformin or sulfonylureas. In terms of antidiabetic therapy, they more frequently received 0–1, instead of 2–3, oral glucose-lowering agents. Additionally, these patients were more likely to use insulin, antihypertensive medications, aspirin, and corticosteroids.

Discussion

The findings of this large population-based study demonstrate a significant graded association between the number of microvascular complications and the risk of developing osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures among patients newly diagnosed with T2DM. Notably, patients with diabetic neuropathy exhibited the highest risk for osteoporosis and specific fracture types compared to those with diabetic retinopathy or diabetic kidney disease.

These results are consistent with prior studies demonstrating an increased fracture risk among individuals with microvascular diabetic complications [16–20]. Notably, our study further identifies a significant graded association between the number of microvascular complications and the risk of developing osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures in patients with newly diagnosed T2DM. A greater burden of microvascular complications likely reflects poorer gluco-metabolic control and a higher susceptibility to further vascular damage. This condition is plausibly associated with the accumulation of advanced glycation end products within the bone matrix, microvascular impairment, and a proinflammatory milieu, all of which adversely affect bone cell function and compromise bone microdamage repair [21]. Moreover, the coexistence of multiple microvascular complications may promote increased generation of reactive oxygen species and more extensive vascular injury [22–24]. These processes can lead to widespread endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and vasoconstriction, ultimately reducing bone vascularization, impairing blood flow and oxygen delivery, and disrupting normal bone remodeling processes [2, 25–27].

Our study provides novel insight by differentiating risks associated with individual microvascular complications. Notably, fracture risk was significantly higher in patients with diabetic neuropathy than with diabetic retinopathy or diabetic kidney disease, despite the kidneys’ central role in calcium and phosphate homeostasis and bone metabolism. Previous studies in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus have also demonstrated that diabetic neuropathy is associated with increased cortical bone porosity and poorer performance on physical function tests [28]. Moreover, the presence of neurological complications was linked to a markedly elevated risk of hip fracture, with an estimated 7.3-fold increase compared to those without microvascular complications [29]. Several neuropathy-specific mechanisms could explain this observation. First, diabetic neuropathy is known to cause impaired sensory and motor function, altered gait patterns, and muscle weakness, potentially exacerbating fracture risk independent of fall history, which we have controlled for in our propensity matching [30]. Neuropathy may also directly impact bone health through sympathetic denervation, which negatively affects bone remodeling processes, leading to reduced bone density and impaired bone quality [31–33]. Treatment of neuropathic pain may further exacerbate fracture risk, as commonly used agents such as tricyclic antidepressants and opioids increase fall risk via side effects including orthostatic hypotension, sedation and impaired coordination [34].

In contrast, despite the kidneys’ established role in mineral metabolism, diabetic kidney disease did not show the highest association with fracture risk in our analysis. This could be partly explained by stringent exclusion criteria, removing individuals with advanced kidney disease, potentially reducing the observed impact. Furthermore, early-stage renal dysfunction is often aggressively managed with calcium-phosphate regulating therapies, likely diminishing its association with fracture risk. It could also be due to the use of ICD codes rather than laboratory data to define diabetic kidney disease, which could result in a relatively imprecise method for accurately identifying the condition.

Our study’s robust design, including an extensive Asian population-based cohort, substantial sample size, and comprehensive adjustments for numerous confounding factors such as demographics, medication use, comorbidities, and history of falls, significantly strengthens its validity. Although the observed hazard ratios are modest, especially for those with one microvascular complication, the consistent graded association across increasing microvascular burden suggests a cumulative effect that may be clinically meaningful at the population level, particularly given the high prevalence of diabetes. This comprehensive approach improves the generalizability of findings and highlights critical differences relevant to Asian populations, which remain underrepresented in osteoporosis research.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the lack of bone mineral density measurements and fracture risk assessments, such as the FRAX score, precluded direct evaluation of osteoporosis severity and individual fracture risk. Second, DKD, DR, and DN were identified using ICD codes; reliance on administrative diagnostic codes and medication records may have introduced misclassification bias. Finally, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as information on lifestyle factors, physical activity, nutritional status, vitamin D deficiency, and diabetes-related indicators (e.g., HbA1c) was unavailable. These factors may independently influence bone metabolism and fracture risk [21]. To address this, we attempted to use surrogate indicators of glycemic control, including the type and number of oral antidiabetic agents as well as the use of injectable therapies such as GLP-1 RAs and insulin, for matching and model adjustment. This approach was intended to maximize comparability between the study and control groups and thereby strengthen the validity of our findings. Furthermore, the impact of diabetes duration on osteoporosis and fracture risk could not be evaluated in this study. Because only patients with newly diagnosed diabetes were included, all participants had a uniform diabetes duration of approximately one year at baseline.

Our findings suggest that microvascular complications—particularly diabetic neuropathy—may contribute to an increased risk of osteoporosis and fractures in patients with T2DM. Clinicians should recognize DN as a significant independent risk factor and prioritize early preventive interventions to mitigate future osteoporosis and fracture risks. Further studies incorporating direct bone health assessments and additional metabolic parameters are essential to refine and validate these recommendations.
