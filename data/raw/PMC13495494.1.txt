Diagnostic sensor development and clinical translation lag, in part, due to a lack of rapid, high-throughput screening methodologies. Optimization of high-throughput sensor development and deployment will likely help in expediting tools toward the clinic. To address this issue, we optimized high-throughput screening parameters using a near-infrared (NIR-II) plate reader attached to an external probe for in vivo testing. We assessed spectroscopy parameters to improve the speed and precision in screening a single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)-based optical sensor. To do so, we assessed the appropriate well-plate specifications, including laser power, excitation wavelength, exposure time, and focal height parameters for SWCNT-based optical sensor development. We also used the plate reader to screen fluorescent SWCNTs that were endocytosed by a macrophage cell line. We then performed NIR probe spectroscopy to assess SWCNTs embedded within a methylcellulose hydrogel. Finally, we used the NIR probe to measure the SWCNT center wavelength and intensity from live immunocompetent mice. We anticipate that this framework may be broadly applicable to the development of near-infrared nanosensors with the potential for more rapid clinical diagnostic translation.

Introduction

Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy is a valuable tool for biomedical applications, including noninvasive deep-tissue imaging and nondestructive chemical analysis of biofluid samples. , Alternative clinical diagnostic methods have significant disadvantages in that many are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and costly. Imaging modalities such as MRI, CT, and PET have notable drawbacks, including low temporal resolution, exposure to ionizing radiation, and incompatibility with point-of-care or bedside diagnostics. The NIR-II (also called short-wave infrared, or SWIR) region of the electromagnetic spectrum (1000–1700 nm), commonly referred to as the biological tissue transparency window, is especially attractive for supplementary diagnostic purposes through biological imaging. This region of the electromagnetic spectrum exhibits high tissue penetration depth with minimal water and photon absorption, along with minimal tissue autofluorescence and light scattering. − In most cases, an NIR fluorophore is necessary to transduce information. Applications of NIR-II spectroscopy include quantifying the contents of biofluids, measuring oxygen levels in blood, and biological imaging. Some more recent applications include tumor delineation, vascular imaging, photothermal/photodynamic therapy, and drug delivery. Further, NIR spectroscopy is a straightforward method in comparison to other modalities such as Raman spectroscopy, which requires more training and technical expertise.

Many studies have sought to expand the library of NIR fluorescent probes for diagnostic purposes and in vivo imaging. NIR-I fluorescent dyes (700–1000 nm emission) such as methylene blue and indocyanine green are the only two NIR dyes approved by the FDA. Each has been used in several clinical applications, including breast cancer diagnosis, lymphatic mapping, and visualization of tissue perfusion. , Many NIR-I organic dyes, such as rhodamine and squaraine, exhibit photobleaching, rapid degradation, or off-target uptake in vivo. , Beyond traditional organic fluorophores, a prominent example of a NIR fluorophore is quantum dots (QDs), which have substantial potential for in vivo and in vitro applications. Rare-earth-doped nanoparticles (RENPs) are another type of probe that have significant advantages in imaging, including a large Stokes shift, long fluorescence lifetime, and minimal photobleaching, though their cost of production and low materials abundance limit their application. , Engineered NIR fluorescent proteins have similarly not been widely adopted due to low quantum yields.

Semiconducting single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) hold substantial promise as optical transducers for biosensors, both in vitro and in vivo. SWCNTs are a quasi-one-dimensional nanomaterial that exhibits NIR-II fluorescence due to a small bandgap in their electronic structure. , SWCNT fluorescence emission is photostable, nonphotobleaching, and exhibits a large Stokes shift. Further, it is highly sensitive to perturbations in its surrounding electrochemical environment, leading to solvatochromism or changes in brightness in response to external stimuli. SWCNT structures are defined by their (n,m) indices, which describe their chiral angle and diameter. Each (n,m) has a unique electronic structure, leading to a distinct emission wavelength. These optical properties have enabled the design of many SWCNT-based optical sensors for a variety of biological analytes, including small molecules, − macromolecules, proteins, − lipids, enzymes, and nucleic acids. , Furthermore, several SWCNT-based sensors have been used in vivo due to the ability of NIR-II fluorescence to penetrate through biological tissues. There are several ways in which SWCNTs can be implemented for in vivo use with the potential to realize rapid sensing, enhanced personalized treatment, and robust output. ,−

SWCNT-based nanosensor development employs both screening approaches and rational design with known biomolecular recognition elements. The most common example of a screening approach is the corona phase molecular recognition (CoPhMoRe) method. This entails screening a library of organic polymers or DNA sequences that can simultaneously solubilize SWCNT and create binding affinity to a specific analyte. Another example is high-throughput screening coupled with machine learning to ascertain spectral disease signatures. − Both examples require substantial screening of analytes and recognition probes, typically in triplicate, though machine learning-based approaches require many thousands of data points. Rational design of SWCNT-based sensors is also possible, typically using aptamers, antibodies, − , peptides, , or other elements with known binding affinity for the desired target. In these studies, high-throughput sensor analysis is required to iterate through sensor design and evaluate standard sensor characteristics.

It is clear that high-throughput instrumentation is necessary to screen and efficiently develop high-performance nanosensors. Such instrumentation is widely available on the market for visible fluorophores but is only emerging for NIR fluorophores. Further, to make use of the unique properties of SWCNTs to study disease biology or develop translational sensors, in vivo deployment and validation are necessary. While there are examples of visible whole-animal imaging systems, there are few, if any, examples of rapid spectroscopic data collection, largely due to the autofluorescence of tissue in the visible range. In this work, we optimized experimental conditions for plate reader-based high-throughput screening in solution and cells to in vivo use via an integrated workflow framework.

Methods

High-Throughput and In Vivo Near-Infrared Spectroscopy

High-throughput data collection was obtained via absorbance and fluorescence spectroscopy using the ClaIR NIR-II Microplate Reader with an attached point-and-shoot IRina spectral probe (Photon etc., Montreal, QC) (Figure ). The system contains two diode lasers at excitation wavelengths of 655 and 730 nm, with a maximum output in the microplate reader of 3 W in a continuous wave and 1 W for the spectral probe. The microplate reader measures NIR-II spectra from 900 to 1600 nm and absorbance spectra from 500 to 1600 nm using a halogen white lamp. The distance of the well plate height from the bottom of the laser is adjustable with a 0.1 mm resolution. The resolution of the spectrometer was less than 5 nm. The ClaIR/plate reader system is operated by independent ClaIR software, while the IRina/probe system is operated by PHySpec software (Photon, etc.).

1 Schematic of fluorescent SWCNT screening. High-throughput plate reader-based NIR spectroscopy was performed for SWCNT in buffer and cells, collected using ClaIR software for in vitro experiments and PHySpec for in vivo and hydrogel experiments. NIR probe spectroscopy was performed for SWCNT embedded into a hydrogel and in live mice. Spectral measurements were validated with single cuvette instrumentation. Well plate, macrophage, and mouse illustrations from the NIAID NIH BioArt Public Domain (BIOART-000007, -000312, -000282).

Single Cuvette Spectroscopy

We compared plate reader measurements to those from single-cuvette NIR spectroscopy and a single-cuvette UV–vis spectrometer. We used a V-730 UV–Vis Spectrometer (Jasco; Easton, MD) for visible absorbance measurements and a Nano Spectralyzer (NS) MiniTracer (Applied NanoFluorescence; Houston, TX) single-cuvette NIR absorbance and fluorescence spectrometer. For both instruments, single-use BrandTech UV-Cuvettes (Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH) were used, with 1× PBS as a reference. The absorbance measurements on the UV–vis spectrometer were taken with a scan speed of 400 nm/min. The absorbance measurements on the NIR spectrometer were taken with an integration time of 40 counts/s, averaged over 3 measurements, while the fluorescence measurements were taken with a 1000 count/s integration time, averaged over 3 measurements.

Fluorescent SWCNT Formulation

HiPCo-prepared SWCNT (NanoIntegris Technologies; Boisbri and, QC) were dispersed in a 1% sodium deoxycholate solution (DOC; Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH) using probe tip sonication with a 1/8″ microtip probe (Sonics; Newtown, CT), followed by ultracentrifugation at 58,000 X g for 1 h using an Optima Max-XP Ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter; Brea, CA). The top 75% of the supernatant was collected and stored at 4 °C until ready for use. For single-stranded DNA (ssDNA; Integrated DNA Technologies; Coralville, IA) suspended SWCNTs, an additional filtration step to remove free DNA was performed using a 100 kDa Amicon centrifugal filter (Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louis, MO) for 15 min at 14,000 X g and resuspended in 100 μL of 1× PBS.

Screening Commercial Well Plates for High-Throughput Measurements

To find the optimal transparent plate for near-infrared sample measurements, five were tested for well-to-well variation in spectral acquisition. Tested plates included the Corning UV transparent half-area 96-well plate (3679; Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH), Brandtech 96-well plate (781611; Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH), Corning 384-well plate (3640; Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH), Brandtech 384-well plate (781627; Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH), and Corning 1536-well plate (3831; Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH). Three plates of each type were filled with 0.5 mg/L sodium deoxycholate-suspended SWCNT (DOC-SWCNT): 7.5 μL in the 1536-well plate, 100 μL in Corning 96-well, 384-well, and BrandTech 384-well plates, and 150 μL in the BrandTech 96-well plate, into a total of 16 wells per plate. 96-well plates were skipped every 3 columns and 1 row, whereas 384-well plates were skipped every 6 columns and 3 rows. This was to ensure that there was no variation in the signal acquisition relative to the well position on the plate. To assess variability introduced by pipetting and other environmental factors, we evaluated the impact of single channel vs multichannel pipets in plate preparation, along with cleaning the bottom of the plate with a KimWipe (Fisher Scientific; Hampton, NH) prior to acquisition. Following plate preparation, we acquired fluorescence spectra with a 200 mW laser power with an exposure time of 1000 ms, and full emission spectra were obtained between 900 and 1400 nm.

Exposure Parameters for Optical Spectroscopy

We next sought to optimize optical SWCNT characterization for high-throughput analysis by adjusting the laser power, exposure time, gain, and binning. To test these parameters, we used the Corning half-area UV-transparent 96-well plate for all experiments except for those involving cell culture. We captured absorbance and fluorescence spectra of a 1% DOC-SWCNT dispersion. We modified the exposure time between 1 and 1000 ms for both visible and NIR absorbance. Then, we modified the laser power from 200 to 1750 mW and the exposure time from 5 to 500 ms for fluorescence measurements.

High-Throughput Spectroscopy of SWCNTs Endocytosed by Macrophages

Next, we sought to adapt this methodology for high-throughput SWCNT screening in living cells. We investigated whether modification of the instrument focal height for surface-attached cell cultures was necessary, as the standard laser-to-plate distance is 500 μm. We grew RAW 264.7 murine macrophages (ATCC; Rockville, MD) in 12 wells of a 96-well tissue culture-treated plate (Fisher Scientific), each a biological replicate, to 80% confluency. We then added 10 mg/L ssDNA (GT)15-SWCNTs to each well for 30 min, followed by two washes with 1× PBS and addition of fresh media. Fluorescence measurements in each of the 12 biological replicate wells were acquired using a laser power of 1750 mW and an exposure time of 500 ms, with the z-height adjusted between 0 and 1 mm to determine the influence of the distance between the bottom of the plate and the laser on the resulting spectra.

Assessment of Hydrogel-Encapsulated SWCNTs Using a Spectroscopic Probe Attachment

One potential method of immobilizing SWCNTs for in vitro or in vivo spectroscopy is via encapsulation within a hydrogel. To optimize the use of the spectral probe, we encapsulated 1 mg/L (GT)15-SWCNTs within methacrylated methylcellulose hydrogels. We cast gels into three wells of a black Brandtech 96-well plate with a transparent bottom prior to gelation at room temperature for 30 min. Six spectral acquisitions were obtained from each well as we adjusted the exposure time from 250 to 1000 ms, at 1000 mW, and power from 250 to 1000 mW, at 1000 ms to optimize data collection. A single acquisition of an individual well was first taken and used as a background for subtraction in data analysis.

Rapid Spectral Analysis in Live Mice

We next evaluated the optimal fluorescence spectral acquisition parameters in live mice. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The City College of New York. We subcutaneously injected 2 mg/L (GT)15-SWCNTs into the flank of three hairless, immunocompetent, SKH1-Hr hr female mice (4–6 weeks old). Mice were anesthetized with vaporized isoflurane for the spectral acquisition. Data were acquired immediately after injection, 1, 4, and 24 h postinjection using a laser power of 1000 mW with an exposure time of 1000 ms. A single acquisition was taken of the area being measured prior to injection and was used as a background. Further data processing was performed with OriginPro.

Data Collection, Analysis, and Statistics

Spectral data were collected in triplicate, except that macrophage experiments were performed with 12 replicates, with the mean ± standard deviation reported. Peak fitting analysis was performed on the fluorescence spectra using a custom MATLAB code (available upon request) to fit the peaks to a pseudo-Voigt profile and obtain the center wavelength and maximum intensity values for each (n,m) species. Fits with R 2 > 0.99 were considered successful. The standard square error (SSE) and root-mean-square error (RMSE) were also calculated for each fit.

Results and Discussion

SWCNT Spectral Analysis

Using a high-throughput NIR-II microplate reader, we obtained representative absorbance and fluorescence spectra of DOC-SWCNTs (Figure A–C). Absorbance spectra were captured over the visible and NIR regions, from 500 to 1600 nm. Fluorescence emission spectra were acquired with both 655 and 730 nm excitation wavelengths between 900 and 1700 nm. The representative absorbance spectra show signature peaks at 1150 and 1250 nm, while fluorescence spectra display the characteristic peaks of SWCNTs for the (7,6), (7,5) (655 nm), and (9,4) (730 nm) SWCNTs at 1040, 1137, and 1129 nm, respectively.

Absorbance acquisition using the single-cuvette UV–vis spectrometer provides spectral measurements over the range of 300–1100 nm, while the single-cuvette NIR-II spectrometer collects absorbance between 900 and 1600 nm (Figure D). We compared these two standalone instruments with continuous absorbance measurements from the plate reader over both the visible and near-infrared regions. We found a slightly higher signal with the UV–vis absorbance spectrometer (∼20%) compared to the microplate reader. This may be due to the plate reader using a halogen white lamp while the single-cuvette instrument uses a tungsten-halogen lamp, differences in relative absorbance of the microplate and cuvette, or in detector efficiencies across its range. However, over the NIR-II range, the microplate reader yielded approximately 2-fold higher absorbance than the single-cuvette NIR-II spectrometer. On the lower end of the single-cuvette spectrometer’s range, inefficiencies in data collection by the instrument yielded slightly negative absorbance values. It is unlikely that differences are due to resolution, as the spectral resolution of the plate reader is <5 nm, while that of the single-cuvette spectrometer is 6 nm.

In comparing fluorescence spectra obtained from the microplate reader and single-cuvette spectrometer, we found some minor variation in emission due to the single-cuvette laser source being 638 nm, while the plate reader source is 655 nm. Because of this, the characteristic peaks of SWCNTs are resolved at slightly different wavelengths, and a minor variation in SWCNT excitation resonance can be seen (Figure E). In addition, the laser of the single-cuvette spectrometer has a fixed power of 110 mW, while the plate reader spectrometer allows laser power to be adjusted, with the minimum power setting to obtain clear spectra from SWCNTs being 200 mW. In addition, the spectral resolution of the high-throughput plate reader is less than 5 nm, while that of the single-cuvette instrument is similar at 5 nm. In addition, we assessed the signal-to-noise ratio of both instruments, finding no clear differences in that regard for either instrument (Supporting Information Table S2).

The spectral acquisitions demonstrated here realize the potential of high-throughput screening. While obtaining the fluorescence signal is dependent on the quantum yield of photoluminescent samples, it is possible to obtain suitable SWCNT spectra in 0.125 s. To account for movement between wells of approximately 2 s, start-to-finish for 96 samples could be performed in slightly greater than 3 min, with an additional minute or two necessary for automated acquisition setup and laser warm-up. Individual spectra may be obtained from the single-cuvette instrument in 0.5 s; however, this is substantially compounded by the time required to remove the sample, replace it, and enter acquisition parameters, which takes at least 30 s if the cuvettes are well-organized and files are not given separate names. In total, that would require almost 50 min of supervised, manual data acquisition compared to 5 min of unsupervised, automated data acquisition for 96 samples in the plate reader. Further, high-throughput acquisition may reduce variability introduced by the cuvette placement or sample volume. We also note that the plate reader allows for automated time course acquisition, whereas this would require manual placement and supervised acquisition otherwise.

Well Plate Evaluation to Reduce Intraplate Variability

While most well plates are manufactured for use with visible absorbance and fluorescence plate readers, we sought to determine which of several commonly used options were most compatible for NIR-II data collection. We tested five different plates: Corning half-area 96-well (UV transparent), Brandtech 96-well, Corning 384-well, Brandtech 384-well, and Corning 1536-well plates. Comparing the variance in the center wavelength and intensity values among the plates tested for the (7,5) and (8,6) SWCNTs shows that the Corning 96-well half-area plates resulted in the lowest maximum variance (0.098 nm) compared to the other plates used (Figure ). Additionally, we loaded 16 wells of 3 Corning 96-well plates with both a single-channel pipet and a multichannel pipet to compare the impact on the fluorescence signal for each sample. Based on the variance in the peak intensity and center wavelength for the (7,5) and (8,6), the multichannel pipet demonstrated no statistically different results compared to single-channel pipetting (two-tailed t test). Another potential point of variance in plate preparation is the bottom of the well plate collecting ambient dust, which may interfere with the signal acquisition. However, we found no statistically significant difference in Brandtech 96-well plate fluorescence spectra before or after gentle wiping of the bottom surface for both SWCNT species analyzed (two-tailed t test). Overall, the variance of the center wavelength shifts and intensities points to the Corning half-area 96-well plates to be the most compatible with the NIR-II plate reader (Figure ). The plate being rated for UV use is also beneficial for absorbance acquisition using the microplate reader, along with fluorescence measurements. The Corning 96-well plate has also been used in previous experiments of similar design with different instruments, demonstrating its compatibility and utility for nanotube-based fluorescence detection. ,,

Excitation Parameters for High-Throughput Spectroscopy

We then sought to optimize signal quality in the plate reader experimental setup. We first assessed a range of incident light and excitation times with 1 mg/L DOC-SWCNTs to evaluate their impact on the resulting absorbance and fluorescence. For absorbance spectra, exposure times between 1 and 1000 ms were tested for both visible and IR absorbance. As exposure time increased between 10 and 100 ms, the absorbance peaks became smoother and brighter, as expected (Figure A). The maximum exposure time for absorbance measurements on the high-throughput plate reader is 1000 ms. Very low exposure times (∼10 ms) need to be used to get a proper signal of SWCNTs in the visible region (Figure B); otherwise, the characteristic peaks become oversaturated. Increasing the IR absorbance exposure time to 100 ms does improve spectral acquisition (Figure C), displaying a spectrum with smooth, defined peaks in the IR range. While exposure time selection will vary based on the sample concentration, it is ideal to use lower times (1–10 ms) to obtain readable spectra in both the visible region and higher times (50–250 ms) in the IR region.

For fluorescence spectroscopy, exposure times ranging from 5 to 500 ms were tested using DOC-SWCNTs at 10 mg/L for excitation wavelengths 655 and 730 nm, with the laser power remaining constant at 200 mW. As expected, increasing exposure time to the sample increases the intensity of the SWCNT fluorescence signal for each of the 655 and 730 nm lasers (Figure D,E). Upon pseudo-Voigt peak-fitting analysis of the fluorescence spectra, we found that higher exposure times yielded lower standard deviation of the center wavelength and low root-mean-square error (RMSE) and sum of squared error (SSE) values across samples for (n,m) species, while the standard deviation of intensity values saw an increase with higher exposure times (Supporting Information Figure S1). This indicates consistent measurements among samples for the spectra obtained at higher exposure times. Within the range of exposure times that obtain suitable spectra, 125–500 ms, we note that the top of the range would only add approximately 36 additional seconds to measuring 96 samples, as the majority of the <5 min would be devoted to plate setup and automated movement between wells.

We then examined the effect of laser power on spectral acquisition using 1 mg/L DOC-SWCNTs. With a fixed exposure time of 500 ms, the laser power for both 655 and 730 nm excitation wavelengths was adjusted between 200 and 1750 mW. As expected, SWCNT fluorescence intensity increased with increasing laser power (Figure F,G). At 1500 mW and above for the 655 nm laser, the (7,6) peak became overexposed, saturating the detector. The standard deviation of the peak center wavelength for most SWCNT species analyzed showed minimal differences as the power increased. The standard deviation for maximum intensity values, however, did increase with increasing laser power from 45 up to 2400 ADU. Lower RMSE and SSE values were also observed with increased laser power (Supporting Information Figure S2). Nevertheless, using a higher laser power does produce more defined peaks. On the basis of these results, we found that it is best to use laser powers above 1500 mW with our samples to obtain brighter signals. However, subsequent adjustment of the exposure time based on the sample type and concentration is needed to avoid overexposure.

We also evaluated the effect of 2 × 1 data binning during spectral acquisition when adjusting the laser power. Data binning is a preprocessing method that condenses data in an effort to increase the signal-to-noise ratio. Two × 1 binning was applied to data acquisition, summing the pixel values into pairs and reducing the resolution of the output signal by a factor of 2. For the (8,3), (7,5), (9,5 + 10,3), (10,2), (9,4), and (8,6) peaks, the unbinned data points had lower standard square error (SSE) and root-mean-square error (RMSE) values compared to binned data points (Supporting Information Figure S6). This may be explained by the relative abundance of each (n,m) species, wherein more abundant species, such as the (7,5) and (9,4), had stronger emission, and thus, more data were collected. Less prominent peaks, such as the (9,5 + 10,3) and (8,7) species, produce less intense fluorescence; thus, binning the data points reduces the information obtained from those peaks. While enabling binning produces smoother peak fitting, it does reduce the resolution of the data and would mostly be useful when samples are inherently dim. With binning enabled during spectral acquisition, the number of data points collected over the full emission spectrum is half of that acquired without binning (Supporting Information Figure S3A). Therefore, when data are obtained across the NIR-II spectrum, we found that it is best to do so without binning for SWCNTs to ensure maximum data is obtained, though it could be useful if using other fluorescent materials.

We also assessed variability in spectral acquisition when acquiring data in high-gain mode, which is designed to amplify dim fluorescent spectra. We tested the use of the high-gain mode with low laser powers (200 mW). However, we found that the high-gain mode produced a noisy signal with undefined SWCNT spectral peaks for the specific sample we tested, which indeed has a strong signal. Therefore, it is likely that the high-gain mode would indeed be optimal for low-signal samples, but with our samples, the enhanced electronic sensitivity induced unnecessary noise (Supporting Information Figure S3A).

NIR-II Fluorescence Analysis of SWCNTs Endocytosed by Macrophages

We next sought to obtain NIR-II spectra from (GT)15 ssDNA-SWCNT endocytosed by murine RAW 264.7 macrophages. Macrophages play an important role in both acute and chronic disease progression, as they may have both pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes (M1 and M2). Low-throughput NIR-II spectral microscopy has been used to assay macrophage phenotype with (GT)15-SWCNTs, while others have evaluated surface binding of SWCNTs to cells. That work and others demonstrated that ssDNA-SWCNTs are nontoxic to cells, while 10 mg/L is suitable for detecting internalized SWCNTs and assessing cellular changes. ,, Further, macrophages are central to directing immune responses through cytokine expression. Noncell-based spectroscopy studies have implemented SWCNTs for detection of such pro-inflammatory cytokines, though none have incorporated their expression from live cells to date. ,,, By using a SWCNT sensor that can differentiate M1 and M2 macrophage phenotypes, we sought to assess whether sensor assessment was amenable to high-throughput screening relative to previous microscopy findings. Indeed, we found that we were able to obtain distinct NIR-II emission from SWCNTs internalized within macrophages in high-throughput format, as evidenced by characteristic SWCNT emission peaks (Figure A,B). Across 12 biological replicates, substantial variability in measurements was observed up to approximately 1100 nm, while peaks from 1100 to 1400 nm were much more defined with limited variability. We then sought to determine whether adjustment of the focal distance between the laser and the plate modified the signal obtained. The default laser-to-plate distance of the plate reader is 500 μm. We therefore modified the distance as allowed by the instrument (0–1 mm), finding only minimal differences in the spectral signal of SWCNTs inside macrophages. The signal was slightly dimmer at 1 mm and was only marginally increased at 750 and 10 μm. These differences are negligible and likely represent experimental variance, as no clear trend was observed (Figure C). It is likely that the allowable changes to focal height do not cause the macrophages to fall outside of the excitation beam waist. We also did not observe any phenotypic changes that would be indicative of cell death or a change in phenotype. These studies demonstrated that we were able to rapidly assess SWCNT sensor fluorescence in macrophages using high-throughput screening, which could obviate time-consuming single field-of-view screening with a microscope but does lose information as it is an ensemble method. This has the potential to facilitate high-throughput drug screening or understanding inflammatory disease biology.

Assessment of Hydrogel-Encapsulated SWCNTs

After sensor lead development in solution or in cells, preclinical development would often proceed to live animal validation. To do so, the plate reader was attached to a flexible probe for rapid animal point-and-shoot spectroscopy. To first assess immobilized SWCNTs in vitro using this device, 12 wells were cast with methylcellulose hydrogels containing 1 mg/L of (GT)15-SWCNTs as in our prior work (Figure A,B). We obtained fluorescence spectra with the NIR-II spectral probe with a laser power at a maximum of 1000 mW, as we noticed some gel melting above that power, evidenced by gel opacity and inhomogeneity. We note that this maximum power could be different for other gels, for example, perhaps lower for thermal hydrogels. Observations of the resulting fluorescence spectra reveal that using low laser power and short exposure times produces a measurable NIR-II fluorescent signal. Peak intensity values with excitation powers of 200 mW and 1000 mW increased by 2.7-fold for 730 nm excitation and by 4.3-fold for 655 nm excitation. As expected, higher laser powers and longer exposure times produced smooth, narrow peaks (Figure C–F). Decreased RMSE and SSE values were observed with an increase in exposure time and laser power. The standard deviation of the peak center wavelength for all (n,m) species analyzed decreased with increasing laser power and exposure time. However, the standard deviation of peak intensity values increased with laser power and exposure time for all analyzed data (Supporting Information Figures S4 and S5).

We also investigated the influence of standard overhead fluorescent lighting on the probe data acquisition. Interestingly, we found no substantial differences in the peak intensity and center wavelength (Supporting Information Figure S3B). This is despite the standard best practice of turning off overhead lighting for such measurements. Automatic binning and high-gain modes for signal acquisition were also tested using the spectral probe. High-gain mode did not provide any measurable signal (Supporting Information Figure S3C). Binning during spectral acquisition slightly lowered the intensity of the signal, but as seen in the plate reader studies, the amount of data points collected with binning enabled is half of that collected with binning disabled. Binning could be beneficial for samples that exhibit photobleaching or blinking or have a low quantum yield. As the resolution of the signal is diminished, , it should not be used by default. High gain mode could be useful for samples that are either very dilute or have inherently dim fluorescence.

SWCNT Spectral Analysis in Live Mice

We performed in vivo spectral acquisition using hairless, immunocompetent SKH1-Hr hr mice injected subcutaneously with 200 μL of 2 mg/L (GT)15-SWCNTs, as previous work used this concentration to obtain fluorescence signals from SWCNT in vivo. , Spectral measurements at excitation wavelengths of 655 and 730 nm were acquired immediately postinjection, as well as after 1, 4, and 24 h at the site of injection and surrounding area using the spectral probe attachment. We used the laser power and exposure times established with the hydrogel system. At 1000 mW and 1000 ms, the power density of the laser onto the sample is approximately 3.5 × 105 mW/cm2. We found no signs of tissue damage, no obvious scarring, nor any physiological signs of stress in the animals at these settings. However, future safety studies that perform staining for free radical stress and other biochemical markers of damage would be necessary to systematically rule out this possibility.

We found a signature SWCNT fluorescence signal at each time point at the site of injection (Figure A). Peak evaluation of three (n,m), (7,6), (7,5), and (9,4), demonstrated relatively homogeneous fluorescent spectra up to 4 h postinjection, with greater heterogeneity in signals between animals observed at 24 h (Figure B–G). We hypothesize that heterogeneity at 24 h between animals may be due to biological heterogeneity in depth of SWCNT injection, cellular uptake, and tissue transport, or biomolecular interactions (for example, inflammation or free radical stress). It may also be due to the height and angle at which the spectral probe was placed, relative to the mouse skin. Future studies may implement a method of standardized probe and animal placement at each time point to reduce this heterogeneity. Despite that heterogeneity, these studies demonstrate the ability to collect freely injected SWCNT sensors over the period of 1 day, as well as demonstrating the translation of a lead sensor to testing in a preclinical model.

Conclusions

In this work, we evaluated high-throughput spectroscopy of SWCNT NIR-II sensors using a microplate reader in solution and after endocytosis by macrophages. We then assessed rapid data acquisition from these sensors after immobilization in a hydrogel and subcutaneous injection of mice. We developed and provided suggested parameters for each use case with the high-throughput plate reader and optical probe setup (Supporting Information Table S1). Such an integrated screening and testing methodology portends the possibility of rapid diagnostic device development, both in vitro and in vivo. We did so with a model NIR fluorophore, single-walled carbon nanotubes that exhibit stable, bright fluorescence in solution, cells, and mice. However, additional optimization may be necessary for other SWCNT formulations or other NIR fluorophores on the basis of their quantum yield and emission profile. Another limitation is the noise found from macrophage experiments in vitro, which may be dependent upon total SWCNT endocytosis and quantum yield, which would be different for each fluorophore formulation and cell line. Further, another is the observed variability in vivo due to placement of the laser probe, which could be addressed with custom-printed masks to standardize height and angle of animal placement. In this work, we optimized and validated an expedited framework workflow for preclinical lead development and testing. However, future work must leverage this framework, advancing from a large library of potential sensors to several hits and ultimately a preclinical lead. It is likely that machine learning would be necessary to incorporate and execute upon the large data sets obtained. The potential exists to rapidly develop novel sensors and diagnostics for a wide variety of disease conditions with both cell line and animal model testing, ranging from chronic inflammatory diseases, cancer, to even pharmacological profiling. The next generation of research tools and clinical diagnostics requires greater throughput, more data, and more rapid optimization, which we aimed to approach here.
