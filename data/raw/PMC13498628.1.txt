Opinion statement

Microscopically positive surgical margins (R1) complicate 15–30% of soft tissue sarcoma (STS) resections and independently worsen local recurrence and overall survival, yet no randomised trial has targeted the R1 population; we recommend risk-adapted management directed by a multidisciplinary team at a specialist sarcoma centre. Decision-making should integrate histological subtype, tumour grade, anatomical site, margin context, and individualised prognostic risk estimated by validated nomograms such as Sarculator and PERSARC. Planned positive margins on critical anatomical structures, most often encountered in retroperitoneal sarcoma, do not mandate re-excision, as local recurrence rates approximate those of R0 resection, and management should proceed directly to histotype-tailored adjuvant radiotherapy where indicated. Inadvertent positive margins after unplanned or non-specialist excision carry a substantially higher local recurrence risk and warrant re-excision whenever secondary R0 is achievable without prohibitive morbidity, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy in high-grade or deep tumours. When further surgery is not feasible, adjuvant radiotherapy alone remains the standard adjunct and substantially reduces local recurrence in extremity STS. Adjuvant anthracycline-ifosfamide chemotherapy should be considered in patients identified as high-risk by validated nomograms, in whom R1 status is the strongest predictor of survival benefit. Active surveillance with structured imaging follow-up is a reasonable option for small, low-grade R1 tumours at sites of prohibitive re-excision morbidity. Referral to a specialist sarcoma centre before any initial intervention remains the single most impactful measure to reduce R1 incidence and optimise oncological outcomes and should be regarded as the overarching standard of care.

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) are a heterogeneous group of mesenchymal malignancies for which complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment [1, 2]. The quality of surgical margins is the single most important modifiable prognostic factor, with margin status independently predicting local recurrence (LR), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and overall survival (OS) [3]. Analysis of margin classification systems in a cohort of 2,217 extremity and truncal STS patients demonstrated 10-year LR rates of 8% for R0, 21% for R1, and 44% for R2 resections, underscoring the stepwise increase in risk conferred by incomplete excision [4]. Positive margins have been associated with a nearly twofold increase in disease-specific mortality (HR 1.95), establishing margin adequacy as a central determinant of oncologic outcome [3]. However, achieving R0 resection in STS remains a significant clinical challenge. The anatomic complexity of many sarcoma sites, the proximity of tumours to critical neurovascular structures, and the frequent presentation of these rare tumours to non-specialist surgeons all contribute to suboptimal initial surgery. Particularly in the context of initially marginally resectable disease- where the inherent risk of an R1 margin is markedly elevated-intensive multimodal neoadjuvant strategies are increasingly necessitated to optimise surgical clearance [5]. A prospective phase 2 clinical trial (UNRESARC) evaluating preoperative hypofractionated RT (5 × 5 Gy) interdigitated with AI CHT demonstrated profound efficacy in this challenging demographic. Despite the presence of bulky, technically demanding tumours (median largest dimension 17.4 cm), this regimen facilitated en bloc R0 resections in 71.7% of patients, yielding an estimated 2-year LRFS of 67% and a 2-year DRFS of 57% [5]. Data from the French NETSARC nationwide registry demonstrated that only 36–47% of initial STS operations achieve R0 margins, with the higher figure reflecting improvements following implementation of a structured reference centre network [6]. A US National Cancer Database analysis of 25,406 extremity STS patients found that high-volume centres (≥ 20 cases/year) achieved significantly fewer positive margins than low-volume hospitals (12% vs. 17%, p < 0.001), with corresponding improvements in OS (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.75–0.88) [7, 8]. Similarly, the Spanish GEIS prospective registry reported 3-year OS of 82% at reference centres compared with 70.4% at local hospitals (p = 0.003) [9]. A systematic review of 66 studies confirmed that specialist centre surgery is associated with higher negative margin rates, lower LR, greater limb conservation, and improved OS [10].

Unplanned excisions

(whoops) remain a major driver of suboptimal margins and represent a systemic failure in the early recognition and appropriate referral of sarcoma. Population-based data from the Netherlands indicate that 18.2% of all first STS operations are unplanned excisions, with 14.4% occurring within hospitals [11]. A Latin American referral centre series found that 35% of extremity STS patients had undergone prior unplanned resections before specialist consultation, with 40–50% of primary resections for extremity sarcomas occurring without prior biopsy [12, 13]. Unplanned excisions are associated with significantly higher rates of positive margins; one comparative analysis reported R0 rates of 78.8% for planned resections vs. only 12.6% for unplanned procedures [14]. The Swiss Sarcoma Network reported that 25% of patients experienced unplanned resections, and a fragmented care pathway (initial treatment outside a specialist centre) was an independent predictor of LR (adjusted OR 2.7, p = 0.003) [12]. The timing of referral following unplanned excision is critical. A recent study demonstrated that late consultation (> 2 months after unplanned excision) was independently associated with inferior LRFS (HR 1.95), DMFS (HR 2.76), and OS (HR 2.53) compared with early referral [15]. Implementation of structured referral guidelines can mitigate this problem: the UK experience showed that implementation of NICE referral guidelines reduced the proportion of non-radical excisions from 27.2% to 19.8% (p = 0.001), with reduced regional variation [16]. These data support early referral to specialist sarcoma MDT as the optimal strategy after R1 resection remains complex and context dependent, particularly across different histological subtypes, anatomical sites, and margin scenarios, and therefore requires integration of guideline recommendations with site-specific and histology-specific evidence [17, 18]. Therefore, we aim to provide a comprehensive, evidence-based framework for the management of R1 resection in STS, addressing the decision-making process from margin positivity through definitive adjuvant treatment, with particular attention to the clinical variables that should guide individualised management and the essential role of MDT.

The Role of (Early) Specialist Unit Referral

The superior outcomes achieved at sarcoma reference centres reflect a comprehensive infrastructure that extends beyond surgical expertise alone. It is recommended that patients with suspected or confirmed STS should be evaluated and managed within specialist multidisciplinary sarcoma services, reflecting the consistent association of reference-centre care with higher-quality diagnosis, surgery, and oncological outcomes [18, 20]. The essential components of a sarcoma reference centre include a core multidisciplinary team comprising site-specific surgical expertise (including orthopaedic, thoracic, head and neck, gynaecologic, and reconstructive surgeons), medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, musculoskeletal radiologists, and sarcoma pathologists, as well as an extended team of allied professionals including interventional radiologists, physical medicine and rehabilitation specialists, genetic counsellors, and palliative care providers [19, 21, 22]. The impact of multidisciplinary tumour board (MDTB) presentation on outcomes has been prospectively demonstrated in the NETSARC registry. Among 12,528 patients with first diagnosis of soft tissue and visceral sarcoma, those presented to a specialized MDTB before treatment initiation (42.2%) had significantly better compliance to clinical practice guidelines, including higher rates of biopsy before surgery, appropriate imaging, quality of initial surgery, and fewer reoperations (all p < 0.001) [23]. Critically, presentation to an MDTB before treatment was associated with significantly better LRFS and RFS in both univariate and multivariate analysis. The NETSARC nationwide study further demonstrated that surgery in a reference centre was independently associated with improved OS (HR 0.681, 95% CI 0.618–0.749, p < 0.001), LRFS, and event-free survival after controlling for other prognostic factors [24].

The diagnostic pathway at reference centres is structured to optimise treatment planning. Diagnostic work-up should include appropriate cross-sectional imaging before biopsy, followed by carefully planned core-needle sampling performed in a manner that does not compromise subsequent definitive resection [17, 18]. Carefully planned core needle biopsy, performed after adequate imaging and placed along the future resection axis with minimal dissection and careful attention to hemostasis, is the preferred diagnostic approach. The biopsy should establish both histologic subtype and grade, utilizing ancillary diagnostic methodologies including immunohistochemistry, classical cytogenetics, and molecular genetic testing as appropriate [25]. The Toronto Margin Context Classification has introduced important nuance to the interpretation of positive margins, demonstrating that planned positive margins on critical structures carry LR rates not significantly different from R0 (11% vs. 8%, p = 0.18), whereas inadvertent positive margins are associated with a 10-year LR rate of 35% [1, 4]. This distinction highlights that the clinical context in which a positive margin arises-whether anticipated and managed within a multidisciplinary framework at a reference centre or resulting from inadequate initial surgery is as prognostically important as the margin status itself. Both ESMO-EURACAN-GENTURIS and NCCN acknowledge that a planned close or microscopically positive margin may be acceptable when required to preserve a critical structure, provided that this decision is made within an MDT framework and incorporated into the overall local-control strategy [17, 18]. Local Recurrence After Non-Radical Resection

Reporting heterogeneity across margin classification systems limits direct comparison of LR rates, and site-specific biology further complicates generalisation, as retroperitoneal and extremity sarcomas behave distinctly. In addition, tumour location poses a challenge to optimal resection, as retroperitoneal and extremity sarcomas require different management strategies. A meta-analysis of 33 studies assessing the risk of recurrence in extremity STS found that positive margins were associated with an increased risk of recurrence in 21 studies [26]. However, the meta-analysis of retroperitoneal sarcoma (RPS) assessing disease-free survival (DFS) showed no significant differences between the extended resection group and the tumour resection alone group (OR: 1.22; 95% CI: 0.84–1.60; p = 0.409) [27]. A recent study of optimal margin width in extremity sarcomas showed that the wider the margin, the longer the LRFS (LRFS; p = 0.0045). However, a margin of more than 10 mm did not significantly increase LRFS compared to a margin of more than 5 mm [13]. These findings support the idea of wide resection, while suggesting the possibility of sufficiently sparing surgery with a 5 mm margin.

Analysis of the most used AJCC classification showed that the probability of LR at 10 years was 8% for R0, 21% for R1 and 44% for R2 (p < 0.001) [28]. None of the surgical margin grading systems showed superiority (p = 0.14). After analysing each of the subgroups distinguished in the MSTS and AJCC classifications, it was found that dichotomisation into negative/positive margins was sufficient to indicate the risk of recurrence. Among patients with negative (R0 and non-intralesional) but close (< 1 mm) surgical margins, 24% had a recurrence within 3 years of surgery, compared to 50% with positive margins and 12% with negative margins in both systems, supporting the role for the UICC system [29]. Kainhofer et al. compared the UICC and AJCC systems and showed that the LR rate for all patients was 2.4%, 10.5% and 15.6% after 2, 5 and 10 years of follow-up, respectively. Non-radical resection showed a negative impact on LR in multivariate analysis in both UICC (HR: 15.634; 95% CI: 2.493–98.029; p = 0.003) and AJCC (HR: 11.214; 95% CI: 2.394–52.517; p = 0.002) classifications. In comparison, lower LR rates were observed in patients with radical resection according to the UICC classification [30]. Analysis of UPS confirmed that resection quality (P = 0.001, HR = 3.626, 95% CI: 1.675–7.846) was an independent risk factor for LR [31]. In the general population of RPS patients, recurrence can occur in up to 56.2%, often with multiple recurrences. In the group of patients with LR, non-radical resections were significantly more common than R0 resections (55.1% vs. 64.8%; p = 0.05). The LR rates for individual resection results were 39.5% for R0 resections, 54% for R1 resections and 6.5% for R2 resections, but the group of patients with macroscopically non-radical resection was small. furthermore, most LRs were observed in liposarcomas (27.8%) [32].

The correlation between surgical margin and local and distal control for RPS was observed. The DFS was 4.19 years (95% CI 3.39–5.00) for R0 margin and 0.40 years (95% CI −1.61-2.41) for R2 margin. However, R0 resection showed a significant advantage in terms of LFS − 4.47 years (95% CI 3.67–5.26) compared to R2 0.41 years (95% CI −1.58-2.40) [33]. Similarly, median OS for RPS with R2 resection was 21 months compared to 69 months for those with R0/R1 resections (P < 0.001) [34]. Watch and Wait Strategy

Active surveillance (watch and wait) after an R1 is not a standard strategy in adult STS. Contemporary UK guidance states that when further excision is likely to cause considerable morbidity or is unlikely to achieve complete clearance, observation or RT are reasonable alternatives; it also distinguishes a planned close or microscopic positive margin at a critical structure from an unexpected R1 margin. In a prospective database of 169 adults with positive margins, 5-year LRFS and -specific survival (CSS) were 85.4% and 59.4% after planned close dissection with persistent positivity, 91.2% and 63.6% when a critical structure had to be sacrificed, and 63.4% and 59.2% after an unexpected positive margin, underscoring that R1 disease is context dependent rather than biologically uniform [4, 18].

While LR does not uniformly translate into inferior OS - reflecting aggressive tumour biology rather than serving as a direct causal driver of distant metastasis - [50, 52]. Moreover, immediate adjuvant interventions - such as RT and re-excision - introduce substantial morbidity. Complications including fibrosis, oedema, joint stiffness, wound breakdown, and functional impairment can easily outweigh the anticipated oncological benefit in low-risk scenarios. Moreover, for specific histological subtypes and anatomic locations, the baseline risk of LR following an R1 resection remains sufficiently low that the marginal benefit of adjuvant therapy cannot justify its associated toxicity [1, 18, 53]. Atypical lipomatous tumours/well-differentiated liposarcomas (ALT/WDLPS) of the extremity, trunk, and abdominal wall represent the most robust indication for observation following R1 resection [54]. For ALT/WDLPS of the extremity or trunk, guidelines support a more conservative approach in selected cases, because marginal excision may be acceptable for atypical lipomatous tumours and observation after focal R1 resection can be reasonable when salvage at recurrence would not be unduly morbid [17, 18, 20, 55, 56]. A single-centre retrospective analysis of 54 patients undergoing marginal resection of ALT demonstrated LR rates of 18.8% for R0 and 22.7% for R1 resections, yielding no statistically significant difference in RFS (p = 0.42) [53]. Notably, no cases of distant metastasis were observed within this cohort [4]. Furthermore, a SEER database analysis encompassing 2,549 patients with WDLPS and positive margins found no significant difference in CSS between cohorts receiving adjuvant RT and those managed with observation (p = 0.81) [34]. These findings underscore that the inherently indolent biology of ALT/WDLPS rarely justifies the morbidity associated with immediate adjuvant intervention.

Stage IA encompasses small (≤ 5 cm), low-grade tumours, which possess an inherently favourable prognosis. The ARST0332 prospective trial, conducted by the COG, demonstrated that among 22 patients with low-grade R1 non-rhabdomyosarcoma STS managed exclusively with surgery, only 4 patients (18%) experienced events, all comprising LR or progression. Three of these four patients were effectively salvaged, and the observed overall LR rate was lower than statistically anticipated [18, 50]. The investigators concluded that the omission of RT should be actively considered in patients harbouring microscopic residual low-grade STS after maximal resection. The singular exception to this paradigm involves anatomic scenarios where subsequent LR would create substantial surgical or functional risks [50]. RPS represent a unique anatomic context wherein R1 margins are frequently encountered-and often anticipated as “planned R1” resections-due to the direct abutment of tumours to critical vascular or visceral structures. According to consensus guidelines from the TARPSWG, an R1 margin on critical structures (e.g., aorta, inferior vena cava, or spine) is deemed oncologically acceptable and is explicitly preferred over devastating surgical morbidity [26], [17, 18, 27]. Accordingly, postoperative RT is not routinely recommended after complete gross resection of RPS, although highly selected cases may still require individualised discussion when the predicted morbidity of LR is substantial [17, 18, 27, 54]. This paradigm is further substantiated by the STRASS trial, which demonstrated no definitive OS benefit for preoperative RT in the broader RPS population [26, 28]. In clinical practice, the standard of care for R1 RPS is therefore active observation coupled with structured radiological surveillance [18]. It should be noted that ARST0332 enrolled patients aged < 30 years and was conducted predominantly in a paediatric and young adult population; direct extrapolation of its findings to older adult STS populations requires caution [50, 57].

Risk Stratification

A critical component of the decision to pursue active surveillance following R1 resection is the accurate prognostication of the individual patient’s risk for DM and STS-related mortality. The Sarculator nomogram, developed by Callegaro et al. and externally validated across multiple international cohorts, provides a robust clinical tool for this purpose [1, 29, 30]. Developed utilising a cohort of patients with resected primary extremity STS, the Sarculator generates predictions for 5- and 10-year OS alongside the cumulative incidence of DM [29]. The nomogram incorporates four covariates: patient age, tumour size (as a continuous variable), FNCLCC grade (I, II, or III), and histological subtype (stratified into nine well-defined categories based on the WHO classification) [29]. Notably, the Sarculator possesses a dynamic capacity to update survival and recurrence risks over time, adjusting prognostication based on newly emergent clinical events such as locoregional recurrence or systemic spread. The model underwent three independent external validations (French, Canadian, and UK cohorts) demonstrating robust calibration and discrimination [29]. A multi-institutional validation study of 1,326 patients from nine high-volume US institutions confirmed the prognostic utility of the Sarculator, with C-indices of 0.72 (95% CI 0.70–0.75) for 5- and 10-year OS and 0.72 (95% CI 0.69–0.75) for 5- and 10-year DM [31]. A subsequent National Cancer Data Base analysis of 9,738 patients similarly demonstrated a C-index of 0.726 for the entire cohort, confirming that the Sarculator is a highly reliable predictor of actual OS in US patients [32].

Clinically, the Sarculator can be used to stratify patients into risk groups that inform the decision to observe versus treat after R1 resection [1]. Patients with a predicted 10-year OS ≥ 60% are classified as lower risk, while those with a predicted 10-year OS < 60% are classified as higher risk [33]. This threshold was validated in the ISG-STS 1001 randomised trial, which demonstrated that high-risk patients (10-year predicted OS < 60%) treated with neoadjuvant AI achieved significant improvements in LRFS and DFS and performed better than predicted, supporting the efficacy of CHT in this subgroup [33]. Specifically, within this high-risk stratum, AI significantly outperformed HT regimens regarding DMFS, yielding observed survival outcomes superior to those predicted by the Sarculator-a benefit particularly notable in specific histotypes such as LMS, SS, and MPNST. For patients being considered for observation after R1 resection, the Sarculator can help identify those at low risk of DM for whom local control is the primary concern. Malik et al. demonstrated that patients in the low-risk group (predicted 10-year OS ≥ 60%) have a lower chance of recurrence in the first two years, supporting a less intensive surveillance strategy in this population [49].This approach is further substantiated by ISG-STS 1001 data confirming that lower-risk patients derive minimal LRFS benefit from neoadjuvant AI, indicating that the potential toxicities of systemic therapy likely outweigh the oncological advantages in this cohort. Conversely, patients with high Sarculator-predicted risk necessitate more aggressive adjuvant therapy rather than observation [33].

The PERSARC model is an alternative risk prediction tool that incorporates additional variables including tumour depth and margin status [1, 58, 59]. A comparison of PERSARC and Sarculator in 664 patients demonstrated a high degree of correlation between the two models, with agreement in risk classification in 90% of patients [58]. Patients classified as high-risk by only one method had similar DFS and OS as those classified as high-risk by both methods, suggesting that either tool can be used for risk stratification [1]. The Sarculator has also been validated for RPS, where it demonstrated good discrimination for OS and DFS [60]. The addition of the CINSARC molecular signature did not improve Sarculator’s performance a limitation corroborated within the ISG-STS 1001 trial where the CINSARC signature similarly failed to accurately predict DFS or OS outcomes between low- and high-risk groups [60]. These findings suggest that clinical variables alone currently provide adequate prognostication. Crucially, it is imperative to delineate the scenarios in which observation after R1 resection is definitively contraindicated. The French NETSARC nationwide cohort of 1,284 patients with R1 margins after initial surgery outside a reference centre demonstrated that re-excision was an independent favourable factor for OS (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.23–0.56, p < 0.0001), LRFS (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.36–0.56, p < 0.0001), and RFS (HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.26–0.46, p < 0.0001) [61]. These data strongly support re-excision as an indispensable part of adjuvant care for the majority of patients with R1 margins, particularly those with high-grade tumours operated outside specialist centres. Similarly, in myxofibrosarcoma, a highly infiltrative histology, R1 resection without adjuvant RT is associated with significantly worse LRFS. In a multicentre study of 425 patients, R1 resection was an independent prognostic factor for local relapse (HR 1.26, p = 0.001), and adjuvant RT was beneficial after both R0 (p = 0.002) and R1 resection (p = 0.0001) [62]. Do We Need a Second Surgery After R1 Resection?

Re-excision is considered as the preferred strategy for most unexpected R1 resections when a secondary R0 resection can be achieved without major functional compromise, particularly after surgery performed outside a specialist sarcoma centre [17, 18, 63]. The AJCC‑endorsed R classification defines R0 as microscopically negative margins, R1 as microscopic tumour at the inked margin, and R2 as macroscopic residual disease [4, 18]. Despite advances in multimodality therapy, management of R1 margins remains one of the most controversial issues in sarcoma surgery, whether to perform re‑excision after R1 resection depends on histologic subtype, anatomic site, margin context, tumour grade, and the feasibility of achieving secondary R0 without undue functional morbidity. Multiple large retrospective and registry‑based series have established that R1 margins are associated with significantly higher LR than R0. In the landmark Toronto analysis of 2,217 patients with non‑metastatic extremity and truncal STS, 10‑year LR rates were 8%, 21%, and 44% for R0, R1, and R2 resections, respectively [4]. One study, analysing 363 patients with primary extremity STS, confirmed that positive margins were an independent adverse prognostic factor for both OS (HR 1.99, 95% CI 1.15–3.45) and DSS (HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.05–3.63) on multivariable analysis [3]. However, the relationship between margin status and DM or OS is more nuanced. In the Callegaro nomogram study, which included 7,926 patients from prospectively maintained European sarcoma databases, tumour depth and margin status were not significant in multivariable models for OS or DM after adjustment for tumour size, histology, grade, and age, suggesting that the independent effect of R1 resection may be largely confined to local control when major prognostic covariates are considered [29].

The 10-year LR rate for planned positive margins at critical structures was 11%, which was not statistically different from R0 margins (8%, p = 0.18). In contrast, inadvertent positive margins carried a dramatically higher 10-year LR rate of 35% (95% CI 25%−46%, p < 0.001). O’Donnell et al. further validated this concept in a prospective database of 169 patients with positive margins, demonstrating that 5-year LRFS was 85.4% for planned positive margins at critical structures versus only 63.4% for unexpected positive marginsc[42]. Importantly, resecting critical structures to achieve negative margins only marginally improved LRFS (91.2%) without any improvement in cause-specific survival (63.6% vs. 59.4%, p = 0.9), supporting the safety of planned close dissection to preserve function [42]. These data have important implications for the re-excision question: when a positive margin is planned and occurs at a critical structure in the setting of multimodality therapy, re-excision is generally not warranted and may cause unnecessary morbidity. Conversely, an inadvertent positive margin in soft tissue - particularly after an unplanned excision - represents a strong indication for re-excision. The traditional binary R0/R1 classification has been challenged by studies examining the prognostic significance of quantitative margin width. Gundle et al. demonstrated that when the R1 definition was expanded to include tumour within 1 mm of the inked margin (R+1 mm classification), the LR rate for R1 margins decreased from 21% to 12% at 10 years, while R0 rates remained unchanged at 8% [4]. This suggests that a negative but very close margin ( 1 mm) may be adequate in the context of multidisciplinary treatment including perioperative RT. Stauss et al. analysed 210 patients at a national sarcoma reference center and found that metric margin width as a continuous variable was not a significant prognostic factor for LR (OR 0.98, p = 0.574), distant recurrence (OR 1.0, p = 0.908), or OS (HR 0.98, p = 0.609) [64]. Notably, subgroup analysis revealed no differences between primary R0 resections and re-resections achieving R0 (re-R0) for LR (p = 0.727) and OS (p = 0.125), but re-R0 cases had a significantly higher distant recurrence rate (p = 0.022), suggesting that the biology of tumours requiring re-excision may be inherently more aggressive [64] For high-grade pleomorphic sarcomas specifically, Cates and Cates determined that the minimum safe tumour clearance was 5 mm without adjuvant RT and 1 mm with adjuvant RT [65]. After neoadjuvant therapy, close but negative margins (1 mm) appeared sufficient for local control, with minimal additional benefit from increasing margin width [66].

Myxofibrosarcoma (MFS) presents a unique challenge due to its highly infiltrative growth pattern with microscopic extension along fascial planes, often beyond what is apparent on imaging. Sstaged resections with temporary wound coverage for MFS and DFSP to obtain negative pathologic margins prior to definitive closure is recommended [18, 25, 66]. A large French multicenter study of 425 patients with localized MFS from the Conticabase found that R1 resection was an independent prognostic factor for local relapse (HR 1.26, p = 0.001), and adjuvant RT significantly reduced LR after both R0 (p = 0.002) and R1 resection (p = 0.0001) [62]. The 5-year local RFS was 67% overall, underscoring the high recurrence propensity of this histology [62] A study from the Stockholm Sarcoma Centre analyzing 87 MFS patients found that the cumulative LR incidence at 3.5 years was 80% for intralesional margins, 40% for marginal margins, and only 10% for wide margins, confirming that margins are the foremost determinant of LR in MFS [67]. Yurtbay et al. demonstrated that in MFS, patients with positive margins or margins ≤ 1 mm had significantly worse 2-year LRFS compared to those with margins > 1 mm (46.9% vs. 91.7%, p = 0.005), and positive margins were associated with decreased OS (HR 6.96, p = 0.018) [68]. Interestingly, Tomáš et al. found that while clear margins had a positive impact on recurrence-free survival in MFS, they did not significantly affect OS, suggesting that other factors such as grade and tumour size may play a more dominant role in determining long-term outcomes [68, 69]. Staged resection strategies for MFS have shown promising results. Berner et al. reported that 47% of 97 MFS patients had positive margins after first resection, but staged re-excision until clear margins were obtained resulted in a LR rate of only 14%, equivalent to patients who achieved clear margins at the first operation (p = 0.001) [70]. Similarly, Garayua-Cruz et al. demonstrated that single-stage resection with intraoperative frozen margin analysis achieved a 10-year LRFS of 90% in superficial MFS [71]. These data support re-excision for R1 margins in MFS whenever technically feasible.

In contrast to high-grade sarcomas, ALT/WDLPS of the extremity and trunk represents a histology where observation after R1 resection is often appropriate [72, 73]. The NCCN Guidelines explicitly recommend observation for focally positive margins in ALT/WDLPS if re-resection in the event of recurrence would not result in undue morbidity [18]. RT is reserved for selected patients with recurrent or deeply infiltrative primary lesions. Olson et al. analysed 97 patients with primary extremity WDLPS across seven US institutions and found no difference in recurrence-free survival between R0 and R1 resections; 5-year recurrence-free survival was 59.5% for R0 versus 85.2% for R1 [74]. Osterloh et al. similarly found that LR occurred in 18.8% of R0 and 22.7% of R1 resections of ALT, with no significant difference in recurrence-free survival (p = 0.42) [53]. A systematic review by Choi et al. encompassing 793 patients confirmed that marginal excision of truncal or extremity ALT/WDLS resulted in a slightly higher LR rate (11.9%) compared to wide excision (3.3%), but recurrences were almost always amenable to re-resection, and dedifferentiation was rare (1.1%) [75]. However, not all WDLPS subtypes behave identically. Kooby et al. demonstrated that margin-positive sclerosing WDLPS had a 10-year LRFS of only 17%, compared to the more favourable behavior of lipoma-like WDLPS and ALT, and recommended function-preserving re-excision or adjuvant RT for this subtype [73]. Segu et al. confirmed that the sclerosing subtype (adjusted OR 5.47) and tumours with previous recurrence (adjusted OR 4.92) were significant risk factors for LR in ALT [76]. Angiosarcoma consistently emerges as a histology with particularly poor outcomes regardless of margin status. In the NETSARC cutaneous sarcoma analysis, angiosarcoma was an independent adverse prognostic factor for both local RFS (HR 2.95, p < 0.001) and OS (HR 2.03, p = 0.065) [77, 78]. In the cutaneous sarcoma cohort, angiosarcoma subtype was a significant unfavorable factor for both local RFS and OS on multivariate analysis [72]. The aggressive biology of angiosarcoma means that while re-excision should be attempted when feasible, the benefit may be limited by the high propensity for both local and distant failure [77, 78].

The approach to R1 margins differs fundamentally by anatomic site (Table 3). For extremity and trunk wall STS, it is recommended to re-resect when margins are unexpectedly positive on soft tissue surfaces, provided it can be accomplished with minimal functional impact [17, 18]. For margins positive on critical structures (bone, nerve, major vessels), re-resection is usually not technically possible without significant morbidity, and adjuvant RT is recommended instead [2, 88]. For retroperitoneal and intra-abdominal sarcomas, the approach is markedly different. Given the close proximity to critical structures, close or microscopically positive margins are common and expected [2]. Guideline recommendations are concordant in supporting re-excision for unexpected R1 margins when technically feasible, whereas observation or RT are generally reserved for anatomically constrained, biologically indolent, or morbidity-sensitive scenarios [17, 18, 55, 56, 89] .

FNCLCC grade is one of the most powerful prognostic factors in STS. The ARST0332 study confirmed that grade, along with tumour size and extent of resection, was strongly associated with both event-free and OS [50, 57]. Higher-grade tumours with R1 margins carry a greater risk of both local and distant failure, strengthening the indication for re-excision. tumour size is consistently identified as a significant prognostic factor across studies. As discussed above, histology profoundly influences the significance of R1 margins. Infiltrative histologies such as myxofibrosarcoma warrant aggressive re-excision strategies, while indolent histologies such as ALT/WDLPS may be safely observed [63, 90, 92]. Surgery performed outside a sarcoma reference center is associated with higher rates of R1 margins and worse outcomes. Sugiura et al. demonstrated that LR developing within 2 years of primary surgery was associated with significantly worse 5-year survival (46%) compared to recurrence after 2 years (83%, p = 0.01), suggesting that early recurrence may be an indicator of aggressive tumour biology [93]. The available evidence supports a nuanced, histology- and context-dependent approach to re-excision after R1 resection in STS. Re-excision should be strongly considered for inadvertent positive margins in soft tissue, particularly after unplanned excisions performed outside reference centres, where Level II evidence from the NETSARC registry demonstrates significant improvements in OS, LRFS, and RFS [61]. For highly infiltrative histologies such as myxofibrosarcoma, staged resection strategies to achieve negative margins are associated with substantially lower LR rates [67, 70]. Conversely, for indolent histologies such as ALT/WDLPS, observation with surveillance is appropriate for focally positive margins when re-excision in the event of recurrence would not be unduly morbid [53, 74]. Planned positive margins at critical structures, when combined with perioperative RT, yield LR rates comparable to R0 resection and do not warrant re-excision [4, 42]. For retroperitoneal sarcomas, R1 margins are common and expected, and re-excision is generally not recommended [18]. In a TARPSWG analysis of 681 adults undergoing resection of first locally recurrent RPS, 83.3% achieved R0/R1 resection, major complications occurred in 16%, and 90-day mortality was 0.4%; transfusion requirement independently predicted both major morbidity and worse OS, suggesting that repeat surgery may be justified only in carefully selected patients treated at specialist centres [83]. In parallel, benchmark data from the RESAR registry showed that for primary retroperitoneal WDLPS/DDLPS resected at high-volume centres, acceptable outcomes included major morbidity ≤ 21%, 90-day mortality 0%, 3-year OS ≥ 88%, and 3-year LR ≤ 22%, supporting the concept that RPS margins must be interpreted within an expert-centre, histology-aware framework rather than by analogy to extremity STS [84]. Ultimately, the decision to re-excise must be made within a multidisciplinary sarcoma tumour board, weighing the oncologic benefit against functional morbidity, and informed by the specific histology, grade, size, anatomic constraints, and clinical context of the positive margin [1, 22, 55, 94, 95].

Does Chemotherapy after R1 Resection Prolong Prognosis?

Despite decades of randomised trials, no single study has definitively established adjuvant CHT as a universal standard of care after R1 resection. However, pooled analyses and risk-stratified data increasingly support its use in selected high-risk populations [59, 81, 82]. The pooled EORTC-STBSG analysis of 819 patients from two phase III trials (EORTC 62771 and 62931) identified R1 resection as the most important predictive factor for benefit from adjuvant CHT [82]. Patients with R1 margins had significantly better RFS and OS when treated with doxorubicin-based adjuvant CHT compared to observation, with a significant interaction term on both endpoints. Notably, this was a predictive (not merely prognostic) finding-meaning R1 status identified patients who differentially benefited from treatment, not just those with worse outcomes [82]. Interestingly, a network meta-analysis found that the benefit of adjuvant CHT was restricted to male patients (HR 0.78; p = 0.004 for DFS), with no effect observed in female patients (HR 1.08; p = 0.410) [96]. The largest randomised trial of adjuvant CHT in resectable STS enrolled 351 patients with grade 2–3 tumours randomised to doxorubicin plus ifosfamide (AI) versus observation. The primary analysis showed no significant benefit for adjuvant CHT in RFS (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.67–1.22) or OS (HR 0.94; 95% CI 0.68–1.31) [97]. However, an updated meta-analysis incorporating this trial and others suggested a possible small survival benefit (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.76–0.97; p = 0.02) [98]. Interestingly, doxorubicin-based monotherapy significantly improved OS (HR 0.80; p = 0.01) and DFS (HR 0.74; p = 0.0003), whereas the doxorubicin-ifosfamide combination did not reach statistical significance for OS (HR 0.78; p = 0.078), likely due to moderate heterogeneity across studies [98]. As mentioned, using the Sarculator nomogram to stratify patients from EORTC 62,931 retrospectively Pasquali et al. demonstrated that adjuvant AI CHT halved the risk of recurrence (HR 0.46; 95% CI 0.24–0.89) and death (HR 0.46; 95% CI 0.23–0.94) in patients with a predicted 10-year OS ≤ 51% (low pr-OS category). Among patients with pr-OS < 60%, significant DFS (HR 0.49; 95% CI 0.28–0.85) and OS (HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.30–0.90) benefits were detected [81].

In the ISG-STS 1001 phase III trial concurrent administration of epirubicin plus ifosfamide with preoperative RT was feasible and safe, resulting in an increased number of RECIST partial responses (19% vs. 10%, p = 0.041) compared to preoperative CHT alone, though at the cost of higher wound dehiscence (9% vs. 3.5%) and seroma rates (10.5% vs. 3%) [33, 99, 100]. Interestingly, adding regional hyperthermia (RHT) to neoadjuvant CHT (etoposide, ifosfamide, doxorubicin) significantly improved local progression-free survival (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.49–0.86, p = 0.002) and OS(HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54–0.98, p = 0.04) at a median follow-up of 11.3 years, with 5-year survival of 62.7% vs. 51.3% and 10-year survival of 52.6% vs. 42.7% [86]. The treatment response rate was 28.8% with RHT versus 12.7% without (p = 0.002) [87].A subgroup analysis of 149 patients with macroscopically complete resection (R0/R1) of retroperitoneal/abdominal STS showed that RHT significantly improved LPFS (56% vs. 45% at 5 years, p = 0.044) and DFS (34% vs. 27% at 5 years, p = 0.040) [101]. Translational analyses from this trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant therapy reprogrammes a non-inflamed tumour at baseline into an inflamed tumour, with post-treatment high tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) correlating with improved LPFS (HR 0.34, p = 0.008) and DFS (HR 0.38, p = 0.015). The combination with RHT primed the tumour microenvironment, enabling enhanced anti-tumour immune activity [102].

The applicability of adjuvant CHT data to the R1 population specifically is limited by several factors. No RCT has been conducted exclusively in R1-resected patients; all available evidence derives from subgroup analyses of trials enrolling mixed margin populations. Heterogeneity of STS histology means CHT benefit is likely confined to high-grade, chemosensitive subtypes - synovial sarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST), and high-grade leiomyosarcoma - with minimal benefit expected in chemo-insensitive subtypes such as well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDLPS) or solitary fibrous tumour.It should be noted that ARST0332 was conducted in a paediatric and young adult population (age < 30 years), and direct extrapolation of its findings to older adult STS populations requires caution [50, 57]. Grade 3–4 toxicity of AI regimens - including febrile neutropenia, nephrotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity - must be weighed against oncological benefit, particularly in patients with low predicted risk of distant metastasis by Sarculator. The ongoing NACLESS trial (JCOG2102, jRCTs031220446) is the first RCT comparing neoadjuvant plus adjuvant versus adjuvant-only AI CHT and may clarify optimal sequencing; results are awaited.

Radiotherapy After R1 resection

Regarding the role of RT in the setting of positive margins, a US Sarcoma Collaborative analysis of 514 patients found that RT may play a larger role in minimising LR than margin status alone. In patients with margins ≤ 1 mm, LR was 5.7% without RT versus 0% with either preoperative or postoperative RT [44]. Preoperative RT is now generally preferred per NCCN and ASTRO guidelines [18, 103]. The French CONTICABASE analysis showed adjuvant RT was protective against local relapse (HR 0.35, p = 0.0001) after both R0 and R1 resection [62]. However, 47% of MFS recurrences occur out-of-field versus 8% for leiomyosarcoma, cautioning against reduced field margins [67]. Stockholm study found surgical margin was the foremost determinant of LR, with RT not conferring additional benefit in that cohort [67].

Histology-specific neoadjuvant treatment may reduce the risk of R1 resection in borderline operable disease. The HYPORT-STS phase 2 trial investigated a moderately hypofractionated preoperative regimen (15 × 2.85 Gy over 3 weeks) in 120 patients with localized extremity or superficial trunk STS. With a median follow-up of 43 months, the 4-year LR-free survival was 93%, and grade ≥ 2 late toxicities were observed in only 9% of patients at 2 years. Both functional outcomes and quality of life improved over time from treatment. Moreover, In a prospective trial of preoperative hypofractionated RT (5 × 5 Gy) in 29 adults with locally advanced myxoid liposarcoma, median tumour size was 13 cm; among 27 evaluable patients, all specimens showed pathological response, 25 (92.6%) achieved R0 resection, and no LRs were observed, despite wound complications in 37.9% [80]. However, as the O’Sullivan trial showed preoperative RT had higher acute wound complications (35% vs. 17%, p = 0.01) but equivalent local control and a trend toward improved survival [67]. Long-term follow-up showed postoperative RT caused significantly more late fibrosis, edema, and joint stiffness due to larger fields and higher doses [69, 104].

The role of RT after R1 resection in RPS differs fundamentally from extremity STS. The STRASS trial (EORTC-62092), the first large, randomised trial in primary RPS, randomised 266 patients to preoperative RT (50.4 Gy) plus surgery versus surgery alone. The trial was negative, with no difference in abdominal recurrence-free survival (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.71–1.44, p = 0.95) or OS between the two groups [28]. However, a post-hoc exploratory analysis suggested a potential benefit in the liposarcoma subgroup, and the subsequent STREXIT analysis combining on-trial and off-trial patients demonstrated that preoperative RT was associated with improved abdominal recurrence-free survival in patients with well-differentiated liposarcoma and grade 1–2 dedifferentiated liposarcoma (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.40–0.97), but not in patients with grade 3 DDLPS or leiomyosarcoma [85]. The most recent TARPSWG propensity score-matched analysis of 582 patients with primary retroperitoneal WDLPS confirmed that neoadjuvant RT was associated with a significant decrease in LR (5-year CIF of LR: 6% vs. 26%, p < 0.001) [105]. Pisters et al., provided the first prospective evidence that adjuvant RT improves local control after complete resection of STS. In this trial, 164 patients were randomised to receive either adjuvant brachytherapy (42–45 Gy via iridium-192 implant) or no further therapy after complete resection. With a median follow-up of 76 months, the 5-year local control rates were 82% with brachytherapy versus 69% without (p = 0.04). Critically, this benefit was confined to patients with high-grade lesions, where local control was 89% versus 66% (p = 0.0025), while brachytherapy had no impact on local control in low-grade tumours (p = 0.49). Importantly, the improvement in local control did not translate into a reduction in distant metastasis or DSS [106, 107]. A more recent single-institution comparison by Alektiar et al. demonstrated that IMRT achieved significantly better 5-year local control than brachytherapy (92% vs. 81%, p = 0.04), despite IMRT patients having more adverse features including higher rates of positive/close margins [108]. Contemporary brachytherapy series using perioperative high-dose-rate techniques combined with EBRT have reported 5- and 10-year local control rates of 89% and 87%, respectively, with positive margins correlating with decreased local control on multivariate analysis [99]. A recent retrospective comparison confirmed that brachytherapy offers comparable local control to both neoadjuvant and adjuvant EBRT, with a lower risk of wound complications compared to neoadjuvant EBRT (25% vs. 50%, p = 0.02) [99, 100].

When LR occurs after prior RT and surgery, re-irradiation combined with surgery represents a viable alternative to amputation [109, 110]. The Princess Margaret Cancer Centre experience demonstrated a 5-year local control rate of 82.8% with re-irradiation plus surgery versus 67.9% with surgery alone, and a significantly higher 5-year limb preservation rate (80.8% vs. 40.2%, p < 0.001), though complications requiring intervention were more frequent with re-irradiation [109]. Guidelines note that brachytherapy, IMRT, and/or proton beam therapy may be utilized to reduce the morbidity of re-irradiation [18].

Regarding minimum safe margin distance, evidence suggests that ≥ 1 mm clearance with adjuvant RT or ≥ 5 mm without RT may define adequate margins for high-grade pleomorphic sarcomas of the extremity and trunk [65, 66]. For RPS, where R1 margins are common given the close proximity to critical structures, the NCCN states that re-resection and/or adjuvant RT are not typically considered, with adjuvant RT reserved for highly selected patients where LR would cause undue morbidity[18, 111].

Summary and Conclusions

R1 resection in adult STS represents a heterogeneous clinical scenario that cannot be managed algorithmically. The four principal management strategies - re-excision, adjuvant RT, adjuvant CHT, and active surveillance - are not mutually exclusive and should be deployed in combination, guided by histological subtype, FNCLCC grade, tumour size and depth, anatomical site, margin context per the TMCC framework, and patient-specific risk quantified by the Sarculator nomogram. Treatment at specialist sarcoma reference centres independently reduces LR and improves OS across population-based analyses; referral before any initial intervention remains the single most impactful preventive measure [6, 8, 10]. Re-excision should be the default strategy after unexpected R1 resection when secondary R0 can be achieved without disproportionate functional morbidity, particularly following unplanned excision outside a reference centre (Fig. 2). The TMCC framework provides essential contextual nuance: planned close margins at critical structures carry LR rates not significantly different from R0 (11% vs. 8%; p = 0.18), whereas inadvertent positive margins are associated with 10-year LR of 35% and warrant active intervention [4]. Active surveillance predominates for retroperitoneal and low-grade extremity disease, while high-grade, high-risk tumours warrant combined re-excision, radiotherapy, and anthracycline-based chemotherapy per ESMO-EURACAN-GENTURIS guidance

Adjuvant RT reduces LR by approximately 50% in extremity and trunk wall STS and should be offered when re-excision is not feasible or when the risk of local failure is high [44, 103]. Preoperative RT is generally preferred per NCCN and ASTRO guidelines given superior late-toxicity profile, though postoperative RT remains appropriate when preoperative planning was not possible [18, 103]. For retroperitoneal STS, the STRASS trial demonstrated no benefit from preoperative RT in unselected patients (HR 1.01; p = 0.95), supporting a histology-stratified approach in which WDLPS may derive preferential benefit [28, 85].

Adjuvant AI CHT should be considered in high-risk, chemosensitive STS when margins are R1, given EORTC-STBSG pooled evidence that R1 status is the most important predictive factor for CHT benefit [82]. Patient selection based on a predicted 10-year OS < 60% by Sarculator identifies those deriving significant absolute benefit [81]. Active surveillance is appropriate in a carefully selected minority - small, low-grade tumours at anatomical sites where further intervention carries disproportionate morbidity - and should be a formal, MDT-sanctioned decision with structured radiological follow-up.
