Background

Neuropathic pain (NP) frequently co-occurs with sleep disorders, yet the causal relationship between the two remain unclear, necessitating further investigation to elucidate their intrinsic connection.

Methods

We conducted linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC), Mendelian randomization (MR), multivariable mendelian randomization (MVMR) and causal analysis using summary effect estimates (CAUSE) analyses based on genome-wide association study summary statistics of six NP subtypes (FinnGen R12) and six sleep traits (UK Biobank) to verify causal associations. Multi-method combined gene prioritization screening genes, with comprehensive sensitivity analyses and bias correction performed.

Results

Our analyses revealed that among all NP subtypes, only insomnia exerted a stable potential causal effect on sciatica. LDSC analysis revealed a significant positive genetic correlation between insomnia and sciatica (rg = 0.395, P < 0.0017). MR analyses demonstrated that insomnia significantly increased the risk of sciatica (OR = 3.15, 95% CI: 2.20–4.51, PIVW = 3.48E-10), with robust results confirmed by multiple sensitivity analyses. CAUSE further validated a positive causal association between insomnia and sciatica (OR = 3.899, 95% CI: 1.823–8.339, P = 0.003). MVMR proved the causal association remained significant after adjustment for psychiatric disorders (PIVW < 0.05). Heritability enrichment analysis indicated that the heritability of insomnia was mainly enriched in the central nervous system. And six potential pathogenic genes were screened via multiple integrated analytical methods. Among these candidates, EIF3KP1 and PPIP5K2 showed the strongest genetic evidence and were validated in the FinnGen database.

Conclusion

We identified a potential causal effect of insomnia on sciatica. The comorbidity of the two conditions may be regulated by genetic mechanisms associated with the central nervous system. EIF3KP1 and PPIP5K2 were prioritized as candidate genes, and further cellular and animal experiments are required to validate their potential as molecular targets for intervening in this comorbidity.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a common type of chronic pain characterized by persistent and irreversible pain resulting from pathological changes or diseases affecting the peripheral or central somatosensory nervous system. Such pain is typically caused by nervous system injury or disorders, including peripheral nerve injury, spinal cord injury, stroke, and others.1,2 The pathogenesis of NP is complex, involving peripheral and central sensitization, abnormal neuronal excitability, and neuroimmune interactions, among others.3,4 Epidemiological surveys indicate that the prevalence of NP in the general population ranges from approximately 6.9% to 10%.5 Nevertheless, the exact pathological mechanisms responsible for neuropathic pain remain incompletely clarified, which contributes to the scarcity of potent therapeutic agents and intervention strategies.

Sciatica is a subtype of NP and a prevalent painful disorder in orthopedics, with a lifetime incidence varying from 13% to 40%,6 predominantly triggered by lumbosacral nerve root compression secondary to lumbar disc herniation or lumbar degenerative lesions.7 Relevant studies have demonstrated that disc herniation accompanied by nerve root compression accounts for the primary etiology in approximately 90% of patients.8 Other pathogenic factors include lumbar spinal stenosis, muscular spasm, and intra- or extra-spinal canal tumors.9 In contrast to sciatica, other NP phenotypes arise secondary to neuroinflammation, metabolic derangements or central nervous system injury.10–13 Such divergent pathological mechanisms imply that the correlation patterns between sciatica and sleep disorders differ from those observed in other NP variants.

Sleep represents a physiologically regulated biological state consisting of distinct phases, namely non-rapid eye movement sleep and rapid eye movement sleep. Slow-wave sleep, a substage of nonrapid eye movement sleep, delivers the deepest and most restorative sleep effects. By contrast, rapid eye movement sleep is characterized by dream generation and elevated cortical activity.14 Patients with chronic pain frequently present with sleep disturbances, mainly manifested as reduced sleep efficiency and abnormal sleep architecture:15 significant decreases in total sleep time and sleep efficiency, as well as marked prolongations in sleep latency, nighttime wakefulness, and the number of awakenings, accompanied by insufficient sleep duration, delayed sleep onset, and prolonged wakefulness.16,17 Among them, insomnia, a prevalent sleep disorder, has a comorbidity rate of up to 50–80% with chronic pain.18 There exists a bidirectional interaction between sleep and pain, which manifests differently across various pain phenotypes.19–22 This suggests that instead of generally analyzing the overall correlation between sleep and neuropathic pain, targeted research should be conducted to clarify the causal associations between diverse sleep traits and distinct subtypes of neuropathic pain.

Numerous previous studies have confirmed a bidirectional interaction between sleep and chronic pain. Accumulating evidence from recent studies suggests that sleep disturbances often precede pain onset and can serve as predictive markers for pain prognosis.23 In recent years, multiple potential shared mechanisms have been proposed to explain the link between sleep disorders and NP, include central sensitization,24,25 neuroinflammation,26,27 dysregulation of pain processing pathways,28,29 hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysfunction, altered neurotransmitter signaling,24,30,31 and fibromyalgia.32 Nevertheless, most evidence supporting these shared underlying mechanisms stems from animal models. Due to limitations in extrapolating findings from experimental models to clinical settings, their underlying mechanisms and causal links remain unclear. The majority of clinical research exploring the correlation between NP and sleep disorders is observational. Such designs are susceptible to confounding bias and reverse causality, making it difficult to reliably infer genuine causal effects between variables; furthermore, relevant Mendelian randomization (MR) studies remain scarce in this field.

MR is a causal inference approach based on genetic variants. It adopts single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) derived from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as instrumental variables (IVs) to evaluate the causal association between exposures and outcomes. Rooted in the random allocation of genetic alleles following Mendel’s laws of inheritance, this method effectively mitigates confounding bias and reverse causality, two major limitations of conventional observational researches.33

In this study, we systematically dissect the comorbidity mechanism underlying sleep traits and neuropathic pain through a multi‑dimensional integrative bioinformatics strategy. First, bidirectional MR analysis was primarily performed to explore the causal associations between NP and six common sleep phenotypes. Meanwhile, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) was adopted to precisely quantify the degree of genetic overlap and genetic correlation coefficients among these phenotypes and dissect their shared genetic basis. Furthermore, transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) was integrated to combine genetic variants with gene expression regulatory information for joint analyses using multiple methodologies. Findings from this study advance our understanding of the genetic mechanisms mediating comorbidity between the two disorders, and provide theoretical evidence to optimize clinical interventions for NP and sleep management.

Materials and Methods

Research Design

The GWAS datasets used in this study were all obtained from publicly published studies, and all relevant studies had obtained approval from the corresponding ethics committees and institutional review boards. In this study, LDSC, MR, causal analysis using summary effect estimates (CAUSE), and multivariable mendelian randomization(MVMR) were sequentially applied to systematically evaluate the genetic correlation and potential causal association between sleep traits and NP subtypes. On this basis, further multi-method combined analyses were performed. Using GWAS data from the UK Biobank (UKB) and FinnGen databases, we integrated TWAS, genetic fine-mapping, and Bayesian colocalization to screen and identify key genetic drivers regulating sleep traits. Meanwhile, combined with tissue-specific expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data from GTEx v8, multi-strategy joint analysis was used to achieve prioritization of candidate genes. During the analysis, the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test was used to correct for horizontal pleiotropy, together with false discovery rate (FDR) correction and sensitivity analyses to ensure the rigor of statistical inference. A multi-level cross-validation design was adopted to improve the reliability of the results and minimize confounding bias. Data Source

GWAS summary data for the six NP subtypes required in this study were all obtained from the FinnGen R12 database (https://r12.finngen.fi/), specifically including Diabetic peripheral neuropathy, Drug-induced polyneuropathy, Unspecified neuralgia, Trigeminal neuralgia, Postherpetic neuralgia, and Sciatica (with and without lumbago). Full phenotype pipelines for all NP subtypes, including registry filters, cross-version ICD codes and phenotype-specific exclusion criteria, are provided in the Supplementary Material 2. The UKB is a comprehensive UK cohort resource containing genetic, clinical and lifestyle data from around 500,000 participants; GWAS data for six sleep traits were obtained from this database,34–38 which also supplies genetic markers for anxiety, depression, and schizophrenia. In addition, the exposure and outcome data used for MR analysis were derived from the UKB and FinnGen datasets, respectively. Participants in both cohorts were predominantly of European ancestry, with both males and females enrolled. There was negligible sample overlap between the two datasets, fully satisfying the primary prerequisite for the MR design, which requires a shared ancestral genetic background across study populations. Full demographic characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Genetic Correlation

LDSC evaluates genetic correlation from GWAS summary statistics by analyzing the association between test statistics and linkage disequilibrium (LD), quantifying genuine polygenic signals and bias without relying on sample overlap. Specifically, genetic covariance is estimated by regressing the product of z-scores for variants between trait 1 and trait 2 against their LD scores, and normalized by SNP-based heritability.39 In addition, this method can quantify the contribution of various factors by examining the correlation between test statistics and LD, where LD effectively distinguishes between inflation from true polygenic signals and confounding bias.40,41

MR Analysis

The STROBE-MR checklist was compiled and submitted as Supplementary Material 3.42 In this study, MR analysis was mainly performed using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method.43 Meanwhile, analyses were conducted using the MR-Egger method,44 weighted median method,45 constrained maximum likelihood model averaging (cML-MA),46 debiased inverse variance weighted (dIVW),47 and contaminated mixture model (ConMix)48 to ensure the reliability of the results.

Strict IVs screening criteria were applied: genome-wide significant SNPs (p < 5×10−8) associated with each exposure were initially retained, followed by clumping based on the 1000G reference panel with an LD threshold R2 = 0.001 and a 10000 kb aggregation window;49 SNPs correlated with the outcome (p < 5×10−5) were eliminated. F-statistic (cutoff < 10)50 was calculated to filter weak IVs and mitigate weak instrument bias, where F-statistic relies on IVs-exposure explained variance (R2), sample size (n) and IVs count (k). Steiger filtering was performed to remove variants with larger effect estimates for the outcome than the exposure, confirming the true causal direction.51 Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity among IVs, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.43 The MR-Egger method was applied to detect unmeasured horizontal pleiotropy, with P > 0.05 indicating no horizontal pleiotropy. To further reduce the impact of potential pleiotropy on the results, leave-one-out analysis was performed for robustness evaluation. The Steiger test was used to determine whether IVs showed evidence of reverse causality.51 Finally, we performed statistical power analysis.52

Cause

To improve the reliability of the results, we performed validation analysis using the CAUSE method following MR and sensitivity analyses.53 The CAUSE method accounts for both correlated and uncorrelated horizontal pleiotropy while estimating the posterior distribution of causal effects. This method compares a model with a fixed causal effect of zero (the shared model) with a model allowing for a causal association (the causal model). The expected log pointwise posterior density (ELPD, delta_ELPD = ELPD shared – ELPD causal) was used to evaluate the goodness of fit of the two models. A negative delta_ELPD indicates a better fit of the causal model; when delta_ELPD is negative and statistically significant (P < 0.05), the inferred causal effect can be considered robust to horizontal pleiotropy.

MVMR Analysis

We performed MVMR54 to assess whether the observed causal effect of insomnia on sciatica was confounded or mediated by other risk factors. Genetic instruments were derived from European-ancestry GWAS summary statistics, with anxiety, depression and schizophrenia included as covariates. The SNPs used in the MVMR analysis were those exhibiting overlapping associations with both insomnia and confounding factors. For causal estimation in the multivariable analysis, we primarily employed the IVW method,55 which is well-suited for integrating effects of multiple genetic variants on outcomes, providing robust estimation of composite causal effects. In addition, we also used MR-Egger56 and Lasso57 to estimate the MVMR effect.

Heritability Enrichment Analysis

To identify the tissues and cell types most strongly associated with the diseases in this study, heritability enrichment analysis was first performed using the linkage disequilibrium score regression for specifically expressed genes (LDSC-SEG) software.58 This method estimates the genetic contribution of SNPs within genomic regions and compares it with the genetic risk correlation for specific phenotypes, thereby providing a basis for tissue selection in subsequent expression-based analyses.

Multi-Method Combined Gene Prioritization Screening

Numerous genetic variants shape complex traits by regulating gene expression; thus, exploring overlapping genes helps to clarify their underlying causal mechanisms. To identify potential associated genes as comprehensively as possible, the FDR correction was applied in this study, and the significance threshold for TWAS was set at PFDR < 0.05. On this basis, a multi-pronged strategy integrating four complementary methods was further adopted to improve the robustness of the findings.

TWAS

TWAS analysis was performed using the functional summary-based imputation (FUSION) method.59 GWAS summary statistics for sleep trait–associated significant results were integrated with expression weights from the Genotype-Tissue Expression database (GTEx, v8). Expression weight data were obtained from the official FUSION website (http://gusevlab.org/projects/FUSION/#reference-functional-data).

Fine-Mapping

To further refine our candidate gene list and identify the most likely causal genes in regions with multiple signals, the fine-mapping Of causal gene sets (FOCUS) method was employed.60 FOCUS is a fine-mapping approach that uses predicted eQTL weights, LD, and GWAS summary data to estimate credible causal genomic regions from TWAS results. Similar to statistical fine-mapping in GWAS, FOCUS evaluates causality by calculating the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) for each feature within the associated region. A default 90% credible set is constructed based on the sum of PIP values, with the expectation that this set contains the causal feature. A feature with a PIP > 0.9 indicates a higher likelihood of causality compared with other features in the region.

SMR

Summary data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) analysis was performed to examine the causal relationship between gene expression and significantly associated sleep traits, while the integrated HEIDI test helped distinguish genuine causality from confounding effects due to LD. A probe was considered significant if it passed both the SMR threshold (PFDR < 0.05) and the HEIDI threshold (P > 0.05). GTEx v8 eQTL data61 used for SMR analysis were available from the official SMR website (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn).

Colocalization

To verify the hypothesis that gene expression and sleep traits share the same underlying causal genetic variants, Bayesian colocalization analysis was conducted on all significantly associated genes identified in TWAS.62 In this analysis, associated loci with P < 0.05 in TWAS were tested within a 1 Mb window. Generally, a high posterior probability (PPH4 > 75%) indicates the colocalization of GWAS signals and eQTL signals at the specific locus.63

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R software version 4.4.2 (https://www.R-project.org) using the R packages TwoSampleMR (v0.6.8), MendelianRandomization (v0.8.0), MVMR (0.4), coloc (v5.2.3), and locuscomparer (v1.0.0). Additional analytical tools used in this study include LDSC (https://github.com/bulik/ldsc), CAUSE (https://github.com/jean997/cause), LDSC-SEG (https://github.com/omicverse/py-ldsc), SMR (downloaded from its official website: https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr), FUSION (https://github.com/gusevlab/fusion_twas), and FOCUS (https://github.com/bogdanlab/focus). In addition, considering the potential inflation of the overall type I error rate in multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction (P / number of exposures / number of outcomes) or FDR correction was applied to adjust the P-value thresholds for all results, thereby reducing the risk of false positives.

Results of Causal Inference Analysis Based on Summary Effect Estimates

Bidirectional two-sample MR analysis revealed a significant causal relationship only between insomnia and sciatica (with and without lumbago), while no clear causal associations were observed between other sleep traits and any NP subtypes. Therefore, further causal analysis was conducted only for the insomnia-sciatica (with and without lumbago) to validate their causal relationship. CAUSE analysis provided evidence for a causal association of increased risk between insomnia and sciatica (with and without lumbago) (delta_elpd < 0, indicating a better fit of the causal model over the shared model, P = 0.003) (Supplementary Table 21). Causal effect estimation based on 40 IVs showed that a doubling in genetic liability to insomnia was associated with more than a threefold increased risk of sciatica (with and without lumbago) (OR = 3.899; 95% CI: 1.823–8.339). In addition, reverse-causality analysis using CAUSE indicated that genetically predicted sciatica (with and without lumbago) did not increase susceptibility to insomnia (P = 0.067), which was consistent with findings from Mendelian randomization analysis. Causal Effect Estimates of Insomnia on Sciatica in MVMR

To further investigate the association between insomnia and sciatica (with and without lumbago), we performed MVMR analysis and the final IVs are documented in Supplementary Table 23. After adjusting for schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety, insomnia retained a significant causal effect on sciatica (with and without lumbago) (PIVW < 0.05). Results from the other three MVMR methods (Egger, Lasso and median) are similar to those of the IVW method, with detailed findings presented in Supplementary Table 24. In addition, we conducted sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of our results. The heterogeneity Q test based on IVW and Egger regression indicated no significant heterogeneity (P > 0.05), and the Egger intercept test suggested an absence of horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05), with detailed findings presented in Supplementary Table 25.

Determination the Highest Level of Potential Pathogenic Genes

Heritability enrichment analysis of insomnia showed that heritability was mainly concentrated in 13 brain tissues. Based on these findings, TWAS was performed, identifying 1681 significant features corresponding to 682 genes across 13 tissues, among which NMT1 was the most significant (Supplementary Table 27). Conditional analysis uncovered 1180 co-significant features that exhibit co-expression with insomnia-associated genes (Supplementary Table 28). Fine-mapping identified 65 genes with strong causal evidence, including 28 genes with a PIP of 1 (Supplementary Table 29), and 48 genes were consistent with TWAS results. SMR analysis detected 250 probes, of which 153 passed the HEIDI test (Supplementary Table 30). In addition, colocalization testing revealed 408 features from 174 genes that shared causal variants with insomnia (Supplementary Table 31). Finally, among the four methods, six genes—EIF3KP1, MEIS1-AS3, SMIM32, SNCA-AS1, CFAP74, and PPIP5K2—were identified as the most causally relevant and promising candidates derived from five GTEx v8 tissues (Table 1, Figure 6a–f, and Supplementary Table 32). Each key gene exerted distinct effects on insomnia risk. For example, in the cerebellar hemisphere, MEIS1-AS3 was associated with increased insomnia risk, whereas EIF3KP1 significantly decreased risk. In the cerebellum, SMIM32 was linked to elevated risk, while SNCA-AS1 exhibited a protective effect. Based on these results, we scored the identified targets according to the level of evidence (Figure 6g). The highest level included EIF3KP1 in the cerebellar hemisphere and frontal cortex (BA9), and PPIP5K2 in the spinal cord (cervical segment C-1), which showed significant results in all four GTEx v8-based analytical methods, passed the HEIDI test (P > 0.05), and had significant SMR results in the FinnGen data (PFDR < 0.05). The next tier included MEIS1-AS3 in the cerebellar hemisphere, SMIM32 and SNCA-AS1 in the cerebellum, and CFAP74 in the cerebral cortex. Discussion

Comorbidity of NP and sleep disorders is common, yet the underlying genetic mechanism linking them remains unclear, and the level of evidence from previous studies is limited. Based on large-scale public GWAS data, this study employed an integrated multi-dimensional bioinformatics strategy including LDSC, MR, CAUSE and TWAS to dissect their association. Through LDSC analysis of six sleep traits and six NP subtypes, we found a potential genetic correlation among daytime napping, excessive daytime sleepiness, insomnia, and sciatica. To further validate the reliability of these genetic correlations, we performed bidirectional MR analyses combined with CAUSE. Forward MR analyses supported a potential causal effect of insomnia on sciatica, whereas reverse MR detected no significant genetic causal effects of NP subtypes on sleep traits. In reverse MR analyses, we adopted a relaxed threshold of P < 5×10−6 to screen instrumental SNPs for drug-induced polyneuropathy, unspecified neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, and postherpetic neuralgia due to the scarcity of genome-wide significant variants. Strict IVs quality control was performed to avoid false positives and weak instrument bias, including LD clumping, removal of SNPs associated with outcomes (P < 5×10−5), and Steiger test filtering to exclude variants with stronger outcome effects. Variants with F-statistics < 10 were also excluded. The IVW method served as the primary analytical strategy, which further strengthened the stability of the causal estimation results. Power calculation revealed that the MR analysis of insomnia on sciatica achieved 100% statistical power, indicating robust and reliable causal estimates. In contrast, all other phenotypic pairs yielded power below 80%. The relatively small sample sizes of certain NP subtypes may have limited the ability to detect weak causal effects, potentially leading to false-negative results. Pierce BL et al reported that subgroup analyses with merely 10% of exposure participants maintained 90% statistical power relative to full-sample analyses.65 Accordingly, the null findings observed in the NP subgroup with relatively fewer cases analyses still have certain reference value but cannot completely rule out subtle causal associations. Insufficient statistical power constitutes a major limitation of this study. The reliability of MR causal inference relies strictly on three core IVs assumptions.66 We further performed CAUSE analysis to exclude potential horizontal pleiotropy and validate the robustness of the primary MR results.53 Consistent causal estimates were obtained from CAUSE and MR analyses, supporting a potential causal effect of insomnia on sciatica. Insomnia frequently coexists with anxiety,67 depression68 and schizophrenia.69 Nevertheless, MVMR analyses still identified a potential causal effect of insomnia on sciatica after accounting for these comorbid psychiatric confounders.

Each NP subtype originates from disparate nerve injury mechanisms and exhibits distinct heritable genetic backgrounds. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is driven by metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms, characterized by multiple minor susceptibility genes involved in lipid metabolism, oxidative stress and insulin signaling, together with metabolic and vascular risk genes, presenting prominent gene-metabolism interaction effects.70 Drug-induced polyneuropathy is dominated by drug metabolic and neurotoxic susceptibility genes. Its pathogenesis depends on gene-drug environment interaction, and individual genetic polymorphisms determine the threshold of neurotoxic injury. Trigeminal neuralgia shows a clear genetic predisposition with enrichment of major effector genes. Ion channel gene variants cause abnormal neuronal excitability, and autosomal dominant inheritance has been identified in some families.71 Postherpetic neuralgia represents a polygenic susceptibility pattern without major pathogenic genes. It is regulated by immune-neural crosstalk, and its genetic penetrance is triggered by acquired herpes zoster virus infection. Sciatica has low heritability and is associated with minor polygenic variants related to spinal development and intervertebral disc degeneration, which indirectly affect disease occurrence, while mechanical compression serves as the predominant pathogenic factor. Unspecified neuralgia and neuritis represent heterogeneous mixed subtypes without a unified and fixed genetic architectural spectrum. Encompassing multiple phenotypes such as unexplained inflammatory neuralgia and idiopathic peripheral nerve injury, they possess a complex and mixed genetic background with randomly distributed polymorphic loci across multiple pathways, including immune inflammation, nerve repair, and pain perception. Marked disparities in genetic architecture across the six common neuropathic pain subtypes may explain why insomnia exerts divergent causal effects on individual NP subtypes.

We conducted heritability enrichment analysis and found that the heritability of insomnia was predominantly enriched in 13 brain tissues of the central nervous system. We integrated multiple screening methods targeting the central nervous system and identified six candidate pathogenic genes. Among these genes, EIF3KP1 and PPIP5K2 exhibited the strongest genetic evidence, whereas the remaining four (SNCA-AS1, MEIS1-AS3, SMIM32 and CFAP74) lacked supporting validation data from the FinnGen database.

EIF3KP1 is located at 5q21.1 and belongs to processed pseudogenes. It is formed by the retro-transposition and genomic integration of mRNA from EIF3K (19q13.2). Sequence homology analysis shows that the cDNA similarity between EIF3KP1 and EIF3K is approximately 85%-90%.Its biogenesis resembles that of most pseudogenes, involving mRNA reverse transcription and genomic insertion,72 a phenomenon widely observed during biological evolution. Although EIF3KP1 does not encode a functional protein, it can competitively bind miRNAs through a ceRNA mechanism similar to other pseudogenes,73 thereby indirectly regulating EIF3K expression at the post-transcriptional level and playing an important role in gene regulatory networks.74 Given that EIF3K participates in key biological processes including ribosome assembly,75 translation initiation,76 ATP metabolism, and pain signal transduction,77 EIF3KP1 may contribute to neural homeostasis,77 modulation of pain sensitivity,78,79 and energy metabolism regulation75 by fine-tuning EIF3K expression levels. This provides a new potential direction for investigating the genetic regulatory mechanism underlying the comorbidity of insomnia and sciatica.

PPIP5K2 belongs to the family of diphosphoinositol pentakisphosphate kinases (PPIP5Ks) and is also a member of the histidine phosphatase superfamily (His_Pase_clade-2). Its encoded protein is a bifunctional enzyme involved in inositol pyrophosphate metabolism. PPIP5K2 plays essential roles in cellular signal transduction and energy metabolism regulation. A hallmark of this enzyme is its dual kinase and phosphatase activities, which confer a unique regulatory role in maintaining intracellular phosphate homeostasis and mediating signal transduction.80,81 Studies have confirmed that PPIP5K2 participates in regulating cell growth and metabolic processes by modulating intracellular inositol pyrophosphate levels.82 Furthermore, the kinase activity of PPIP5K2 is closely linked to cellular energy metabolism.83 Reduced intracellular ATP levels can induce thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical hyperalgesia,84 ultimately exacerbating symptoms of sciatica. On the other hand, the phosphatase activity of PPIP5K2 catalyzes the hydrolysis of inositol pyrophosphates, thereby enabling fine-tuned regulation of intracellular phosphate levels.85,86 Disordered phosphate metabolism can lead to abnormal deposition of calcium pyrophosphate crystals in the spine and peripheral nerve tissues, causing nerve compression or inflammatory stimulation. Under specific pathological conditions, such crystal deposition may involve the sciatic nerve, thereby triggering or aggravating sciatica.87

SNCA-AS1 is located at chromosomal region 4q22.1 and encodes a ncRNA. SNCA-AS1 has been proven to be associated with Parkinson’s disease,88 dementia89 and Alzheimer’s disease,90 and may participate in aging-related biological processes. Overexpression of SNCA-AS1 leads to the upregulation of both the SNCA gene and its encoded α-synuclein protein.91 Abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein occurs, and pathological α-synuclein oligomers and fibrils spread to the spinal dorsal horn and supraspinal pain-modulating nuclei via transsynaptic pathways. These species activate the microglial NLRP3 inflammasome, excessively potentiate glutamatergic NMDA receptor signaling, and impair the descending pain inhibitory pathway, collectively triggering and sustaining central sensitization characterized by mechanical allodynia and hyperalgesia.92–94 Upregulated SNCA expression further exacerbates this hyperalgesic cascade.95

Current studies on MEIS1-AS3, SMIM32, and CFAP74 remain limited. A literature search conducted in PubMed was performed to summarize the existing evidence regarding these three genes. MEIS1-AS3 is located at chromosomal region 2p14 and encodes a non-coding RNA (ncRNA). MEIS1-AS3 is a CpG site associated with age epigenetic markers, and the chronological age of individuals can be estimated according to its methylation level.96,97 The biological function of MEIS1-AS3 remains unclear, and it is hypothesized that elevated methylation of this locus may contribute to the progression of sciatica. Alamholo et al98 reported that MEIS1-AS3 is involved in temozolomide-resistant glioblastoma. Current MR analyses indicate that MEIS1-AS3 is associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease.99 SMIM32 maps to chromosomal region 5q31.1. It is a protein-coding gene, and its encoded protein is predicted to localize to the cell membrane. As an overweight/obesity-associated gene,100 this gene may be involved in the initiation and progression of hepatocellular carcinoma,100 pancreatic cancer101 and glioma.102 CFAP74 is a protein-coding gene mapped to chromosomal locus 1p36.33. It is predicted to participate in axoneme assembly, and its encoded protein localizes to the cytoplasm, nucleus, and sperm flagellum. It has been confirmed to be associated with spermatogenesis103 and primary ciliary dyskinesia.104 The synergistic regulatory mechanisms of the above genes in the comorbidity of insomnia and sciatica remain to be further elucidated in future studies. Nevertheless, the biological pathways of these genes are merely hypothesized based on published literature rather than confirmed causal mechanisms. Our study only identifies potential genetically associated signals, and in vitro and in vivo experiments are still lacking to validate these inferred pathways. Compared with previous relevant studies, the core strength of this study lies in its integrated multi-method bioinformatics strategy based on large-scale public GWAS data, which effectively avoids the confounding bias and reverse causality of conventional observational researches. We detected a potential causal effect linking insomnia and sciatica. Central nervous system-related genetic mechanisms may mediate their comorbidity, offering guidance for further mechanistic investigations. However, this study has several limitations. First, the GWAS data were mainly derived from individuals of European ancestry, and ethnic differences in genetic background may limit the generalizability of the findings. Further validation using data from other ethnic populations, such as Asians, is warranted. Second, this study was purely bioinformatic and did not incorporate clinical sample testing or basic experiments to verify the specific functions and regulatory pathways of candidate genes. The clinical translational value of the results still requires further confirmation. Third, the two-sample MR method used in this study cannot detect gene-environment interactions; accordingly, we failed to evaluate whether environmental factors modify the genetic correlation between sleep disorders and neuropathic pain. Fourth, owing to the absence of stratified summary statistics categorized by disease course in the original GWAS datasets, we could not stratify and compare genetic causal effects across different disease stages of neuropathic pain. Fifth, a major limitation of this study is the diagnostic bias inherent in registry-based sciatica phenotyping. Despite strict case definition using validated ICD-10 codes (M54.3, M54.4) with exclusion of simple low back pain (M54.5) and discogenic radiculopathy (M51.1), all cases were identified via administrative medical records without standardized lumbar MRI verification. This code-based ascertainment may cause phenotypic misclassification, including incorrect inclusion of patients with isolated low back pain and underrepresentation of mild asymptomatic sciatica cases. Such misclassification and incomplete case capture may attenuate the estimated genetic and causal associations between insomnia and sciatica, leading to potential underestimation of the true effect size. Future studies with uniform lumbar MRI-based case validation are warranted to alleviate this bias. In summary, this study clarifies the positive potential causal relationship between insomnia and sciatica at the genetic level and reveals central nervous system-related genetic mechanisms may mediate their comorbidity. We found key candidate genes including EIF3KP1 and PPIP5K2, providing novel insights for the intervention of comorbid insomnia and sciatica. Owing to the design of the current study, we could not directly demonstrate that insomnia-targeted therapy lowers the prevalence or severity of sciatica. Large-scale prospective clinical interventional studies are required to dissect the relevant mechanisms in the future.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant positive genetic correlation and genetic association between insomnia and sciatica, suggesting insomnia may represent a genetically potential risk factor for sciatica. Genetic mechanisms related to the central nervous system may mediate the development of insomnia-sciatica comorbidity. Two candidate genes, EIF3KP1 and PPIP5K2, were prioritized via genetic analyses. Further cellular and animal experiments are required to validate their potential roles as molecular targets for interventions targeting insomnia-sciatica comorbidity. Collectively, these genetic findings offer potential evidence to inform refined clinical co-diagnosis and management strategies for sciatica and insomnia comorbidities.
