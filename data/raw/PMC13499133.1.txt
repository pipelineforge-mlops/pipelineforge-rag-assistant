Caroli cyst presenting as recurrent biliary colic with cholesterol cholelithiasis: A case report and review of the literature

Caroli disease is a rare congenital disorder characterized by segmental, nonobstructive dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts. Its association with cholesterol cholelithiasis is infrequently reported and presents a diagnostic challenge, particularly when clinical findings and conventional imaging are inconclusive. We report the case of a 45-year-old woman, nonsmoker, with no prior medical or surgical history, who presented with 2 episodes of severe abdominal pain. Initial workup, including abdominal ultrasonography and liver function tests (LFTs), was unremarkable on first presentation, yet LFTs were elevated on both admissions. Repeat ultrasonography remained negative. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) excluded choledocholithiasis but identified a dilated left intrahepatic biliary duct. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) subsequently confirmed cholesterol gallstones in association with a Caroli cyst. The patient underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy with an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged 2 days later in good clinical condition. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of Caroli cysts coexisting with cholesterol cholelithiasis and highlights the critical role of advanced hepatobiliary imaging, particularly MRCP and EUS, when standard investigations fail to explain recurrent biliary symptoms. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for congenital biliary anomalies in patients with unexplained recurrent abdominal pain and elevated LFTs.

Introduction

Caroli disease (CD) is a rare congenital hepatobiliary disorder, first described by Jacques Caroli in 1958, characterized by multifocal, segmental, nonobstructive dilatation of the large intrahepatic bile ducts [1]. The condition belongs to the spectrum of fibropolycystic liver diseases and is closely related to ductal plate malformation during embryological development [2]. Two distinct forms are recognized: the "pure" form (Caroli disease), which involves isolated biliary ectasia without significant hepatic fibrosis, and Caroli syndrome, which is associated with congenital hepatic fibrosis and portal hypertension [3].

The estimated prevalence of Caroli disease is approximately 1 in 1,000,000, with no clear sex predilection, although the syndrome form may be more common in females [4]. Clinically, patients may present with recurrent cholangitis, abdominal pain, jaundice, or, in advanced cases, biliary cirrhosis and cholangiocarcinoma, the latter occurring in up to 7% of cases [5]. The diagnosis is frequently delayed due to the rarity of the condition, variability in clinical presentation, and limited sensitivity of initial imaging modalities such as transabdominal ultrasonography.

The coexistence of Caroli cyst with cholesterol cholelithiasis is a particularly uncommon and underreported clinical entity. The association between biliary stasis within ectatic ducts and the formation of intrahepatic stones, most commonly pigment stones, is well established [6]. However, the concurrent presence of cholesterol gallstones in the gallbladder with a Caroli cyst poses unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, necessitating advanced hepatobiliary imaging for accurate delineation.

We present a case of a 45-year-old woman with recurrent biliary colic and elevated liver enzymes, in whom the diagnosis of a Caroli cyst associated with cholesterol cholelithiasis was established only after multimodal imaging including MRCP and EUS, following 2 inconclusive ultrasonographic examinations. This report aims to enhance clinician awareness of this rare association and to outline an evidence-based diagnostic and management approach.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old woman, nonsmoker, with no documented past medical or surgical history and no regular medications, presented to the emergency department on May 8, 2025 (Day 1) with a chief complaint of severe, acute-onset right upper quadrant (RUQ) and epigastric abdominal pain. The pain was colicky in nature, radiating to the right shoulder, with an intensity of 8/10 on the numeric pain rating scale. The patient denied fever, chills, jaundice, acholic stools, or dark urine. She reported no recent travel, alcohol consumption, or use of hepatotoxic medications.

On physical examination, vital signs were stable: blood pressure 118/76 mmHg, heart rate 88 beats per minute, temperature 37.1°C, oxygen saturation 99% on room air. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the right upper quadrant without guarding, rigidity, or palpable organomegaly. Murphy's sign was equivocal.

Laboratory investigations revealed elevated liver function tests: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 112 U/L (reference range: 7-56 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 98 U/L (reference range: 10-40 U/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 187 U/L (reference range: 44-147 U/L), and total bilirubin 1.9 mg/dL (reference range: 0.1-1.2 mg/dL). Complete blood count and renal function tests were within normal limits. C-reactive protein (CRP) was mildly elevated at 18 mg/L.

Initial abdominal and pelvic ultrasonography performed on admission was unremarkable: the liver demonstrated normal echogenicity and size, the gallbladder appeared thin-walled without evident calculi, the common bile duct (CBD) measured 4 mm without dilatation, and no free intraperitoneal fluid was identified. The patient was managed conservatively with analgesics and intravenous hydration. She showed transient clinical improvement and was discharged home on May 9, 2025 with a plan for outpatient hepatobiliary follow-up.

The patient was readmitted on May 12, 2025 (Day 5) with recurrence of severe right upper quadrant pain, now accompanied by nausea and 2 episodes of vomiting. Vital signs remained stable. Repeat physical examination was consistent with the initial presentation. Inflammatory markers showed a mild rise in CRP to 32 mg/L; liver function tests again demonstrated elevation: ALT 134 U/L, AST 118 U/L, ALP 204 U/L, and total bilirubin 2.1 mg/dL. Serum lipase and amylase were within normal limits, effectively excluding acute pancreatitis.

A second abdominal and pelvic ultrasonography was performed and remained negative for gallstones, biliary dilatation, or any other hepatobiliary pathology. Given the persistent clinical picture, repeated LFT elevation, and inconclusive sonographic findings, advanced hepatobiliary imaging was pursued.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed, which excluded choledocholithiasis and demonstrated a patent common bile duct of 5 mm. However, it revealed a focal, saccular dilatation of the left intrahepatic biliary duct, measuring approximately 18 × 14 mm, with no evidence of upstream stricture or malignant obstruction, a finding consistent with a Caroli cyst. Long-term hepatobiliary surveillance for the Caroli cyst was arranged, including annual MRCP and liver function monitoring, given the associated risk of cholangiocarcinoma and recurrent cholangitis.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge posed by the rare coexistence of a Caroli cyst and cholesterol cholelithiasis in a previously healthy middle-aged woman. Several clinically instructive points merit detailed discussion.

Pathophysiology and classification of Caroli disease

Caroli disease arises from a ductal plate malformation, an arrested remodeling of the embryological biliary ductal plate, resulting in segmental or diffuse dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts [7]. This malformation follows a continuum, with the level of involvement determining the phenotype: malformation at the level of large intrahepatic ducts produces the isolated form (Caroli disease), whereas involvement of smaller interlobular ducts leads to congenital hepatic fibrosis (Caroli syndrome) [3,8].

The Todani classification of bile duct cysts, while primarily applied to extrahepatic choledochal cysts, classifies Caroli disease as Type V, representing communicating multifocal intrahepatic cystic dilatation [9]. Importantly, Caroli disease is distinguished from simple hepatic cysts and other cystic hepatic lesions by its communication with the biliary tree, predisposing patients to biliary stasis, intrahepatic lithiasis, and recurrent cholangitis.

Diagnostic challenges and role of multimodal imaging

A defining feature of this case was the failure of 2 separate transabdominal ultrasonographic examinations to identify either the gallbladder cholesterol stones or the intrahepatic biliary dilatation. This finding is consistent with published literature documenting the limited sensitivity of ultrasonography for Caroli cysts, particularly when the lesion is small, segment-specific, or mimics normal vascular structures [10]. The well-described "central dot sign" on ultrasound, a portal vein radical surrounded by dilated bile duct, while considered pathognomonic for Caroli disease, is frequently absent or misidentified in routine practice [11].

MRCP has emerged as the gold standard noninvasive modality for the diagnosis of Caroli disease, offering superior soft-tissue resolution and direct visualization of biliary-cystic communication without radiation exposure or contrast agent requirement [12]. In this patient, MRCP was instrumental in identifying the segmental left intrahepatic biliary dilatation and excluding choledocholithiasis, which might have otherwise directed the management toward endoscopic biliary intervention.

EUS played an equally critical complementary role. Its superior resolution compared to transabdominal ultrasonography allowed identification of small cholesterol gallstones that had been repeatedly missed on standard imaging [13]. EUS has a reported sensitivity of 97%-99% for cholelithiasis and outperforms conventional ultrasonography for stones smaller than 5 mm [14]. The combined use of MRCP and EUS in this case exemplifies the necessity of a stepwise, multimodal imaging approach when initial investigations fail to account for recurrent biliary symptoms and LFT derangement.

Association between Caroli disease and gallstone formation

The pathophysiological relationship between Caroli disease and intrahepatic lithiasis is well-established and relates to biliary stasis within ectatic ducts, promoting bacterial colonization and secondary precipitation of calcium bilirubinate stones [6,15]. However, the concurrent occurrence of cholesterol gallstones in the gallbladder with a Caroli cyst, as observed in this case, is considerably less documented in the literature. Cholesterol stone formation, driven by bile supersaturation with cholesterol, impaired gallbladder motility, and nucleation factors, is a distinct pathophysiological process from intrahepatic pigment stone formation [16].

Whether Caroli disease directly contributes to altered gallbladder bile composition or motility, thereby predisposing to cholesterol cholelithiasis, remains unclear and warrants further investigation. In this patient, the cholesterol composition of the gallstones was histologically confirmed, and no intrahepatic stones were identified on MRCP or EUS, suggesting the 2 pathologies were concurrent rather than causally linked.

Management considerations

The management of Caroli disease is dictated by the extent of ductal involvement, symptom burden, and presence of complications. In patients with localized, unilobar disease without significant hepatic fibrosis, as in this case, a conservative-surveillance approach is generally advocated, with ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) occasionally employed to reduce biliary lithogenicity and dissolve small stones [17]. Hepatic resection is reserved for patients with recurrent cholangitis localized to a single hepatic lobe or lobe-dominant disease [18].

The decision to proceed with cholecystectomy in this case was appropriate and evidence-based. Symptomatic cholelithiasis in a patient of this age, without contraindications to surgery, carries a well-established indication for laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the current gold standard of care, associated with low morbidity, short hospital stay, and rapid recovery [19]. Intraoperative cholangiography was performed to exclude concomitant CBD stones, in accordance with best practice guidelines.

Long-term surveillance for Caroli cysts is of paramount importance given the significantly elevated risk of cholangiocarcinoma, estimated at 7%-14% over a lifetime, compared to 0.01%-0.46% in the general population [5,20]. Annual surveillance with MRCP and serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) is recommended, along with clinical monitoring for features of cholangitis, hepatic fibrosis, or portal hypertension.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of focal intrahepatic cystic biliary dilatation includes simple hepatic cysts (noncommunicating), choledochal cyst (extrahepatic or intrahepatic), polycystic liver disease, biliary cystadenoma or cystadenocarcinoma, Echinococcal (hydatid) cysts, and biliary hamartomas (von Meyenburg complexes) [21]. The key distinguishing feature of Caroli cysts, communication with the biliary tree, as elegantly demonstrated on MRCP, differentiates them from noncommunicating cystic lesions and dictates appropriate management. MRCP, supplemented by EUS and occasionally endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), remains central to this differentiation.

Conclusion

We present a rare and diagnostically challenging case of a Caroli cyst coexisting with cholesterol cholelithiasis in a 45-year-old woman. The case emphasizes several key clinical lessons: (1) conventional transabdominal ultrasonography has limited sensitivity for both Caroli cysts and small cholesterol gallstones; (2) MRCP is the investigation of choice for suspected biliary cystic lesions and should be pursued early in patients with unexplained recurrent RUQ pain and LFT abnormalities; (3) EUS provides critical complementary information, particularly for gallstone detection; and (4) multidisciplinary hepatobiliary management is essential to address both the acute symptomatic pathology (cholecystectomy) and the long-term surveillance requirements of Caroli disease.

As imaging technologies advance and clinical awareness of rare biliary anomalies increases, earlier diagnosis and more individualized management of Caroli disease will reduce the risks of complications including cholangitis, biliary cirrhosis, and cholangiocarcinoma. This case serves as a reminder that congenital biliary anomalies should remain on the differential diagnosis in any patient with recurrent biliary symptoms unexplained by initial investigations.
