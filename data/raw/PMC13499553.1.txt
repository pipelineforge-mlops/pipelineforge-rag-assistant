Background

Chronic pain is neurophysiologically heterogeneous and may be obscured by group-average EEG metrics. We hypothesized that chronic pain would be characterized by reduced cortical complexity relative to a healthy-control-derived normative reference, defined as the age- and sex-conditioned expected distribution of EEG features, and that larger negative deviations would be associated with greater pain intensity.

Methods

Resting-state EEG from 147 participants, including 78 patients with chronic pain and 69 healthy controls, was analyzed. Complexity was quantified using Higuchi fractal dimension (HFD) across seven scalp regions. A Gaussian process regression normative model was trained exclusively on healthy controls, using age and sex as covariates, to estimate expected HFD values and predictive uncertainty. Individual deviations were expressed as W-scores, with more negative values indicating reduced complexity relative to the normative reference. In patients, regional W-scores were correlated with average pain intensity using Pearson correlations with false discovery rate correction. Robustness was examined after adjustment for age, sex, depression, and anxiety, and spectral power measures were evaluated as exploratory benchmarks.

Results

More negative frontal HFD W-scores were associated with higher pain intensity (r = −0.545, P_FDR < 0.001). Significant HFD–pain associations were observed across multiple regions, with correlation coefficients ranging from −0.441 to −0.545 among FDR-significant HFD features. The parietal association also survived correction (r = −0.523, P_FDR < 0.001). Compared with the examined spectral power benchmarks, HFD-based deviations showed larger descriptive associations with pain intensity. The frontal HFD–pain association persisted after adjustment for age, sex, depression, and anxiety (r = −0.520, two-tailed P < 0.001). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested larger negative latent deviation scores in neuropathic conditions, particularly PNP and PHN.

Conclusion

Normative deviation mapping of EEG complexity may provide a candidate research marker for quantifying inter-individual variability in chronic pain severity. These findings support further validation of HFD-based normative modeling for mechanism-informed stratification, while requiring replication in larger, harmonized, and longitudinal datasets.

Introduction

Chronic pain is fundamentally a disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by maladaptive functional and structural plasticity.1 However, the field’s reliance on conventional case-control designs has obscured the heterogeneity of these neural alterations. By aggregating patients into broad diagnostic categories, traditional analyses succumb to the “average patient fallacy”, generating group-level activation maps that fail to characterize individual pathological signatures.2 This methodological limitation elucidates why, despite the identification of the “pain matrix”, neuroimaging has yet to yield biomarkers capable of guiding precision medicine.3

To address this challenge, we employed Normative Modeling—a statistical framework analogous to pediatric growth charts—to quantify individual deviations from a healthy reference population.4 We integrated this framework with the Higuchi Fractal Dimension (HFD), a non-linear metric of signal complexity. Unlike conventional spectral power analyses, which average neural activity over time and assume local stationarity within analysis windows, HFD quantifies the non-linear and irregular temporal structure of EEG signals, a feature that may be sensitive to pathological loss of dynamical flexibility.5 Grounded in the “loss of complexity” hypothesis, which posits that pathological states manifest as a degradation of physiological flexibility, we predicted that chronic pain is characterized by measurable cortical rigidity relative to age- and sex-conditioned healthy norms.6

Recent advances have begun translating normative modeling from structural neuroimaging to electrophysiological data. In neurodegenerative disease,7 charted normative aging trajectories of EEG spectral power and source connectivity across 14 clinical units, demonstrating that individual deviation scores correlated with motor and cognitive severity and revealing substantial electrophysiological heterogeneity that conventional case-control designs obscured. These findings illustrate that EEG-based normative frameworks can disentangle pathological signatures from normal lifespan variability. Complementing this framework, converging evidence indicates that Higuchi fractal dimension itself follows systematic age-related trajectories across the lifespan—from developmental increases in neural complexity during childhood to inverted U-shaped patterns in adulthood—underscoring the necessity of age-conditioned baselines when interpreting individual patient data.2 Methodological work has further consolidated HFD as a robust non-linear metric for short, non-stationary EEG epochs, with contemporary systematic reviews emphasizing its emergent clinical utility in neurophysiological assessment.8 Despite these parallel developments, normative modeling of EEG complexity has not yet been extended to chronic pain, and whether individualized cortical complexity deviations are associated with symptom severity or vary across nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain mechanisms.

Grounded in the loss-of-complexity hypothesis, the present study tested two specific hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that chronic pain is characterized by individualized negative deviations in cortical HFD relative to age- and sex-conditioned healthy norms, and that the magnitude of these deviations would exhibit a robust inverse association with clinical pain intensity. Second, we examined whether the degree of complexity loss differed across diagnostic subgroups. Given prior evidence that some neuropathic pain conditions may involve somatosensory deafferentation and cortical reorganization, we hypothesized that negative HFD deviations would be more pronounced in neuropathic subgroups than in musculoskeletal or functional pain syndromes. Indeed, functional remapping of the primary somatosensory cortex has been observed specifically in neuropathic, but not nociceptive, states.9 Because normative modeling primarily quantifies individual-level deviations, these subgroup comparisons were considered exploratory and were used to contextualize individual deviation patterns at the diagnostic level rather than to establish definitive group-level mechanisms. By integrating non-linear complexity metrics with individualized normative modeling, this study aimed to advance exploratory, mechanism-informed patient stratification beyond conventional diagnostic boundaries.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Data Source

This study utilized the public chronic pain EEG dataset available via the Open Science Framework (OSF) (https://osf.io/srpbg/).10 All original data acquisition procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the relevant institutional ethics committees. The initial cohort consisted of 189 participants. Following a rigorous quality control protocol (detailed in Section 2.2), 42 participants were excluded due to suboptimal EEG quality, primarily because the proportion of bad channels exceeded 25% or an insufficient number of clean epochs remained after artifact rejection. Consequently, the final analytical cohort comprised 69 healthy controls (HC) and 78 patients with chronic pain. Given that this study utilizes a secondary analysis of a pre-existing public dataset, an a priori power analysis for sample size determination was not applicable. However, the current patient sample size (N = 78) provides a statistical power exceeding 95% to detect the moderate-to-strong effect sizes observed in our primary neurophysiological correlations (eg, r = −0.545 at alpha = 0.05). The robustness of these findings was further substantiated by intensive resampling procedures, as detailed in the following sections.

For stratified analyses, patients were grouped into three mechanistic descriptors based on the original diagnostic labels provided in the dataset (no additional clinical adjudication was performed). The Neuropathic Pain group aggregated Polyneuropathy (PNP), Post-Herpetic Neuralgia (PHN), and neuropathic pain not otherwise specified (NP). The Nociceptive Pain group included Chronic Back Pain (CBP) and Joint Pain (JP), utilized here as proxies for predominantly nociceptive presentations. The Nociplastic/Functional Pain group comprised Chronic Widespread Pain (CWP), a condition commonly associated with nociplastic mechanisms as described by the IASP taxonomy. Given that pain mechanisms may overlap within individuals, these groupings should be interpreted as pragmatic mechanistic descriptors for analysis rather than definitive pathophysiological diagnoses.

EEG Data Preprocessing

Eyes-closed resting-state EEG recordings were used for the present analysis. In the original dataset, brain activity was recorded while participants remained awake and relaxed without performing any specific task. For most participants, two 5-minute continuous resting-state blocks were acquired, one with eyes closed and one with eyes open; during the eyes-open block, participants fixated on a centrally presented cross. Because the eyes-closed condition was available for the full original cohort, the present analysis was restricted to the 5-minute eyes-closed recordings. EEG was recorded using 64 electrodes and a BrainAmp MR plus amplifier (Brain Products, Munich, Germany). All EEG electrodes were referenced online to FCz and grounded at AFz; two additional electrodes were placed below the outer canthus of each eye to monitor eye movements. Data were acquired at a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz with online band-pass filtering between 0.016 and 250 Hz. Resting-state EEG data were preprocessed using a standardized automated pipeline implemented in Python (MNE-Python). Following the standardization of channel locations to the international 10–20 system, a robust algorithm based on the Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) was employed for bad channel identification.11 Specifically, the signal standard deviation was calculated for each channel; channels deviating by more than 4 standard deviations from the robust median across all channels, or those manifesting as flat lines \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage[substack]{amsmath} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage[mathscr]{eucal} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \DeclareFontFamily{T1}{linotext}{} \DeclareFontShape{T1}{linotext}{m}{n} {linotext }{} \DeclareSymbolFont{linotext}{T1}{linotext}{m}{n} \DeclareSymbolFontAlphabet{\mathLINOTEXT}{linotext} \begin{document}$(SD < 1e - 15){\ }$\end{document}, were flagged as bad and subsequently reconstructed using Spherical Spline Interpolation. Data were re-referenced to a Common Average Reference (CAR) to mitigate single-reference bias. A 1–45 Hz bandpass filter was applied, accompanied by linear detrending to remove low-frequency drifts. To further mitigate non-neural artifacts, independent component analysis (ICA) was performed in MNE-Python using the FastICA algorithm. Components attributable to ocular and muscle artifacts were identified according to standard ICA inspection criteria, including component topography, time course, and spectral characteristics. Component rejection was performed manually by a trained investigator without formal blinding to group labels, and artifact-related components were excluded before epoching. Continuous recordings were subsequently segmented into 5-second epochs with a 50% overlap. To accommodate inter-individual physiological variability in signal amplitude, we implemented an adaptive threshold strategy for artifact rejection. For each participant, peak-to-peak amplitudes were computed for every EEG channel within every 5-s epoch. The subject-specific rejection threshold was defined as 1.5 × the 95th percentile of this channel-by-epoch peak-to-peak amplitude distribution and was constrained within 75–200 μV. Epochs were rejected when the peak-to-peak amplitude of any EEG channel exceeded this adaptive subject-level threshold. This strategy was designed to exclude extreme outliers deviating from the subject’s intrinsic baseline, while constraining the threshold within a range of 75–200 μV to prevent the erroneous exclusion of data in low-amplitude recordings.12 Finally, to ensure high data reliability, strict exclusion criteria were enforced: participants were omitted from the final analysis if the proportion of bad channels exceeded 25% of the total scalp electrodes or if fewer than 3 clean epochs remained following adaptive artifact rejection. This clean-epoch criterion was used as an absolute lower-bound exclusion rule to remove recordings with virtually no usable artifact-free data, rather than as the intended amount of data for HFD estimation. In the present analysis, HFD was computed for each retained artifact-free 5-s epoch and then averaged across all retained epochs for each participant. Because each 5-minute eyes-closed recording was segmented into 5-s epochs with 50% overlap, each recording yielded approximately 119 candidate epochs before artifact rejection. Thus, the HFD estimates were derived from the full set of retained clean epochs rather than from the minimum exclusion threshold.

Feature Extraction

Resting-state EEG signals were analyzed within seven anatomically defined Regions of Interest (ROIs): Frontal (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, Fz, F7, F8, FC1, FC2, FC5, FC6), Sensorimotor (C3, Cz, C4), Central-Parietal (CP1, CP2, CP5, CP6), Parietal (P3, P4, Pz, P7, P8), Occipital (O1, O2, Oz, PO3, PO4, PO7, PO8), Lateral Temporal (T7, T8), and Posterior Temporal (TP9, TP10). To examine non-linear cortical complexity alongside conventional linear oscillatory measures, two categories of EEG features were extracted.

Non-linear Cortical Complexity (Primary Metric) The Higuchi Fractal Dimension (HFD) was calculated to quantify the complexity and self-similarity of neural time series. HFD serves as a proxy for the flexibility of local cortical processing. Power spectral density (PSD) was estimated using Welch’s method with a 2-second Hamming window and 50% overlap. These linear features were included as baseline comparators to evaluate the specificity of HFD deviations in tracking pain severity.

Normative Modeling Framework

To disentangle the clinical heterogeneity of chronic pain from normal physiological variability, we employed a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) framework to establish a healthy neural benchmark.13 Given that neural characteristics often follow non-linear trajectories across the lifespan, GPR—as a non-parametric Bayesian method—offers superior adaptability compared to linear regression. Models were trained exclusively on the healthy control cohort (N=69). For every EEG feature within each ROI, a separate GPR model was fitted to predict the normative distribution, utilizing age and sex as biological covariates. To capture non-linear physiological trends while accommodating observational noise, the covariance structure employed a combination of a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel and a White Noise kernel. Hyperparameters were automatically optimized within the training set by maximizing the log-marginal likelihood. Model calibration was evaluated within the healthy control cohort using leave-one-out cross-validation. In each fold, one healthy control participant was held out, the GPR model was trained on the remaining healthy controls, and the held-out participant’s W-score was computed from the predicted normative mean and predictive uncertainty. Calibration was assessed by examining whether the resulting control W-scores were centered near zero, showed approximately unit variance, and yielded an expected proportion of extreme values. Kernel hyperparameters were optimized within each training fold by maximizing the log-marginal likelihood, and model adequacy was evaluated based on this cross-validated calibration rather than in-sample fit alone. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.10.19 (scikit-learn and SciPy libraries). To evaluate the linear relationship between neural deviations and clinical severity, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between ROI-wise W-scores and clinical variables. To ensure clinical specificity and avoid confounding by group membership, correlation analyses were restricted to the chronic pain patient cohort (N = 78). To rigorously control for false positives when testing across multiple anatomical ROIs, P -values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (q < 0.05). To further isolate the specific neurophysiological association between cortical complexity deviations and pain severity from affective comorbidities, partial correlation analyses were performed to reassess the HFD–pain association while controlling for age and sex, and then additionally controlling for depression (BDI) and anxiety (STAI). All primary inferential tests, including Pearson correlations, partial correlations, Fisher’s exact tests, and subgroup comparisons, were evaluated using two-tailed tests unless otherwise specified.

To characterize deviation patterns beyond group means, abnormality enrichment was quantified using a conservative threshold (W < −1.96). Differences in abnormality prevalence between patients and controls were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test (two-sided), with effect sizes reported as odds ratios (OR). When a zero cell count occurred, ORs were computed using the Haldane–Anscombe correction, in which 0.5 was added to each cell of the 2×2 contingency table to obtain finite odds ratio estimates in the presence of zero-frequency cells. To identify latent multivariate patterns of brain–behavior covariation rather than relying solely on isolated univariate metrics, Partial Least Squares Canonical Analysis (PLS-CCA; n_components = 1) was performed. The predictor matrix X comprised pre-specified ROI-wise HFD W-scores, whereas the response matrix Y included pain intensity, pain duration, BDI, and STAI. Five-fold cross-validation was used to estimate the out-of-sample stability of the latent brain–behavior association. In each fold, the PLS-CCA model was fitted on the training set and then applied to the held-out fold; the correlation between the held-out brain and clinical latent scores was calculated and summarized across folds. Cross-validation was used as a robustness assessment of the latent association rather than as an additional feature-selection procedure. To ensure that the primary neurophysiological associations were robust and not driven by extreme outliers or specific sample distributions, their stability was further validated using non-parametric bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) and permutation testing (1,000 label shuffles). Finally, to evaluate whether the magnitude of cortical complexity loss differed significantly across pain etiologies, Exploratory diagnostic subgroup analyses were performed to examine whether latent brain deviation scores varied across the original diagnostic labels. Because several diagnostic subgroups were small, these analyses were interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Group differences were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests.

To improve reproducibility, fixed random seeds were used for all stochastic procedures. A seed value of 42 was applied to ICA initialization, cross-validation splitting in the PLS-CCA analysis, bootstrap resampling, and permutation testing. Deterministic procedures, including HFD feature extraction, correlation analyses, FDR correction, ANOVA, and Tukey’s HSD tests, did not require random seeds.

To improve reproducibility, all analyses were conducted using Python 3.10.19. EEG preprocessing was performed using MNE-Python, normative modeling and multivariate analyses were implemented using scikit-learn, and statistical analyses were conducted using SciPy. The custom analysis scripts used for EEG preprocessing, feature extraction, normative modeling, and statistical analyses are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Loss of Cortical Complexity Tracks Pain Severity

The primary discovery of this study is the robust association identified between neural rigidity and clinical pain burden. Specifically, frontal and parietal HFD deviation scores (W-scores) exhibited significant negative correlations with pain intensity (r range: −0.523 to −0.545, P < 0.001; Figure 2, Table 3). This suggests that as pain severity escalates, activity within the frontoparietal network becomes increasingly regular and less complex. Crucially, as illustrated by the feature hierarchy in Figure 3, HFD-based metrics consistently occupied the top ranks among the examined neural deviation features. Compared with the spectral power benchmarks included in this analysis, HFD-based deviations showed larger descriptive associations with pain intensity. Beyond univariate associations, multivariate PLS analysis based on ROI-wise HFD W-scores revealed a latent brain–behavior pattern linking distributed cortical complexity deviations with clinical symptom burden (R = 0.538, P < 0.001; Figure 4). Five-fold cross-validation provided a preliminary estimate of out-of-sample stability for this pattern (mean cross-validated R = 0.42, SD = 0.27). Because the fold-to-fold variability was relatively large, this multivariate association should be interpreted cautiously and requires validation in independent cohorts.

Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that latent brain deviation scores varied across the original diagnostic labels (Table 6 and Figure 5). More negative mean scores were observed in neuropathic subgroups, particularly PNP (N = 4) and PHN (N = 3). Discussion

This study used an age- and sex-conditioned normative modeling framework to quantify individualized deviations in resting-state EEG complexity in chronic pain. By training Gaussian process regression models on healthy controls, we derived HFD-based W-scores that indexed the extent to which each participant’s cortical complexity deviated from the expected healthy range. The principal finding was that more negative frontoparietal HFD deviations were associated with greater pain intensity in patients with chronic pain, and this relationship persisted after accounting for affective symptoms. Multivariate analyses further suggested that distributed complexity deviations covaried with clinical symptom burden. In addition, exploratory subgroup analyses indicated that negative latent deviation scores were more pronounced in some neuropathic subgroups. These subgroup findings, however, should be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional design and small subgroup sample sizes.

The widespread reduction in HFD observed in this study provides a useful entry point for interpreting the findings within the loss-of-complexity framework. According to this framework, healthy biological systems—whether heart rates, gait patterns, or neural oscillations—often exhibit high variability, fractal organization, and irregular temporal structure, which may reflect a broad repertoire of functional states and flexible adaptation to changing physiological demands.14 The healthy brain can similarly be viewed as a multiscale dynamical system, integrating information across different temporal and spatial scales to generate EEG activity with irregular and scale-dependent properties.15 Conversely, disease and aging have been associated with reduced physiological complexity across multiple systems.16,17 Under pathological stress or disrupted regulatory input, physiological signals may become more regular and less flexible, which can be reflected by reductions in fractal dimension and related complexity metrics.18 In this context, the negative HFD deviations observed in patients with greater pain intensity are consistent with a restriction of cortical temporal dynamics, although the present cross-sectional data cannot determine whether reduced complexity contributes to pain persistence or reflects ongoing pain-related processes.

In the present study, the negative HFD deviations observed in chronic pain patients are consistent with the loss-of-complexity framework, but they should not be interpreted as direct empirical validation of this theory. The inverse association between HFD deviations and pain intensity suggests that greater subjective pain burden was accompanied by lower resting-state cortical signal complexity. This pattern may reflect a restriction of cortical temporal dynamics, in which EEG activity becomes more regular and less variable than expected for a given age and sex. Similar reductions in EEG complexity have been reported in other neurological disorders; for instance, Alzheimer’s disease is characterized by EEG complexity degradation associated with cognitive decline.17 However, the present study did not include cognitive or attentional tasks, and therefore cannot determine whether reduced EEG complexity contributes to attentional disengagement, cognitive symptoms, or the persistence of pain perception. Thus, the interpretation that chronic pain reflects a low-complexity or low-entropy neural state remains a hypothesis for future work rather than a conclusion directly demonstrated by the present data.14

While primarily exploratory, the mapping of individualized frontoparietal rigidity may offer preliminary insights for future clinical translation. Diagnostically, extracting HFD-based normative deviations represents a potential pathway to support mechanism-informed patient stratification. If validated in larger longitudinal cohorts, identifying reproducible cortical complexity deviation patterns in neuropathic subgroups might eventually assist in differentiating these states from nociplastic syndromes, providing an objective complement to subjective clinical phenotyping. Therapeutically, the identification of altered frontoparietal complexity patterns may help generate hypotheses about potential spatial targets for non-invasive neuromodulation, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation or transcranial direct current stimulation. Rather than implying immediate clinical application, future neurostimulation studies could test whether modulating these networks is associated with restoration of non-linear dynamical flexibility. This perspective aligns with recent literature emphasizing fractal dimension and complexity metrics as candidate translational measures that may inform neuromodulation and clinical assessment in chronic pain, pending further validation.19,20

Crucially, our partial correlation analysis supports the specificity of the HFD–pain association relative to affective symptoms. After controlling for depression and anxiety scores, the association between frontal HFD deviations and pain intensity showed only minimal attenuation. This finding suggests that reduced HFD was not readily explained by comorbid affective distress alone. Nevertheless, because depression and other psychiatric conditions may also be associated with altered neural complexity, the present results should be interpreted as evidence for a pain-related association rather than as proof that cortical rigidity is an intrinsic or causal signature of nociceptive or neuropathic injury.

Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that latent Brain Scores differed across diagnostic labels, with more negative values observed in PNP and PHN than in several other subgroups. Rather than indicating a confirmed deafferentation hierarchy, these findings should be interpreted as a preliminary diagnostic subgroup pattern. Patients with neuropathic conditions such as PNP and PHN may involve structural nerve injury and altered somatosensory input, which could plausibly contribute to cortical reorganization; however, the present resting-state scalp EEG data did not directly measure deafferentation, thalamic activity, or thalamocortical mechanisms.21,22 Accordingly, the subgroup pattern cannot establish a causal pathway from peripheral nerve injury to cortical complexity loss.

In contrast, CWP, which is generally considered a nociplastic/functional pain condition rather than a nociceptive condition, showed a positive mean Brain Score in this dataset. This finding suggests that CWP did not follow the same negative-deviation pattern observed in PNP and PHN, but it should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a distinct pathophysiological mechanism, particularly given the exploratory nature of the subgroup analysis and the small sample sizes in several diagnostic groups. Prior work has linked fibromyalgia and nociplastic pain to central sensitization and altered cortical excitability,23,24 but the present study did not directly assess these processes. Thus, the observed subgroup differences should be regarded as hypothesis-generating evidence that HFD-based normative deviations may vary across chronic pain diagnoses, rather than as proof that cortical rigidity is a specific biomarker of neural injury.21,23,24

The exploratory subgroup pattern also highlights a broader methodological issue in chronic pain research: clinically defined pain categories may contain substantial neurophysiological heterogeneity. In this context, normative modeling provided a useful way to quantify patient-level deviations from age- and sex-conditioned healthy norms rather than relying only on group averages. Traditional case-control studies often assume that the average pain patient is representative of the broader clinical population, an assumption that may be problematic in chronic pain. For example, the more negative HFD deviations observed in PNP and PHN, together with the positive mean Brain Score observed in CWP, suggest that averaging across all patients could obscure clinically meaningful deviation patterns. Such heterogeneity may partly contribute to inconsistent neuroimaging findings in chronic pain research. However, because we did not perform a formal head-to-head comparison with conventional case-control analysis, these findings should be interpreted as supporting the utility of individualized deviation mapping rather than proving its superiority.

Furthermore, individualized deviation mapping provides a useful framework for interpreting cortical complexity in relation to normal demographic variability. Chronic pain cohorts are inherently heterogeneous, and EEG features such as oscillatory power and HFD may vary systematically with age. By training a GPR model on healthy controls, the present analysis estimated age- and sex-conditioned normative ranges and used these ranges to quantify patient-level deviations. This age-conditioned framework is physiologically relevant because HFD has been shown to vary systematically across the lifespan. Recent normative investigations across wide developmental windows suggest that HFD reflects large-scale integrative brain dynamics that change with maturation and aging.25 Moreover, emerging work indicates that age- and pathology-related variation in HFD may capture dynamical information that is not fully reflected by conventional oscillatory power measures, including changes that may be anti-correlated with spectral power and 1/f slope.26 In this context, the present feature hierarchy should be interpreted as a descriptive finding rather than evidence that HFD is statistically superior to spectral power. Together, these findings support the rationale for using age-conditioned normative models when interpreting EEG complexity measures. The resulting W-scores should therefore be viewed as model-based estimates of deviation from the expected healthy range, rather than as pure measures of pathology, and may help identify neurophysiological patterns that could be obscured in group-mean analyses.27

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based on a secondary, cross-sectional public dataset. Although the sample size was reasonable for resting-state EEG research, the diagnostic subgroup analyses involved small and uneven groups, particularly for PHN, PNP, NP, and JP. These subgroup findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence for a definitive mechanistic classification of chronic pain. Larger independent cohorts with harmonized diagnostic phenotyping will be needed to test whether the subgroup patterns observed here are reproducible. Second, the present data do not provide direct evidence for deafferentation, thalamic hyperpolarization, or thalamocortical dysrhythmia. Resting-state scalp EEG can characterize cortical temporal dynamics, but it cannot directly measure thalamic activity or establish a causal pathway from peripheral nerve injury to cortical complexity loss. Similarly, no cognitive or attentional tasks were administered, so the present findings cannot determine whether reduced HFD relates to cognitive symptoms, attentional disengagement, or other clinical features often reported by patients with chronic pain. These mechanisms remain plausible directions for future multimodal studies rather than conclusions supported directly by the current dataset. Third, although the normative modeling framework allowed individual deviations to be quantified relative to age- and sex-conditioned healthy norms, the analysis remains dependent on the size and composition of the reference cohort. A larger and more diverse normative reference sample would improve the precision of predicted normative ranges and support stronger evaluation of patient-level deviation scores. Because the present analysis used a public dataset, external validation in independently acquired cohorts will also be important to assess generalizability across recording systems, acquisition protocols, and preprocessing pipelines. Conclusions

We established an age- and sex-adjusted normative model of resting-state EEG to derive individualized deviation maps of cortical complexity in chronic pain. The primary finding was that reduced HFD-based cortical complexity, particularly within frontoparietal regions, was associated with greater pain intensity, with the strongest association observed for frontal HFD W-scores (r = −0.545), and remained evident after adjustment for affective comorbidities. Exploratory subgroup analyses further suggested that cortical complexity loss was not uniformly distributed across pain states, with larger negative deviations observed in some neuropathic subgroups, whereas CWP showed a positive mean Brain Score. These subgroup findings remain hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted cautiously given the small subgroup sample sizes and cross-sectional design. Although resting-state EEG offers a scalable and accessible framework for studying pain-related neurophysiological variability, the present study did not establish diagnostic accuracy, test-retest reliability, or incremental value over existing clinical or neuroimaging-based pain assessments. Future longitudinal and harmonized multi-site studies should test whether individualized EEG complexity deviations can predict clinical trajectories, treatment response, or help identify candidate targets for non-invasive neuromodulation therapies.
