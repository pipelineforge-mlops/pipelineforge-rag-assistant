Abstract

High‐dose chemotherapy (CT), followed by autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (auto‐HCT) or consolidation CT, are postremission strategies for European LeukemiaNet 2022 (ELN2022) favorable‐ or intermediate‐risk patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in complete remission (CR) without measurable residual disease (MRD). We conducted a retrospective, nonrandomized analysis to compare the efficacy and safety of auto‐HCT versus CT in newly diagnosed AML patients aged 18–65 years treated within two recent HOVON–SAKK–Nordic trials. From a total of 1005 patients with ELN2022 favorable‐ or intermediate‐risk AML, 224 received busulfan/cyclophosphamide (Bu/Cy), followed by auto‐HCT, and 199 received mitoxantrone/etoposide‐based CT. The 5‐year relapse‐free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were comparable between auto‐HCT and CT (RFS: 60% vs. 56%, P = 0.70; OS: 72% vs. 71%, P = 0.76). In multivariable analysis, auto‐HCT was not associated with a significant difference in RFS (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.56–1.13, P = 0.20) or OS (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.60–1.37, P = 0.64). Median time to platelet and neutrophil recovery was shorter with auto‐HCT than CT (platelet: 42 vs. 57 days, neutrophil: 13 vs. 39 days). Subgroup analysis revealed that patients achieving CR after the second induction cycle or with baseline white blood cell counts >20 × 109/L had longer RFS with auto‐HCT compared to CT (HR 0.17, P = 0.003 and HR 0.48, P = 0.02), which was not observed for OS. Focusing on ELN2022 favorable‐risk patients (excluding NPM1mut MRD‐positive) and MRD‐negative intermediate‐risk patients, RFS and OS were not different between consolidation strategies. Collectively, auto‐HCT yielded similar RFS and OS to mitoxantrone/etoposide‐based CT in favorable‐ and intermediate‐risk AML, particularly in MRD‐negative patients, with faster hematologic recovery.

INTRODUCTION

Treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains challenging, with disease relapse being the most common cause of treatment failure, despite recent therapeutic advances. 1 Achieving and maintaining complete remission (CR) following induction therapy are critical for improving long‐term outcomes, 2 , 3 but the majority of patients in CR are still at high risk of relapse without effective postremission therapy (consolidation and/or maintenance), 4 , 5 highlighting the ongoing need for innovation in AML treatment strategies. Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo‐HCT) is generally considered the preferred postremission treatment for patients with adverse‐risk AML or persistent measurable residual disease (MRD). 6 , 7 , 8 However, the preferred postremission therapy for European LeukemiaNET (ELN) 2022 favorable‐risk patients (excluding NPM1mut MRD‐positive) or MRD‐negative intermediate‐risk patients remains uncertain. For these patients, two postremission treatment strategies are commonly considered: high‐dose chemotherapy (CT) and/or radiotherapy as myeloablation, followed by autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (auto‐HCT) or consolidation CT. 8

Auto‐HCT has been associated with lower relapse rates due to its strong anti‐leukemic effect with relatively low nonrelapse mortality (NRM), although with considerable toxicity and morbidity. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Conversely, CT is considered a well‐established alternative with potentially less toxicity. A clinical trial by Vellenga et al. randomized between these two strategies and found a prolonged relapse‐free survival (RFS) in the auto‐HCT arm, whereas the overall survival (OS) between the two therapeutic approaches was similar, likely due to higher rates of salvage with allo‐HCT in the CT arm. 12 However, this clinical trial was conducted before the adoption of modern molecular risk stratification systems and the routine use of MRD assessment in treatment decision‐making. As a result, it provides limited evidence for a direct comparison between auto‐HCT and CT in the contemporary treatment landscape.

To address this gap, we compared the efficacy and toxicity of auto‐HCT versus CT in ELN2022 favorable‐ or intermediate‐risk patients from two recent randomized multicenter clinical trials from the Dutch–Belgian Hemato‐Oncology Cooperative Group (HOVON), the Swiss Group for Clinical Cancer Research (SAKK), and the Nordic AML group. 13 , 14

METHODS

Study population

Patients with newly diagnosed and previously untreated AML who received intensive induction CT within the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic 102/30‐09 and 132/30‐13 trials were considered for this analysis. 13 , 14 The HOVON–SAKK–Nordic 102/30‐09 trial recruited patients between 2010 and 2013, and the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic 132/30‐13 trial recruited patients between 2015 and 2017. These trials randomized patients aged 18 to 65 years to two cycles of standard 7 + 3 induction CT with or without experimental drugs. In both trials, all patients were scheduled to receive two induction cycles, irrespective of remission status after the first cycle (obligatory double induction). In HOVON–SAKK–Nordic 102/30‐09, the first induction cycle consisted of 12 mg/m2 idarubicin for three days and 200 mg/m2 cytarabine for 7 days (with or without clofarabine), and the second induction cycle consisted of 120 mg/m2 amsacrine for 3 days and intermediate‐dose cytarabine (IDAC) for 6 days (with or without clofarabine). In HOVON–SAKK–Nordic 132/30‐13, the first induction cycle consisted of 12 mg/m2 idarubicin for 3 days and 200 mg/m2 cytarabine for 7 days (with or without lenalidomide), and the second cycle consisted of 60 mg/m2 daunorubicin for 3 days and IDAC for 6 days (with or without lenalidomide). FLT3 inhibitors, other novel therapies, and gemtuzumab ozogamicin were not used in the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic clinical trials as part of the protocol. In both HOVON–SAKK–Nordic trials, consolidation treatment after composite complete remission (CRc, defined as CR or CR with incomplete recovery‐CRi) was determined according to a predefined risk‐adapted algorithm. In the 102/30‐09 trial, favorable‐risk AML patients were planned for CT, whereas intermediate‐risk patients were planned for allo‐HCT if a matched donor and age criteria allowed. If allo‐HCT was not an option, these patients underwent auto‐HCT or CT (in case auto‐HCT was not feasible). In the 132/30‐13 trial, favorable‐risk patients were scheduled to receive auto‐HCT or CT (if auto‐HCT was not possible). Intermediate‐risk patients with negative MRD after cycle 2 were also directed to auto‐HCT (or CT if auto‐HCT was not possible), and those without MRD data were planned for allo‐HCT. Patients underwent auto‐HCT with busulfan (oral 1 mg/kg every 6 hours or intravenous 0.8 or 0.6 mg/kg on Days −7 to −4, depending on the protocol) and cyclophosphamide (60 mg/kg on Days −3 and −2), or received CT with mitoxantrone (10 mg/m2/day) and etoposide (100 mg/m2/day) for 5 days (Figure S1 and Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). In both trials, a minimum CD34+ cell yield of 2 × 106 cells/kg for auto‐HCT was recommended per protocol.

A total of 1751 patients were enrolled in the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic 102/30‐09 and 132/30‐13 clinical trials. Of these, 179 patients (10%) were excluded because of a diagnosis of high‐risk myelodysplastic syndrome or mixed‐phenotype acute leukemia, and 567 patients (33%) were excluded as they did not meet ELN2022 favorable‐ or intermediate‐risk AML criteria. A total of 1005 patients (57%) were classified as favorable or intermediate risk according to the ELN2022 criteria. 8 Among them, 909 patients (90%) were in a CRc, which was achieved after one or two cycles of intensive induction CT. Of those in CRc, 423 received postremission treatment with either auto‐HCT (n = 224) or CT (n = 199), while 350 underwent allo‐HCT and 136 received no further treatment (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3).

Definitions and endpoints

MRD was assessed after the second induction cycle from bone marrow samples using multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC, sensitivity <0.1%) and/or quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) for NPM1 (sensitivity <10−4), as previously described. 15

For this analysis, we defined two subgroups. The first comprised all patients with ELN2022 favorable‐risk AML, excluding those with NPM1mut MRD‐positive disease, for whom allo‐HCT is the recommended consolidation strategy. The second group consisted of intermediate‐risk patients who were MRD‐negative, as assessed by MFC and/or quantitative RT‐qPCR for NPM1. Further subgroup analysis included ELN2022 risk classification, age groups, number of cycles to CR, and baseline white blood cell (WBC) count.

The primary endpoint was relapse‐free survival (RFS), measured from the start of postremission treatment until relapse or death, whichever occurred first. The secondary endpoints included OS (calculated from the start of postremission treatment until death by any cause), cumulative incidence of relapse and NRM, and toxicity parameters. Cumulative incidences of relapse and NRM were estimated with NRM and relapse as competing events. Response to treatment was defined previously. 13 , 14 The safety and toxicity of auto‐HCT and CT were evaluated by comparing the cumulative incidence of neutrophil (>0.5 × 109/L) and platelet (>50 × 109/L) recovery, days of hospitalization, and frequency of grade 3–5 adverse events (AEs). Blood count recovery for the CT group was measured from the start of CT consolidation, and in the auto‐HCT group from the day of HCT infusion.

Statistical analysis

RFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the log‐rank test was used to compare survival estimates, whereas Gray's test was used when competing events were compared. The median follow‐up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. To identify independent predictors of RFS and OS, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was developed, stratified by trial number and adjusted for age, number of induction cycles to CR, ELN2022 risk group, 8 WBC count at diagnosis, and MRD status. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and compared using either χ 2 or Fisher's exact test, when appropriate. For continuous data, the median and the interquartile range (IQR) were reported and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test to assess differences between groups. Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐sided P < 0.05, with confidence intervals (CIs) calculated at a 95% level. R version 4.2.2 or higher was used to perform statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Patient population characteristics

The median age of the auto‐HCT and CT cohorts was similar (50 [IQR: 41–57] and 52 years [IQR: 41–58], respectively) (Table 1). The WBC count at diagnosis was significantly higher in the auto‐HCT cohort compared with the CT cohort (>20 × 109/L: 47% vs. 31%, P < 0.001, respectively). The vast majority of patients (97%) were clinically defined as de novo AML, and most patients (71%) were in the ELN2022 favorable‐risk category. The most frequently mutated genes in both auto‐HCT and CT groups were NPM1 (58% vs. 51%, P = 0.83, respectively), DNMT3A (32% vs. 29%, P = 1.00, respectively), NRAS (21% vs. 23%, P = 0.33, respectively), and FLT3 (either tyrosine kinase domain mutations [TKD] or internal tandem duplications [ITD], 27% vs. 28%, P = 0.43, respectively, Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). The prevalence of core‐binding factor leukemias was comparable between the two cohorts (22% vs. 21%, P = 0.81, Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). Normal karyotype in ELN2022 intermediate risk was similarly found among the consolidation groups (89% versus 80%, P = 0.20, respectively). The median follow‐up for the entire cohort is 99 months (IQR: 85–119).

Stem cell collection and transplantation characteristics

The median number of CD34+ cells collected among auto‐HCT recipients was 5.4 × 106/kg (IQR: 3.4–8.2 × 106/kg), with no significant difference between the two trials. Seven patients had suboptimal CD34+ cell yields (<2 × 106/kg), ranging from 1.26 to 1.90 × 106/kg. Two patients received bone marrow as a graft source. In total, 32 patients failed to achieve adequate CD34+ cell collection and therefore proceeded to CT. These patients were classified according to the consolidation therapy actually received in this as‐treated analysis. It is noteworthy that RFS and OS were not significantly different between patients who failed mobilization versus those who directly proceeded to CT (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5).

Treatment outcomes

Patients with ELN2022 favorable and intermediate risk who received postremission treatment with auto‐HCT had a 5‐year RFS of 60 ± 3% compared with a 5‐year RFS of 56 ± 4% for CT (P = 0.70, Figure 1A). In multivariable analysis, predictors linked to a shorter RFS were ELN2022 intermediate‐ versus favorable‐risk category, two cycles versus one cycle of induction CT to achieve CR, WBC > 20 × 109/L versus ≤20 × 109/L, and MRD versus no MRD. In contrast, the type of postremission treatment was not significantly associated with RFS (HR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.56–1.13, P = 0.20, Table 2).

OS estimates were comparable between the two postremission treatments (5‐year OS for auto‐HCT: 72 ± 3% versus CT: 71 ± 3%, P = 0.76, Figure 1B). Also, in the multivariable model, auto‐HCT did not improve OS compared with CT (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.60–1.37, P = 0.64, Table 2). In contrast, patients who were in the intermediate ELN2022 risk category, who needed two induction cycles to obtain CR, had MRD, or were aged ≥60 years were associated with worse OS (Table 2). Both cumulative incidence of relapse and NRM were not significantly different between auto‐HCT and CT (5‐year cumulative incidence of relapse: 36% vs. 41%, P = 0.31, respectively; 5‐year cumulative incidence of NRM: 4% vs. 3%, P = 0.21, respectively, Figure 2).

Subgroup analyses

Auto‐HCT was associated with significantly improved RFS and OS among patients aged ≥60 years (n = 38 auto‐HCT and n = 36 CT), a difference not observed in the <40 and 40–59 age categories (Supporting Information S1: Figures S6 and S7). However, no difference between the two consolidation strategies was observed among the age groups after adjustment for covariates (Figure 3 and Supporting Information S1: Figure S8).

Patients with a late CR, that is, entering CR following two induction cycles, or patients with WBC counts >20 × 109/L at diagnosis had improved RFS and OS with auto‐HCT (Supporting Information S1: Figures S9–S12). After adjustment for age, WBC count at diagnosis, ELN2022 risk group, and MRD status, and stratifying by trial, achievement of late CR (after two induction cycles) was strongly associated with improved RFS in patients receiving auto‐HCT compared with CT (HR 0.17; 95% CI: 0.05–0.55; P = 0.003, Figure 3). In the same multivariable model, auto‐HCT significantly improved RFS compared with CT in patients with WBC count >20 × 109/L (HR 0.48; 95% CI: 0.26–0.87, P = 0.02, Figure 3). These findings in favor of auto‐HCT were not observed in the multivariable analysis for OS (Supporting Information S1: Figure S8).

ELN 2022 risk groups and MRD status

MRD assessments after two cycles of induction were available in 283 (67%) patients (Table 1). In patients with ELN2022 favorable risk (excluding NPM1mut MRD‐positive, as these patients typically proceed to allo‐HCT), outcomes were not significantly different between auto‐HCT and CT recipients (5‐year RFS: 67 ± 4% vs. 62 ± 5%, P = 0.80; 5‐year OS: 79 ± 4% vs. 78 ± 4%, P = 0.81, respectively, Figure 4). Specifically, for core‐binding factor (CBF) leukemia (Supporting Information S1: Figure S13) and for favorable NPM1 + MRD‐negative patients (Supporting Information S1: Figure S14), no significant differences were observed between auto‐HCT and CT. Similarly, no significant difference was observed between auto‐HCT and CT in patients with ELN2022 intermediate‐risk AML without MRD (5‐year RFS: 53 ± 8% vs. 39 ± 10%, P = 0.38; 5‐year OS: 71 ± 7% vs. 59 ± 10%, P = 0.21, respectively, Figure 5). After adjusting for age, the number of induction cycles to obtain CR, and WBC count, and stratifying by trial, auto‐HCT was not significantly associated with different outcomes compared with CT for RFS (ELN2022 favorable risk excluding NPM1mut MRD‐positive patients: HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.40–1.29, P = 0.26; ELN2022 intermediate‐risk MRD‐negative patients: HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.45–1.73, P = 0.71) and OS (ELN2022 favorable risk excluding NPM1mut MRD‐positive patients: HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.42–1.80, P = 0.71; ELN2022 intermediate‐risk MRD‐negative patients: HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.29–1.55, P = 0.35).

Safety and toxicity analysis

Patients who underwent auto‐HCT as postremission treatment had faster neutrophil recovery and platelet recovery compared with CT recipients (median: 13 vs. 39 days for neutrophil recovery, P = 0.003, and 42 vs. 57 days for platelet recovery, P = 0.007, respectively, Supporting Information S1: Figure S15). The median duration of hospitalization was also shorter for auto‐HCT recipients compared with those receiving CT (median 23 vs. 29 days, P < 0.001). The 32 patients in whom mobilization of sufficient CD34+ cells did not occur and consequently received CT instead of auto‐HCT showed prolonged hematologic recovery (neutrophil recovery 51 days [IQR 37–59], platelet recovery 84 days [IQR 54–185] for patients with mobilization failure versus neutrophil recovery 38 days [IQR 27–49] and platelet recovery 55 days [IQR 36–110] for patients initially planned to receive CT).

The most prevalent grade 3–5 AE was febrile neutropenia, occurring in 37% of patients receiving auto‐HCT and 33% of patients undergoing CT (P = 0.41). Oral mucositis was more frequent in the auto‐HCT cohort (12%) compared with the CT cohort (5%, P = 0.01), while pneumonia was more frequent in the CT cohort (8%) compared with the auto‐HCT cohort (3%, P = 0.048). Despite the longer time to platelet recovery in the CT cohort, grade 3–5 bleeding events were rare and similar between groups (2.2% vs. 2.0% for auto‐HCT and CT, respectively, P = 1.00). Infection‐related deaths (pneumonia or other infectious causes) were not significantly different in auto‐HCT and CT recipients (4.9% and 8.5%, respectively, P = 0.17). Other grade 3–5 AEs were also generally equally distributed between the two cohorts (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The preferred postremission strategy in favorable‐ and intermediate‐risk AML patients remains a subject of ongoing investigation. A previous randomized HOVON–SAKK trial showed improved RFS and similar OS in patients who received auto‐HCT compared with CT as a postremission strategy. 12 We analyzed postremission treatment with auto‐HCT and CT in two recent large multicenter HOVON–SAKK–Nordic clinical trials, with a median follow‐up of 8 years and comprehensive NGS data allowing classification according to ELN2022 risk categories. Auto‐HCT and CT had comparable RFS and OS in both favorable‐ and intermediate‐risk AML patients, whereas subgroup analysis showed that patients who needed two induction cycles to obtain CRc and patients with WBC count >20 × 109/L at diagnosis benefited from auto‐HCT. Regarding toxicity, CT was associated with more frequent grade 3–5 pneumonia and longer hematologic recovery and hospitalization, and auto‐HCT was associated with more severe oral mucositis. These data indicate that both treatments provide high RFS and OS in the current treatment era, particularly in patients without MRD, while no superior postremission treatment can be determined.

In line with previous studies, 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 a recent meta‐analysis has shown that patients who received auto‐HCT had significantly better RFS, but not OS, compared with those who received CT. 20 Another recent propensity‐scored matched analysis from the PETHEMA AML registry found no significant difference in OS and RFS between the two modalities. The study, however, reported an RFS benefit with auto‐HCT for CBF AML as well as for MRD‐negative patients, a finding that we did not observe in our analysis. 21 A Japanese clinical trial found that OS and disease‐free survival were not significantly different between the two postremission strategies. 22 Following the results of the randomized HOVON–SAKK trial, which demonstrated higher RFS with auto‐HCT versus CT as postremission treatment, the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic group adopted a policy favoring auto‐HCT for patients in the ELN2022 favorable‐ and intermediate‐risk groups. However, it should be noted that in the randomized trial, RFS did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06) and that the benefit of auto‐HCT was most pronounced in the favorable cytogenetic risk subgroup. Since the publication of that trial in 2011, advances in molecular risk stratification and the integration of MRD assessment in clinical decision‐making may have influenced treatment strategies and outcomes. The difference in the impact of auto‐HCT on RFS between our cohort and the randomized trial should be interpreted in the context of these developments, especially given the comparable cohort sizes. Our findings suggest that the RFS benefit of auto‐HCT may be diminished when molecularly based risk stratification and individualized treatment approaches are implemented. Multivariable analysis indicated that certain subgroups—patients with a high WBC count at diagnosis and those requiring two cycles of intensive CT to achieve CR—experienced better RFS after auto‐HCT compared with consolidation CT. However, currently, these less CT‐sensitive patients should also be considered for allo‐HCT as postremission treatment instead of a CT‐based strategy.

The CT consolidation regimen used in the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic group, consisting of mitoxantrone and etoposide, could be debated, as consolidation with cytarabine in an intermediate‐dose (IDAC) or high‐dose (HDAC) regimen is predominantly used by most cooperative groups. Numerous studies report that multiple cycles of HDAC may not confer additional benefit over standard‐ or IDAC cytarabine regimens. 12 , 23 , 24 A recent large randomized trial conducted by the ALFA and FILO groups showed noninferior outcomes in patients aged 18–60 years with IDAC or HDAC consolidation CT, with OS estimates similar to those observed in our study with mitoxantrone and etoposide. 25 Similar to our findings, that study also reported higher efficacy for intensified consolidation CT in patients who were refractory to the first cycle of induction CT, which, however, did not translate into prolonged OS. Moreover, in the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic trials, most patients received IDAC as part of a second induction cycle, which effectively served as consolidation for the majority. Consequently, in our cohort, consolidation therapy in most patients was administered after prior exposure to IDAC. Overall, given the lower toxicity profile and the absence of randomized comparisons between regimens, consolidation strategies such as mitoxantrone/etoposide or IDAC are reasonable treatment options, although comparative efficacy data are lacking.

The study is limited by its retrospective, nonrandomized design with inherent uncertainty regarding treatment allocation between the two consolidation strategies. In addition, several subgroups, such as the ELN2022 intermediate‐risk MRD‐negative patients, were relatively small, limiting statistical power for some subgroup analyses. Selection bias related to stem cell mobilization may have influenced group allocation. Patients with deeper CT responses and better disease clearance might be more likely to experience mobilization failure, potentially shifting more favorable patients toward the CT group. 26 However, mobilization success is multifactorial and can be affected by age, marrow reserve, and prior lines of therapy. Furthermore, although patients in whom mobilization of sufficient CD34+ cells did not occur showed delayed neutrophil and platelet recovery, suggestive of reduced hematopoietic reserve, the overall outcomes did not differ between patients with and without mobilization failure. Therefore, the direction and magnitude of bias introduced by mobilization failure remain uncertain. Another limitation is the imbalance in MRD status after cycle 2, with a higher proportion of MRD‐negative patients in the auto‐HCT cohort. Although multivariable analyses adjusting for MRD status and subgroup analyses restricted to MRD‐negative patients were performed, residual confounding related to MRD‐guided consolidation allocation cannot be fully excluded. With respect to MRD assessment, NPM1‐mutated AML was evaluated using bone marrow, whereas emerging evidence suggests that peripheral blood NPM1 MRD after consolidation may provide superior prognostic risk stratification, particularly for OS and allo‐HCT decision‐making in CR1. 27 Finally, patients were treated before the widespread availability of targeted therapies (e.g., FLT3 inhibitors), other novel therapies, and before the reintroduction of gemtuzumab ozogamicin, which may limit generalizability to current clinical practice.

In conclusion, high‐dose CT, followed by auto‐HCT, has been adopted by the HOVON–SAKK–Nordic and other groups in their trials as recommended postremission treatment for patients with favorable‐risk AML (excluding NPM1mut MRD‐positive) or intermediate‐risk AML without MRD. We found comparable outcomes with auto‐HCT or CT, with a benefit for auto‐HCT observed only in relatively small subgroups. Therefore, auto‐HCT and CT should be considered comparable consolidation strategies, with treatment decisions guided by patient characteristics, comorbidities, and institutional preferences. Ongoing prospective studies for patients with FLT3 (HOVON 156/AMLSG 28‐18/NCT04027309) or IDH (HOVON 150/AMLSG 29‐18/NCT03839771) mutations might again address the added value of auto‐HCT in the context of targeted drugs.
