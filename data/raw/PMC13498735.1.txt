Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a major problem in the United States, impacting 5% of American adults. Ensuring medication adherence is of vital importance, as lower adherence to cardiovascular medications is associated with higher risk of cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with CAD. Many adults in the United States have poor health literacy, contributing to difficulty comprehending their conditions and potentially the importance of medication adherence. While the EPA recommended grade level for patient education materials (PEMs) is below an eighth grade level, most are written at a much higher level than this, which may cause disparity in patients’ level of understanding. The purpose of our review was to benchmark the reading levels of several widely used PEMs to determine if they fell within the recommended guidelines and to discuss the potential role of pharmacists in medication education and adherence. Twenty PEMs relevant to CAD were gathered and their readability was analyzed using the software on Microsoft Word to determine the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) of each PEM. It was determined that 85% of the PEMs ranked higher than the current recommendation, indicating that work needs to be done to ensure adequate patient understanding and adherence. Through pharmacist involvement, industry-wide awareness, and collaborative efforts, adherence to the readability standard can be achieved, improving health outcomes and medication adherence in patients with CAD.

Background

According to the CDC, approximately 1 in 20 (5%) American adults over the age of 20 are impacted by coronary artery disease (CAD), costing the healthcare system over $200 billion annually. 1 CAD is characterized by a buildup of plaque within the arteries of the heart, which can lead to blockages causing myocardial infarctions. The standard of care for CAD typically includes the prescription of medications for the management of blood cholesterol, and a recommendation to adjust lifestyle factors. For those with comorbidities, it is typical to use medications for the management of high blood pressure, blood glucose, and others.2-4 For instance, in 2022, prescribers wrote 32 million prescriptions for a statin. 5 Ensuring medication adherence is of vital importance, as lower adherence to cardiovascular medications is associated with higher risk of cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with CAD. 6 However, nonadherence to medications for CAD is a significant problem affecting a large percentage of patients. A study done to assess adherence to CAD medications found that 43.5% of patients failed to adhere to at least one of their medications. 7 This deficit in adherence may lead to poorer outcomes, unnecessary healthcare costs, and deaths.

Many adults in the US have poor health literacy, contributing to difficulty comprehending their conditions and potentially underestimating the importance of medication adherence. 8 Once informed of a cardiac diagnosis, many patients may turn towards academic and research organizations, medication manufacturers, and search engine queries to educate themselves. Currently, the FDA does not require the content of medication guides, the resource intended for patient reference and dispensed with each medication fill, to meet a specific reading level. The only requirement in Part 208 of Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations is that the medication guide must be written in nontechnical, understandable language. 9 While it is necessary for medication guides to thoroughly explain the implications of the drug, often making it difficult to avoid the use of medical jargon if synonymatic words do not exist, this resource should be comprehensible to the majority of those utilizing it. Several organizations, including the National Institutes of Health, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, American Medical Association, Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, and the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), provide recommendations for plain writing for clear communication, with most organizations recommending that patient education materials (PEMs) be written at or below a sixth grade reading level and not to exceed an eighth grade reading level.10-14 However, according to the National Literacy Institute, 54% of adults have a literacy below a 6th grade level, 20% of which are below a fifth grade level. 15 This clearly shows a shortfall on behalf of the health system, impacting patients’ ability to comprehend their condition, manage their treatment, and take ownership of their health.

The lack of patient adherence can possibly be attributed to patients’ not understanding the importance of taking the medication due to the high reading level in which patient education materials are written.16,17 Health literacy is a modifiable risk factor for morbidity and mortality. 18 As pharmacists, this presents an opportunity to improve patient outcomes by encouraging drug manufacturers, providers, and educational and government organizations to make their PEMs more readable. The healthcare team can improve their efforts in enhancing the accessibility of healthcare for those with low health literacy, while adapting their approach to align with each patient’s specific needs, circumstances, and understanding.

The purpose of our review was to benchmark the reading levels of several widely used PEMs to determine if they fell within the EPA recommended guidelines of not exceeding an eighth grade reading level and to discuss the potential role of pharmacists in medication education and adherence. 12

Methods

Identification of sample PEMs, that the CAD patient population was most likely to use, was done by conducting a internet search engine search for basic information regarding the disease state, commonly prescribed medications, and education material. We prioritized the review of sources an average patient may identify as readily accessible which included organizations, such as academic, government sponsored, and pharmaceutical manufacturers. We selected all 20 sources based on organizational source and title prior to reading or analyzing the articles’ readability scores. Medication related PEMs were selected based on the 2023 American Heart Association treatment guidelines for Chronic Coronary Disease. 4

Upon compiling the sources that the patient population was mostly likely to use, the software on Microsoft Word was used to determine the readability of each PEM. The primary outcome was the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL). The secondary outcome was the Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES). An FRES of 100 indicates complete ease of reading for any individual. The Flesch-Kincaid standard for readability recommends a score between 70-80. 19 While both FKGL and FRES are calculated using the average sentence length and the average syllables per word, the weight of variables differ between the formulas. Therefore, while a PEM may meet recommendations for the FKGL they may not meet recommendations for the FRES and vice versa.

Discussion

Current guidelines for plain writing recommend that PEMs should ideally be written at no more than an eighth grade reading level and have a FRES of 70-80.12,19 Of the PEMs, 85% ranked higher than the current grade level recommendation and 100% did not meet the FRES recommendation, indicating that there may still be significant comprehension challenges. Given the results of our analysis, it is clear that this fundamental component of education has fallen short to providing patients with the knowledge they need to understand their disease, adhere to their medication regimen, and feel empowered to manage their health. Lowering the reading level to match the guidelines requires collective awareness, acknowledgement, and action across the entire healthcare industry. Health organizations, drug manufacturers, and federal agencies must be made aware of this shortcoming and begin to evolve their standards to match the recommendations.

The high reading level of PEMs associated with CAD poses many threats to the US healthcare system, contributing further to low health literacy among patients. It has been proven that low health literacy levels are directly linked with poor outcomes, including medication nonadherence, decreased use of preventative services, hospitalizations and excessive use of emergency care, higher morbidity and mortality, and increased costs. 39 Hospitals are becoming overcrowded and overburdened, which lessens the time that HCPs are able to spend with each patient, making it difficult to confirm that the patient is fully aware of the burden of their disease and the importance of taking their medications. 40 Upon discharge, many patients rely on PEMs to answer their questions and provide them with the information which was unable to be clearly conveyed in the inpatient setting. Because many PEM’s grade levels exceed the national average and the NIH’s recommendation, patients may be left confused, discouraged, and noncompliant, further feeding the cycle.

Prior to our analysis, a similar study was conducted examining the readability of PEMs for cholesterol management, including websites, medication information, guides to nonpharmacological interventions, and more. 39 This study identified an adequate reading level to be fifth to sixth grade on the Flesch-Kincaid scale, and found that 21 of the 22 PEMs fell outside of this range. 39 The average grade level was determined to be 11.9 ± 2.59, greatly exceeding the national average and current recommended guidelines. 39 An additional study examined PEMs from the top 25 pediatric orthopedic institutions and assessed the readability using the Flesch-Kincaid grade level and FRES, among others. 41 The average grade level was found to be 9.3 ± 2.5, and the average FRES was 48.3 ± 16.2. 41 The similarity of results to our study further substantiates that PEMs have a higher than recommended reading level, and may potentially impact the outpatient management of other diseases, like diabetes, asthma, and depression.

The Flesch-Kincaid reading assessments are limited by being unable to evaluate the style of writing, format of the PEM, inclusion of pictures and graphical displays of information, and personal factors such as individual motivations and perception of the content. 42 Future research may include newer, more complex formulas which allow for the incorporation of pictures. Such algorithms may allow for a more accurate determination of readability level and better delineate the disparities.

In developing PEMs that align with readability recommendations, it is important to acknowledge that CAD and associated treatments have complex pathophysiologies and mechanisms. There is a fine balance between attaining patient readability and addressing the inherent complexity of this disease state. It is possible that additional research in this area may be able to identify a justifiable balance.

As it will take time to do further research into the readability disparity and encourage an industry-wide shift towards better accommodating the patient population, immediate action can be taken by pharmacists in both the inpatient and outpatient setting. To help mitigate the impacts of PEM readability exceeding recommendations, limited health literacy in the U.S., and preventable exacerbations of CAD, pharmacists can provide counseling, comprehensive medication reviews, and education to patients. Based upon various randomized controlled trials assessing the impacts of pharmacist interventions on cardiovascular disease medication adherence, it has been proven that face-to-face counseling, phone calls, automated voice messages, and assessment of patient knowledge and barriers have significantly improved patient adherence compared to the control group.,43-47

To ensure patients have an adequate understanding of their medications, face-to-face conversations and direct communication should be employed by pharmacists to assess and educate patients at their level of health literacy.

Conclusion

The findings and assessment have demonstrated the readability disparity and need for the critical examination of the current development of these important materials. Our finding of the variability of PEM grade level indicated that work needs to be done to ensure adequate patient understanding and adherence. Through pharmacist involvement, industry-wide awareness, and collaborative efforts, adherence to the readability standard can be achieved, improving health outcomes and medication adherence in patients with CAD.
