ABSTRACT

Objectives

The role of Hemovac drainage after TKA remains uncertain in the tranexamic acid (TXA) era, particularly across patient subgroups. This study investigated whether body mass index (BMI) modifies the association between drainage use and blood loss in patients undergoing TKA.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study compared 201 TKA patients divided into a drain group (n = 101) and a non‐drain group (n = 100). All procedures incorporated standardized intravenous and intra‐articular administration of tranexamic acid. Primary outcomes included hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), erythrocyte count, and estimated blood loss (EBL). Secondary outcomes were hospital stay duration, knee range of motion, and complications. Between‐group comparisons were performed using independent‐samples t‐tests and chi‐squared tests, and multiple linear regression assessed the interaction between BMI and drain use on EBL.

Results

No significant between‐group differences were observed in Hb, Hct, or erythrocyte count at baseline or on postoperative Day 1. In patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, EBL was significantly lower in the drain group compared to the non‐drain group (p = 0.044). Regression analysis revealed a significant interaction between drain use and BMI (p = 0.013). Patients without drains showed an increase in EBL with BMI, whereas the drain group demonstrated a relatively flat relationship. Secondary outcomes were comparable between groups.

Conclusion

Hemovac drains were associated with modestly lower EBL in patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 undergoing TKA. No differences in clinical outcomes were observed. These findings suggest that the effect of drainage may vary across patient subgroups and support a more individualized approach.

In tranexamic acid–treated primary total knee arthroplasty, estimated blood loss increased with rising BMI when no drain was used but remained stable across the BMI range with Hemovac drainage. These findings support a BMI‐based, selective use of drains rather than routine placement.

1 Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis is a leading cause of chronic pain and functional disability worldwide, with its prevalence and associated burden increasing substantially over recent decades [1, 2, 3]. Consequently, the demand for total knee arthroplasty (TKA) procedures has steadily risen [4]. Drainage after TKA is an ongoing topic of debate in orthopedic surgery [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Historically, closed‐suction drainage has been used after TKA to evacuate postoperative blood, reduce hematoma formation, and facilitate wound management. However, recent trends show a shift toward performing TKA without drains [11]. Tranexamic acid (TXA) has been well established to reduce perioperative blood loss in TKA [12, 13, 14]. A combination of intravenous (IV), and intra‐articular (IA) administration of TXA was significantly associated with reduced blood loss in TKA compared with IV or IA alone [15, 16]. Additional perioperative strategies, such as drain clamping in conjunction with TXA, have also been reported to further reduce blood loss [17, 18, 19]. However, the role of postoperative drainage in the TXA era remains controversial, and no consensus has been reached regarding its routine use.

Although TXA substantially reduces perioperative blood loss after TKA, the additional benefit of Hemovac drainage remains uncertain. The effect of drainage may vary across patient subgroups rather than being uniform across the entire TKA population. We therefore examined whether Hemovac drainage was associated with overall perioperative blood loss, whether BMI modified this association, and whether any observed difference was accompanied by differences in short‐term clinical outcomes.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Study Design and Participants

This single‐center retrospective cohort study included patients with end‐stage OA who underwent primary TKA between January 2022 and December 2023. Eligible patients received the standardized combined IV and IA TXA protocol and had complete perioperative clinical and laboratory data. Both varus and valgus knees undergoing standard primary TKA were included. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital (approval No. KMUHIRB‐E(I)‐20240303). Patients with prior ipsilateral knee surgery, bilateral procedures, or conditions that could significantly affect perioperative blood loss (such as known coagulation disorders or ongoing anticoagulation therapy requiring perioperative management) were excluded. The cohort size was determined by the number of consecutive eligible patients during the study period after applying the predefined exclusion criteria.

All TKA procedures were performed through a medial parapatellar approach with tourniquet application using a conventional mechanical‐alignment technique. An intramedullary guide was used for distal femoral resection and an extramedullary guide for proximal tibial resection in both groups. All patients received a standardized combination of IV and IA TXA [20]. A fixed dose of 500 mg IV TXA was administered prior to skin incision and tourniquet inflation. In addition, 500 mg of IA TXA, diluted in 20 mL of normal saline, was injected directly into the joint space after capsule closure and prior to wound closure. According to institutional protocol, TXA was not administered in patients with known contraindications, including a history of thromboembolic events or hypersensitivity; such patients were not included in the present study. No protocol deviations were noted during the study period.

Following a change in institutional protocol in March 2023, routine Hemovac drainage was discontinued. No patient‐specific or intraoperative indications were used to determine drain placement within either study period. This protocol change resulted in two naturally defined groups: (1) TXA treatment with a Hemovac drain (TXA with drain group) and (2) TXA treatment without a Hemovac drain (TXA without drain group). For patients in the TXA with drain group, drains were inserted intraoperatively, clamped for 3 h post‐wound closure, and removed on the first postoperative day.

Both groups underwent identical surgical indications, operative techniques, perioperative management, and postoperative rehabilitation. All procedures were performed by the same surgical team, although not by a single surgeon. Importantly, tourniquet release timing was determined by institutional protocol and was strictly linked to group allocation, with no variation within each group. As a result, tourniquet release timing was fully collinear with the use of drains. In the TXA with drain group, the tourniquet was routinely released after skin closure. In contrast, in the TXA without drain group, the tourniquet was released after joint capsule closure but before skin closure. This study design enabled a controlled comparison of outcomes between patients who received TKA with and without Hemovac drains while maintaining consistency in patient care.

2.2 Data Collection and Outcome Assessment

Baseline demographic data collection included age at surgery, sex, and BMI. Laboratory parameters were assessed preoperatively and postoperatively, including hematocrit (Hct), hemoglobin (Hb), and erythrocyte count. Blood volume estimation utilized Nadler's formula based on the Hb balance method [21]. Height and weight were measured in meters and kilograms, respectively. Estimated blood loss (EBL) was calculated using Meunier's Hb dilution formula, a previously validated Hb‐based method that has been commonly used for perioperative blood loss estimation in arthroplasty research [22, 23]. This formula depends on previously calculated blood volumes and preoperative and postoperative Hb. Postoperative Hb values were obtained on the first postoperative day. No patients received blood transfusion prior to these measurements. The length of hospital stay was measured at discharge, and the final range of motion (ROM) was assessed during the 6‐week outpatient department follow‐up. Postoperative complications such as cellulitis and periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) were reviewed from patients' charts.

2.3 Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25, IBM Corp., USA) and R (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Categorical variables were compared using Chi‐squared tests. Continuous variables were analyzed using independent‐samples t‐tests under the assumption of approximate normal distribution, as assessed by visual inspection of Q–Q plots.

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine the association between BMI, drain use, and perioperative EBL, including a BMI‐by‐drain interaction term. The model was specified a priori to evaluate the interaction between BMI and drain use. BMI was analyzed both as a categorical variable using the WHO‐defined threshold (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) and as a continuous variable in the regression model [24]. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3 Results

3.1 Patient Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Baseline demographic characteristics, including sex, age, and BMI, were comparable between the TXA with and without drain groups (Table 1). Valgus knees accounted for 7 of 101 patients in the drain group and 8 of 100 patients in the no‐drain group, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.773). Similarly, laboratory parameters, including preoperative and postoperative Hb, Hct, and erythrocyte count levels, showed no statistically significant differences between groups. The primary outcome measure of EBL was comparable between groups in the overall analysis. Moreover, the length of hospital stay and final postoperative knee ROM demonstrated no significant intergroup differences.

3.2 Overweight Patients Showed Lower Blood Loss With Drain Usage

In subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, and BMI, only BMI showed a consistent pattern suggesting differential drain effects on EBL. Based on the WHO classification, adults with a BMI of 25 kg/m2 or higher are considered overweight [24]. Our cohort included 162 patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (Table 2). Among these patients, no significant differences were noted in demographic data, preoperative laboratory results, postoperative ROM, or hospital stay. However, the patients in the TXA with drain group had significantly less EBL than those in the TXA without drain group when their BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (mean difference: −0.069 L, 95% CI: −0.135 to −0.002, p = 0.044).

3.3 Significant Interaction Between BMI and Drain Use on Blood Loss

Multiple linear regression confirmed a statistically significant BMI‐by‐drain interaction (p = 0.013, 95% CI: −0.028 to −0.003). While this subgroup result was based on a predefined WHO threshold, BMI was further analyzed as a continuous variable to formally test for an interaction with drain use across the full BMI range. Figure 1 presents a scatter plot with fitted regression lines illustrating the interaction between BMI and Hemovac drain use on EBL in patients receiving TKA. In the without drain group, EBL increased progressively with BMI, showing a clear positive slope across the BMI range. In contrast, the drain group demonstrated a relatively flat relationship between BMI and EBL. The two regression lines intersected at approximately BMI 24–25 kg/m2, beyond which patients without drains showed a marked upward trend in EBL.

Based on the regression model coefficients, the estimated relationship between BMI and EBL differed by drain use (Table S1). For the drain group, the equation was EBL (L) = 0.409 – 0.001 × BMI (kg/m2), indicating a relatively flat relationship between BMI and EBL. In contrast, the TXA without drain group followed EBL = 0.018 + 0.015 × BMI, indicating a progressive rise in EBL with higher BMI.

3.4 Low Rate of Postoperative Complications in Both Groups

No wound‐healing complications were observed in either group. In the drain group, one patient developed cellulitis‐like lower‐extremity erythema and swelling 1 month after surgery; the wound was well healed, with no evidence of joint infection, and antibiotics alone were given. In the no‐drain group, one patient developed periprosthetic joint infection 10 months after TKA, diagnosed based on clinical, laboratory, and microbiological findings, and underwent debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention.

4 Discussion

The routine use of closed drainage for TKA has been suggested for many decades, but growing clinical evidence has failed to show any advantage of postoperative drainage. The current literature suggests no significant variance between utilizing drainage and abstaining from it [8, 25]. In line with current literature, we found no overall advantage of Hemovac drainage across our patient cohort. However, Hemovac drainage was associated with lower EBL in patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, even with TXA use. This modest reduction in EBL did not translate into detectable differences in the clinical outcomes assessed in this study. Tourniquet release timing was fully collinear with drain use, representing a key limitation. Nevertheless, a BMI‐dependent interaction pattern was observed, supporting a more individualized approach to postoperative management.

4.1 The Impact of BMI on Surgical Outcomes

BMI has been shown to impact blood loss following TKA [26]. Higher BMI levels are usually associated with increased blood loss during and after the surgery. This correlation can be attributed to several factors, including increased surgical complexity due to larger soft tissue mass, greater surgical exposure required, and potential difficulties in achieving hemostasis. Patients with higher BMI may also have underlying comorbidities such as hypertension or diabetes, which can further complicate the surgical process and increase the risk of bleeding. Therefore, surgeons often consider BMI when planning TKA procedures, as it may influence the surgical approach, implant selection, and postoperative management strategies to minimize blood loss and optimize patient outcomes. In the present study, Hemovac drain usage was associated with a relatively flat relationship between BMI and EBL, without the increasing trend observed in the non‐drain group. These findings suggest that BMI may be an important factor to consider when evaluating perioperative blood management strategies in TKA.

4.2 Tourniquet Protocol Considerations

Prior studies have demonstrated that the timing of tourniquet release in TKA can influence the distribution of blood loss between the intraoperative and postoperative phases, without consistently affecting total blood loss or transfusion requirements [27, 28, 29, 30]. Early release may facilitate intraoperative hemostasis under direct visualization, although reported benefits in pain and functional recovery remain inconsistent [31, 32].

In our study, tourniquet release timing was protocol‐driven and fully collinear with drain use, precluding independent assessment of their effects. As such, the observed BMI‐dependent pattern should be interpreted as a combined effect of drainage and tourniquet management rather than an isolated effect of drainage. Prospective studies with independent control of tourniquet timing and drainage are needed to clarify these relationships.

4.3 Clinical Implications and Functional Outcomes

The traditional rationale for postoperative drainage includes the reduction of hematoma formation and soft tissue swelling, theoretically facilitating earlier functional recovery. However, our analysis demonstrated no significant differences in knee ROM between the drain and non‐drain groups, consistent with contemporary literature [33].

In patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, Hemovac drainage was associated with a modest (~70 mL) reduction in EBL. Although small, it should be interpreted in current TKA practice, where TXA treatment alone already substantially reduces blood loss (~400–500 mL) [34, 35]. While our findings suggest a measurable blood‐sparing effect in selected patients, its clinical relevance remains uncertain, as no differences were observed in the outcomes assessed. Given the limited sensitivity of the available endpoints, potential subclinical effects (e.g., hematoma formation or inflammatory response) cannot be excluded. Prospective studies using more sensitive, patient‐centered endpoints are needed to clarify its significance.

The impact of drainage on hospitalization duration remains controversial. While some investigators have reported associations between drain usage and extended hospital stays [33], others have demonstrated shorter stays in non‐drain protocols [36]. Notably, a large retrospective study by Chen et al. involving 1660 patients found no significant difference in hospitalization duration between groups [37]. Our findings align with these latter observations, showing no significant effect of IA drainage on the length of hospital stay. This consistency in recent studies suggests that drainage status may not be a primary determinant of hospitalization duration in contemporary TKA protocols.

4.4 Strengths and Study Limitations

The strengths of this study include a standardized combined IV and IA TXA protocol, consistent perioperative management, and formal assessment of the BMI‐by‐drain interaction. This study also has several limitations. First, its retrospective design limited control over procedural variables, including protocol‐dependent tourniquet release timing, and key clinical variables (e.g., blood pressure, comorbidities, medication use, operative time, and anesthesia‐related factors) were unavailable, introducing potential residual confounding. Second, EBL was estimated using an Hb‐based method, which may be affected by perioperative fluid shifts and hemodilution; it should therefore be interpreted as an indirect estimate [23, 38]. Third, patient‐reported outcome measures were not collected, limiting the ability to assess the broader impact of treatment. Fourth, subgroup analyses in patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 may be underpowered. Fifth, all procedures used the same conventional mechanical‐alignment workflow. As different alignment workflows may influence perioperative bleeding, our findings should be interpreted within this specific surgical context and may not be directly generalizable to other cutting‐guide or technology‐assisted alignment systems [39]. Valgus knees represented only a small proportion of the cohort; therefore, the findings may also not be directly generalizable to complex or severe valgus TKA [40]. Finally, as all patients received TXA, generalizability to other TXA strategies or to patients without TXA may be limited.

5 Conclusion

We found no significant differences in EBL, postoperative knee ROM, or length of hospital stay between patients undergoing primary TKA who received TXA with or without Hemovac drainage. However, in patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, the use of Hemovac drainage was associated with modestly lower EBL. These findings suggest that the effect of drainage may vary across patient subgroups, particularly by BMI, supporting a more individualized approach. Prospective studies are needed to determine the clinical relevance of this observation.
