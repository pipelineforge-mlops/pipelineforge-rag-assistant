Background

Oesophageal foreign-body impaction is a frequent emergency that is typically managed with endoscopy. In resource-limited and remote health facilities, strict adherence to standard algorithms is impractical. Context-adapted clinical reasoning is sometimes critical for ensuring safe and timely management.

Case Presentation

We report a case of a 7-year-old male child who presented to a remote health facility with acute dysphagia and odynophagia following accidental ingestion of a padlock confirmed on neck radiography. In the absence of endoscopic services, the padlock was successfully removed under direct laryngoscopy with Magill forceps under general anesthesia. The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged 24 hours after observation.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of pragmatic clinical judgment and adaptive decision making in low-resource settings. When standard endoscopic services are unavailable, carefully selected alternative approaches can save life.

Introduction

Foreign bodies (FB) in the gastrointestinal tract are more common in children and remain a surgical emergency with potential for fatal complications.1–5 This poses a significant economic burden to healthcare system.6,7 Standard management strongly favors endoscopic retrieval because of its high success rate and low complication rate.8 Flexible esophagoscopy remains the safest method of upper digestive tract foreign body extraction.9 However, many remote health facilities lack immediate access to flexible or rigid endoscopes.10,11 Given this situation, clinicians working in these environments are forced to consider alternative options to endoscopy for foreign body removal.11

The balance between patient safety and resource constraints demands rational evidence-informed improvisation. Magill forceps with the aid of a direct laryngoscope has been propped as a safe and effective method for proximal esophageal foreign body removal.9,12 Reports illustrating safe alternative strategies in low-resource settings remain limited. However, there is a scarcity of studies on ingested padlocks and limited literature on safe alternatives to endoscopy for proximal esophageal foreign bodies in low resource settings.

We present a case of successful retained oesophageal padlock removal performed at a remote facility, underscoring the importance of clinical rationality beyond rigid adherence to standard algorithms.

Case Presentation

A 7-year-old boy presented to the emergency unit of a remote hospital with a more than 24-hour history of painful swallowing, foreign body sensation in the throat, and inability to tolerate solid food following accidental ingestion of a padlock.

There was no history of respiratory distress, hematemesis, or prior esophageal disease. His vaccination status was unknown.

Clinical Findings

On examination, the patient was conscious, not pale, jaundiced, and not dyspneic but asthenic. The patient presented with hypersalivation. His mouth had a few scratches and his neck was flexible.

There were vesicular breath sounds with a partial oxygen saturation of 98% on room air, respiration rate of 21 cycles per minute, heart rate of 74 cycles per minute, and a temperature of 37.1 °C. No particular clinical features were found during abdominal and limb examinations.

Given limited resources, plain radiography of the neck and chest was performed, revealing a radiopaque foreign body (padlock) at the level of the upper esophageal sphincter as showed in Figure 1. No signs of perforation or mediastinal emphysema were observed.Figure 1 X-ray showing the unlocked padlock retained at the cervical esophagus.

A grayscale radiograph of the chest and upper abdomen with ribs, spine and lung fields on a dark background.

The patient was then placed in an operating room. Under general anesthesia with the head and neck in hyperextension and a small cushion on the shoulders, suction was used to aspirate the intraoral secretions, which were dried with gauze pads mounted on sponge-holding forceps. Subsequently, the laryngoscope was positioned. The padlock was not visible but could be felt with the Magill forceps, guided by the light from the laryngoscope. The manoeuver for padlock removal was smooth to prevent further injuries. After a few attempts, the padlock was extracted successfully as it is showed in Figure 2 and final suctioning of the mouth and oropharynx was performed, followed by cleaning with swabs soaked in 0.9% saline solution. The laryngoscope was removed and the patient was transferred to the recovery room, where he awoke an hour and a half later.Figure 2 The ingested padlock after removal, found unlocked as it was seen on X-ray.

An unlocked padlock with a worn metallic surface.

Four hours after the extraction, he received tetanus antitoxin, and oral sips were initiated. The patient was discharged from the hospital 24 hours after the clinical review was uneventful. It should be acknowledged that oesophageal injuries could not be excluded after the removal of the padlock because of lack of endoscopy. Decisions were made based on clinical findings. The patient was followed up as an outpatient at 1 and 3 months post discharge. The follow-up was uneventful.

Discussion

A common pediatric emergency that can occasionally have serious or even fatal implications is when a foreign body (FB) obstructs the airway or esophagus.1,13,14 Children are also more vulnerable.5,15 This case involves a 7-year old child who presented to our health facility with a retained ingested padlock. To our knowledge, this is the first time a case of a retained upper oesophageal padlock is being reported.

The clinical manifestations of foreign materials in the aerodigestive tract are diverse. The kind, size, position, and duration of the foreign body’s lodgment in the aerodigestive tract all affect the symptoms and indicators that are generated. While a minor foreign body stuck in the aerodigestive system may cause less severe symptoms, a big foreign body obstructing the upper airway or esophagus may cause severe symptoms and possibly abrupt death.16–18 A foreign body may occasionally be discovered in the patient’s aerodigestive tract when an examination reveals that the patient only has a history of aspiration. Long-term foreign body lodgement in the esophagus can result in complications like mucosal ulceration, esophageal obstruction, perforation, intrinsic stenosis, and esophageal diverticulum, while long-term foreign body retention in the airway can cause lung collapse, severe respiratory distress, and recurrent chest infections.19,20 To avoid death and complications, early identification and treatment are essential.

In a study conducted in Northern Tanzania, Gilyoma et al, reported that aerodigestive foreign bodies are most commonly found in the trachea, with groundnuts being the most common at this site.21 Coins were reported to be the most common foreign body22 found in the esophagus, especially at the cricopharyngeal sphincter.21,23–25 These data should not be generalized, as other studies reported a predominant prevalence of foreign bodies in the esophagus,1,23 mostly in the cervical region, with dentures being the most common.1 In our case, the unlocked padlock was found hanging from the upper esophageal sphincter.

Prior to an endoscopic operation to evaluate the upper abdomen and chest, radiography is typically advised. This assessment may yield precise information about the foreign body’s location, size, and relationship to anatomical structures if it is radiopaque.13 However, since radio-lucent things like rubber materials, groundnuts, and meat boluses are difficult for conventional radiography to detect, a negative radiographic result does not rule out the existence of foreign bodies in the aerodigestive tract. In this case, chest radiography was performed. It showed a radiopaque image consistent with an unlocked padlock retained in the upper esophageal sphincter.

For airway foreign bodies, timely endoscopic detection and removal under conditions of maximal safety and little trauma is typically the preferred course of treatment.13,23 To reduce problems such perforation, mediastinitis, or airway compromise, foreign body impaction in the upper aerodigestive tract should ideally be removed within 24 hours. There are many modalities for management of aerodigestive foreign body.16 Many experts believe that rigid esophagoscopy is the best therapy option for removing foreign bodies. Additional techniques include open thoracotomy, cervical esophagotomy, flexible esophagoscopy, and the use of a Foley catheter under fluoroscopic guidance.24,26 When endoscopy is utilized, the success rate is roughly 90%.27 When foreign bodies in the upper aerodigestive tract are improperly removed, they frequently disappear.28 The literature has reported retained foreign body in the cervical oesophagus being removed safely with Magill forceps under laryngoscopy.9,12,29–33 However, no report on upper oesophageal padlock removal has been found to our knowledge. In our case of retained oesophageal padlock, removal skills minimized the risk of related complications. Decision-making should assess the advantages and disadvantages of every technique. Therefore, less invasive methods should be considered. However, the realities of remote health facilities do not comply with gold standards.

Conclusion

Safe management of retained oesophageal foreign bodies in remote settings sometimes requires thoughtful deviation from standards. A proximal, radiopaque, graspable foreign body at/above the cricopharyngeus, can be removed safely with Magill forceps under laryngoscopy, with anesthesia support. However, clinician should have immediate ability to convert or to refer to specialized health facility. Context-adapted approaches can achieve excellent outcomes when guided by sound clinical judgment, appropriate patient selection, and strict safety measures.
