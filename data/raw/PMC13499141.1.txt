Cancer progression is profoundly influenced by the complex interplay between the tumor microenvironment (TME), the immune system, and the patient's psychological state. This review delineates a critical pathway linking chronic stress to worsened cancer outcomes through inflammatory factors, anti-tumor immune suppression, and the induction of behavioral comorbidities. We postulate that sustained stress hormone signaling via the sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activation orchestrates a systemic immunosuppressive cascade. A central mechanism is the stress-induced expansion and functional polarization of myeloid cells, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and neutrophils within the TME. These cells foster a pro-tumoral milieu by impairing T-cell function, promoting angiogenesis, and facilitating metastasis, among other processes. Beyond the local TME, stress-mediated inflammation propagates to the central nervous system, compromising the blood-brain barrier and activating microglia. This neuroinflammatory response drives structural and functional remodeling in key brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, providing a biological basis for depression and anxiety often seen in patients with cancer. This creates a vicious cycle wherein psychological distress exacerbates tumor-promoting inflammation and immune evasion, which in turn worsens behavioral symptoms. Critically, clinical evidence now links psychological stress to chemoresistance and poor responses to immunotherapy. We argue that integrating stress-reducing interventions and strategies targeting neuroinflammation into cancer care is not merely supportive but essential to disrupt this detrimental cycle. A multidisciplinary approach that concurrently targets the tumor, the immune system, and the patient's mental well-being holds the promise of improving both survival and quality of life.

1 Introduction

Cancer presents profound challenges that extend far beyond the physical domain of the disease. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem surrounding tumor cells, comprising various immune cells, soluble factors, and structural components that facilitate cancer development, progression, and metastasis (Khalaf et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). Among these, myeloid cells, a diverse subset of immune cells originating in the bone marrow (BM), are critical players in the host's defense system. Accumulating evidence highlights the complex, dual role of myeloid cells in cancer biology, influenced by factors such as cancer type, stage, and physiological or behavioral cues (Haas and Obenauf, 2019; Parker et al., 2015).

For patients with cancer, the burden of disease extends beyond somatic symptoms; it frequently evolves into a significant psychological strain, manifesting as behavioral disorders that can profoundly alter the course of the disease. Epidemiological studies consistently report that patients across the cancer care continuum experience significantly higher rates of depression, anxiety, and trauma-related disorders compared to the general population (Goerling et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023). These maladaptive responses are not merely a passive reaction but are often exacerbated by a cascade of stressors intrinsic to the cancer diagnosis and treatment, which could lead to chronic stress and depression. Preclinical and clinical data suggest that these behavioral comorbidities can, in turn, promote cancer progression, potentially through immune system dysregulation (Liu et al., 2022; Miller et al., 2008; Zeng et al., 2024). These findings are mostly observational and primarily correlational, and cannot fully rule out reverse causation or shared risk factors such as treatment intensity, disease severity, and socioeconomic context. All these factors are thought to contribute to a bidirectional cycle where the interaction between cancer and stress-related behavioral disorders (including chronic stress, depression, and anxiety disorders) mutually reinforce one another, promoting a more permissive environment for both diseases.

A key factor in this interaction is the transition from acute to chronic activation of the body's stress response systems. The consequences of stress are neither uniform nor singular; rather, they depend critically on its intensity, duration, and patterns. Acute stress, an adaptive response to a transient threat, involves short-term activation, typically lasting less than one week, of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axis, such as after a near-miss car accident. In mouse models, acute stress is typically modeled by a single exposure (e.g., 2 h of restraint). Preclinical studies demonstrate that acute, short-term, or low-intensity stress can transiently enhance innate immune surveillance and cytotoxic immune activity, whereas prolonged or repeated stress exposure promotes maladaptive immunosuppression and tumor progression (Cassandras et al., 2026; Globig et al., 2023; Gonzalez-Aponte et al., 2025; Kinlein et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2021). In contrast, chronic stress arises from prolonged or repeated stress exposures, leading to a negative behavioral response, such as depression. This is defined experimentally in animal models using paradigms such as daily restraint for 7-21 days or chronic variable stress over weeks, which induce persistent neuroendocrine dysregulation and a pro-inflammatory state. It is this chronic stress response, characterized by sustained SNS/HPA axis activation and loss of regulatory feedback, that significantly disrupts physiological homeostasis (Kinlein et al., 2022). In murine cancer models, chronic restraint stress paradigms lasting 2 to 4 weeks consistently increase tumor burden, angiogenesis, metastatic dissemination, and accumulation of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, including MDSCs and M2-like macrophages (An et al., 2021; Aquino-Acevedo et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2021; Cassandras et al., 2026; Colon-Echevarria et al., 2019, 2020; Frick et al., 2009; Globig et al., 2023; Gonzalez-Aponte et al., 2025; He et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Rivera-López et al., 2025; Rudak et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2026; Thaker et al., 2006; Zhou et al., 2021). For example, daily restraint stress has been associated with increases in tumor growth ranging from ∼30 to 60% depending on tumor model and duration of exposure, alongside significant elevations in catecholamines, inflammatory cytokines, and suppressive myeloid infiltration (An et al., 2021; Aquino-Acevedo et al., 2022; Colon-Echevarria et al., 2020; He et al., 2024; Rivera-López et al., 2025; Sloan et al., 2010; Thaker et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2024). Similarly, repeated social defeat stress induces sustained myelopoiesis, splenic monocyte expansion, glucocorticoid resistance, and anxiety-like behaviors, effects that are substantially greater than those observed after acute stress exposure (Wohleb et al., 2013). Clinically, chronic psychological distress, depression, and social isolation are associated with worse cancer outcomes (tumor progression), poorer treatment responses, and increased inflammatory biomarkers, whereas transient stress responses do not consistently show these associations (Liu et al., 2022; Lutgendorf et al., 2008; Naser et al., 2021). Crucially, a sustained stress response acts as a potent immunosuppressant, impairs the anti-tumoral immune response, and weakens immune surveillance, thereby reducing the ability to eliminate cancer cells (Cao et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). Together, these findings support the concept that the transition from adaptive acute stress to sustained chronic stress represents a critical biological threshold that shifts neuroimmune signaling toward persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, and behavioral dysfunction.

A critical moderator of this pathway is individual resilience, the capacity to adapt positively to adversity, supported by psychological and social resources. Conceptual and mechanistic work indicates that adaptive stress coping, characteristic of higher resilience, is associated with more regulated HPA axis reactivity and better physiological adaptation to stressors (de Kloet and Joëls, 2024; Herman et al., 2016). In the context of cancer, this buffering effect has significant clinical implications. While a cancer diagnosis frequently triggers chronic SNS/HPA activation, leading to elevated catecholamines and cortisol, tumor-promoting inflammation, and immune suppression (Dai et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022), higher resilience appears to attenuate this dysregulation. For instance, higher pre-surgical resilience in breast cancer has been linked to markedly lower rates of cancer progression and mortality over 10 years (Chang et al., 2022), while greater psychosocial resources can disrupt the link between inflammation and depressive symptoms under high stress (Manigault et al., 2022). Thus, resilience may protect against the transition from acute stress to a maladaptive chronic state characterized by flattened or elevated cortisol, increased norepinephrine (NE), and pro-inflammatory cytokines, all factors tied to worse outcomes (Chang et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022).

This review will take a narrative approach to explore the intricate relationship between psychological stress-related processes, the modulation of myeloid-derived cells, and behavioral disorders, highlighting how these factors converge to lead to worse outcomes in cancer (Fig. 1). To explore the foundational mechanisms, key literature was identified for exploring the interplay between stress and immunity in preclinical and clinical cancer models. Furthermore, recent studies will be examined to compare new findings and advancements on these topics. This discussion will detail how the sustained activation of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axis, and the consequent flood of stress hormones such as catecholamines and cortisol, respectively, create a state of systemic dysregulation. This state not only impairs innate and adaptive immune surveillance but also directly contributes to worse cancer outcomes and diminished quality of life. Following this, we will review the literature focusing on the mechanisms of stress-induced myeloid suppression and neuroinflammation and how these pathways fuel a cycle of disease progression and behavioral comorbidity. 2 An overview of the interplay between psychological stress and adaptive and innate immunity in cancer patients

2.1 Association between the stress response, cancer outcomes, and quality of life

The stress response triggers the activation of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axis. Preclinical models demonstrate that prolonged activation, such as during chronic stress, leads to sustained release of stress hormones, including norepinephrine (NE), epinephrine (EPI), and cortisol (Armaiz-Pena et al., 2013; Colon-Echevarria et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2021). In clinical cohorts, elevated levels of these hormones are correlated with poor prognosis, supporting the translational relevance of this pathway (Bastos et al., 2018; Cash et al., 2024). Persistent exposure to these elevated hormone levels is hypothesized to disrupt physiological homeostasis, leading to widespread dysregulation, particularly of the immune system (Liu et al., 2022). This immunodysregulation is proposed to impair critical immune surveillance mechanisms and foster a tumor-permissive microenvironment, thereby facilitating cancer progression. While this mechanistic cascade is well-supported in animal models, establishing direct causal evidence in humans remains challenging, in part due to the difficulty in directly measuring stress hormones, which have substantial within-person variation over time; much of the clinical research is associational, highlighting a limitation in the current evidence.

This systemic dysfunction has profound implications for the patient's mental health. Epidemiological and clinical data consistently link to cancer-associated mental disorders, such as anxiety and depression (Bortolato et al., 2017). The inflammatory response is strongly hypothesized to play a key role in this process. For instance, preclinical evidence indicates that pro-inflammatory cytokines produced in the periphery can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and disrupt cognitive function (Najjar et al., 2013). Experimental models, such as tumor-bearing mice, demonstrate that repeated social stress increases brain macrophage infiltration and upregulates microglial-derived inflammatory markers, resulting in anxiety-like behavior (Bortolato et al., 2017). This directional crosstalk between the central nervous system (CNS) and systemic inflammation creates a feed-forward cycle, simultaneously driving the development of neuropsychiatric disorders and accelerating cancer progression (Bastos et al., 2018; Cash et al., 2024). While this neuroimmune pathway is mechanistically clear in animals, confirming this precise cascade in patients, particularly distinguishing cause from consequence in established depression, remains a challenge and represents a significant frontier for translational research.

Furthermore, chronic stress has also been correlated with chemoresistance. Observational studies indicate psychological stress in patients is associated with poor response to chemotherapy, and preclinical models suggest this relationship may be mediated by glucocorticoids and catecholamines that activate tumor cell survival pathways (Flaherty et al., 2019). This concept is supported by correlative clinical data in breast and ovarian cancer, where tumors from patients with worse outcomes exhibit molecular signatures of heightened stress, including elevated oxidative stress and adaptive autophagy (Pires et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022). Sophisticated single-cell analyses further correlate these stress-adapted cell states, which are enriched during chemotherapy treatment and are associated with worse progression-free survival (Pires et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022). Thus, cumulative evidence provides a plausible, multi-level narrative: stress hormones and the resultant cellular adaptations, including antioxidant responses (e.g., Nrf2) and pro-survival pathways, may help cancer cells evade chemotherapy-induced death (Flaherty et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022). While it is not possible to directly determine causality in patients, as it would be unethical to randomize cancer patients to chronic stress, this work underscores a compelling rationale for integrating stress-modulating strategies into oncologic care to improve outcomes.

2.2 Chronic stress and suppression of adaptive immunity in cancer

Preclinical studies demonstrate that sustained activation of the SNS and HPA axes initiates a broad immunosuppressive cascade (Armaiz-Pena et al., 2013; Colon-Echevarria et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2021), systemically altering the function and distribution of diverse immune populations, including natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, various T cell subsets, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and macrophages (Segerstrom and Smith, 2006) (Fig. 2). This dysregulation establishes a tumor-permissive environment. The direct impairment of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells serves as a core manifestation of immune suppression. Experimental animal models show chronic stress can suppress their activation, inhibit the production of the critical effector cytokine interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and disrupt the metabolic reprogramming essential for their effector function (Aquino-Acevedo et al., 2022; Qiao et al., 2021). This is corroborated by other work in which chronically stressed mice exhibit reduced IFN-γ levels, lower CD3 gene expression, and diminished infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells at tumor sites (Qiao et al., 2021). Furthermore, preclinical evidence indicates that stress-induced glucocorticoids can induce T cell apoptosis and polarize CD4+ helper T cell responses away from pro-inflammatory Th1 immunity towards a Th2-dominant profile, thereby shifting the broader immunological repertoire away from effective tumor control (Qiao et al., 2021).

This stress-mediated immunosuppression is mechanistically linked to T-cell exhaustion, a dysfunctional differentiation state characterized by progressive loss of effector function and sustained expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 (Aquino-Acevedo et al., 2022). Catecholamines act directly on adrenergic receptors expressed by T cells, dampening TCR signaling and enforcing a dysfunctional program marked by impaired mitochondrial fitness. Consistent with this, β-adrenergic signaling has been shown to increase the frequency of exhausted CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and reduce IFN-γ and TNF-α production. Importantly, stress responses reinforce T-cell exhaustion indirectly by reshaping the TME; for example, chronic stress drives the expansion and enhanced suppressive activity of polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs), which, in turn, reduce intratumoral CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration, accompanied by diminished Granzyme B and IFN-γ production (Rivera-López et al., 2025). These stress-induced alterations in myeloid cells and cytokines create sustained antigenic, metabolic, and inhibitory signaling conditions known to promote and stabilize exhausted T-cell phenotypes. Thus, neuroendocrine stress signaling contributes to T-cell exhaustion both directly through adrenergic receptor engagement on T cells and indirectly via myeloid-mediated immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment.

Based on this convergent mechanistic work, stress signaling through adrenergic receptors is proposed to act as a potent immunological “checkpoint” within the TME. This concept is supported by studies demonstrating that β-adrenergic blockade (e.g., propranolol) can partially reinvigorate CD8+ T cells, reduce exhausted phenotypes, and improve responses to immune checkpoint blockade in preclinical models, positioning stress pathways as upstream “neuroendocrine checkpoints” (Massalee and Cao, 2023) However, while this preclinical evidence is robust and defines a clear mechanism, its direct translation to human cancer patients has not yet been fully established. Direct causal evidence that psychological stress induces T-cell exhaustion and clinical immunotherapy resistance in patients is limited, as only correlative studies can be conducted, as discussed above. However, in a study of human ovarian cancer tissue microarrays, depression near the time of diagnosis was associated with increased markers of T-cell exhaustion (Hathaway et al., 2023), demonstrating how prospective, integrated bio-behavioral studies in patient cohorts, along with parallel in vivo and in vitro models, can synergistically demonstrate that causal findings from experimental preclinical studies can be replicated in correlative studies in human populations.

2.3 Chronic stress and modulation of innate immunity in cancer

Beyond suppressing adaptive immunity, preclinical evidence indicates that chronic stress profoundly reshapes the innate immune landscape within the TME, fostering a protumoral state by expanding, recruiting, and functionally polarizing immunosuppressive myeloid cells. Below, we synthesize findings supporting these processes.

2.3.1 Myeloid expansion and recruitment

A central mechanism mediating the chronic stress-cancer interaction is the stress hormone-induced mobilization of myeloid cells. Experimental studies show that stress signaling promotes hematopoietic differentiation towards the myeloid lineage and enhances infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) (Liu et al., 2022). Mechanistically, β-adrenergic signaling upregulates C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) on myeloid progenitors, guiding their migration to tumor sites (Cao et al., 2021), while catecholamines induce the chemoattractant C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) (Hao et al., 2020), guiding cells to the TME. This accumulated, skewed myeloid compartment is thought to sustain an immunosuppressive and pro-inflammatory milieu (Cubillos-Ruiz et al., 2017; Parker et al., 2015).

2.3.2 A feed-forward myeloid-derived cell recruitment circuit

Critically, myeloid-derived cell recruitment can become self-amplifying. Stress-induced activation of the CXCL5–CXCR2–ERK cascade not only guides cells to the tumor but also establishes a feed-forward circuit. Once CXCR2+ myeloid cells (particularly PMN-MDSCs/neutrophil-like suppressor cells) infiltrate the tumor, they can release inflammatory mediators, alarmins, and proteases that remodel stromal and tumor cell chemokine production and tissue remodeling (e.g., MMP-driven matrix remodeling), thereby sustaining continued recruitment and retention of suppressive myeloid populations. This model is consistent with the broader concept that CXCR2 ligands (including CXCL5) are central determinants of PMN-MDSCs/neutrophil trafficking and that infiltrating granulocytic myeloid cells can reinforce inflammatory chemokine gradients within tumors.

2.3.3 Metabolic reprogramming for immunosuppressive function

Beyond direct migration, β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) signaling in myeloid cells can couple stress hormones to profound metabolic rewiring that directly enables immunosuppressive effector functions. In stressed settings, β-AR activation shifts cellular metabolism away from glycolysis and toward mitochondrial oxidative metabolism (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO), a shift characterized by increased expression of lipid transport/FAO machinery (e.g., CPT1A) and enhanced autophagy (Mohammadpour et al., 2021). This reprogrammed metabolic state supports the survival and suppressive capacity of cells like MDSCs and converges on specific metabolite-based pathways to inhibit anti-tumor immunity. First, the upregulation of FAO supports MDSC persistence, while activation of the arachidonic acid–COX axis promotes synthesis of immunoregulatory eicosanoids such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which directly dampens T cells (Kruglov et al., 2024). Second, stress enhances the expression of canonical immunosuppressive enzymes (e.g., ARG1), leading to local arginine depletion that metabolically restricts T-cell proliferation and effector function (Gabrilovich, 2021; Sosnowska et al., 2021). Third, the stress-altered metabolic state of myeloid cells may be further biased within the tumor niche by local immunometabolites; lactate (abundant in acidic tumors) can support M2-like macrophage polarization. Metabolites represent candidate mediators through which the combined effects of chronic stress and tumor metabolism reinforce pro-tumor myeloid phenotypes. In summary, β2-AR signaling serves as a critical link between psychological stress and the metabolic reprogramming of myeloid cells, endowing them with specific biochemical tools, enhanced FAO, PGE2 production, and arginine depletion that collectively suppress T-cell immunity and promote a pro-tumor microenvironment.

2.3.4 Synthesis and translational perspective

In summary, preclinical models illustrate a strategic, multi-pronged stress pathway that drives myeloid cell expansion, recruitment via a potentially self-sustaining circuit, and metabolic polarization toward immunosuppression. These detailed cellular mechanisms, while well-defined in experimental systems, require further validation in human settings. Confirming their operation, by linking patient stress levels to biomarkers of CXCR2 activity, FAO, and specific suppressive metabolites in tumors, is essential for assessing their therapeutic relevance across diverse cancer types.

2.3.5 Chronic stress and modulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells

MDSCs are pathologically activated, immature myeloid cells derived from BM progenitors, as broadly defined in the literature, and are strongly associated with immunosuppression and poor clinical outcomes in cancer (Kumar et al., 2016). Their development is governed by dual signaling networks: one axis drives the accumulation of their immature myeloid precursors, while another induces their pathological activation (Kumar et al., 2016). MDSCs are classified into two main subsets: PMN-MDSCs, which are morphologically similar to neutrophils and are prevalent in chronic inflammation and tumor progression, and monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs), which share monocyte-like characteristics and specialize in potent immune suppression and tissue remodeling (Kumar et al., 2016).

Experimental research indicates that chronic stress and its associated neuroendocrine hormones are potent modulators of this signaling network, profoundly enhancing MDSC function and mobilization. Preclinical models demonstrate that catecholamines dysregulate metabolic pathways in MDSCs, thereby augmenting their immunosuppressive capacity (Mohammadpour et al., 2021). Mechanistic studies show signaling through β-adrenergic receptors promotes MDSC survival by suppressing pro-apoptotic genes while simultaneously inducing the expression of immunosuppressive mediators, such as arginase 1 (Arg1) and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) (Mohammadpour et al., 2019). Furthermore, catecholamines have been shown in models to activate the CXCL5-CXCR2 axis and the ERK pathway and upregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), mechanisms that collectively potentiate MDSC migration, accumulation, and immunosuppressive activity within the TME (Cao et al., 2021). Complementing this, preclinical evidence indicates stress-induced glucocorticoids further enhance MDSC immunosuppression by dysregulating core metabolic processes, including oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), FAO, and the arachidonic acid pathway (Mohammadpour et al., 2021).

This stress-induced reprogramming of MDSCs has correlative clinical relevance. For instance, psychological stress correlates with elevated MDSC levels in breast tumor samples, a finding linked to poorer patient prognosis (Mundy-Bosse et al., 2011a). Once expanded and activated within the TME, MDSCs are understood to orchestrate a multifaceted pro-tumoral response. Based on in vitro and animal studies, they directly drive CD8+ T cell exhaustion, promote regulatory T cell (Treg) proliferation, and inhibit NK cell activity. They also are proposed to indirectly support tumorigenesis by dysregulating key oncogenic signaling pathways in cancer cells, including the JAK/STAT, Notch, Wnt, and NF-κB pathways (Cao et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2020). Thus, stress-mediated modulation of MDSCs has been established in preclinical studies as a critical mechanism by which neuroendocrine signaling fuels tumor immune evasion and progression (Fig. 2A). While this mechanistic pathway is well supported by experimental data, assessing whether psychological stress directly drives human tumor progression by modulating the MDSC axis requires prospective studies that integrate psychological assessment with deep immunophenotyping in patient cohorts.

2.3.6 Chronic stress-mediated reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages

Macrophages are versatile innate immune cells central to antigen presentation, phagocytosis, regulation of immune responses, wound healing, and cytokine production (Liu et al., 2022). Their functional plasticity is characterized by a spectrum of activation states, broadly classified into pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-tumoral macrophages, or immunosuppressive (M2) and pro-tumoral macrophages (Anderson and Simon, 2020). In the context of cancer, this polarization is co-opted within the TME, where tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) predominantly adopt an M2-like phenotype that fosters immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis (Dallavalasa et al., 2021).

A growing body of experimental evidence indicates that chronic stress and its neuroendocrine effectors are potent drivers of TAM recruitment, polarization, and pro-tumoral function. Preclinical models demonstrate that stress significantly increases the infiltration of CD68+ macrophages into the TME, a process associated with elevated release of pro-tumoral factors, including granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Colon-Echevarria et al., 2020). Mechanistic studies show that stress hormones orchestrate this reprogramming through distinct pathways. Signaling through β-adrenergic receptors on macrophages drives polarization towards an M2 phenotype and induces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α (Qin et al., 2015). Furthermore, experimental research indicates that norepinephrine can induce tumor cells to secrete neuropeptide Y (NPY), which, in turn, enhances macrophage recruitment and IL-6 production, creating a pro-tumoral feedback loop (Cheng et al., 2019).

Complementing catecholamine actions, preclinical findings suggest that stress-induced glucocorticoids profoundly suppress anti-tumor immunity by impairing the macrophage's ability to phagocytose cancer cells. This is proposed to be achieved through the repression of the “eat me” signal receptor LRP1 and the upregulation of the “don't eat me” signal SIRP⍺ (Wu et al., 2022). Glucocorticoids also promote metabolic changes in TAMs, such as upregulation of the COX-2/PGE2 pathway, which further contributes to an immunosuppressive TME (Muthuswamy et al., 2017). The resultant pro-tumoral activity is exemplified in models by stress-induced production of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) by macrophages, a key facilitator of tumor invasion and metastasis (Lutgendorf et al., 2008) (Fig. 2B).

Clinically, this stress-TAM axis has been correlated with significant ramifications. Depressive symptomatology in ovarian cancer patients is correlated with higher tumoral levels of MMP-9 in CD68+ macrophages (Lutgendorf et al., 2008); however, whether depression causally drives TAM reprogramming or simply tracks with more aggressive disease remains unresolved. This finding aligns with extensive literature linking increased TAM infiltration to worse disease outcomes across numerous cancer types, including gastric, lung, breast, and ovarian cancers (Anderson and Simon, 2020; Dallavalasa et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings highlight a plausible, multi-level, bidirectional pathway in which the SNS and the HPA axis subvert macrophage function to promote tumor progression and activate inflammatory pathways in the tumor, the periphery, and the brain that could lead to depression, thereby identifying TAMs as a key therapeutic target. While the preclinical mechanistic data are compelling, additional evidence showing that psychological stress can lead to worse cancer-related outcomes in humans, specifically through this macrophage-mediated axis, is needed, especially in integrated biobehavioral clinical studies.

2.3.7 Chronic stress-induced impairment of dendritic cell function

Dendritic cells (DC) are canonical antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that are essential for initiating and regulating adaptive immune responses. They perform this critical function by capturing, processing, and presenting antigens to T cells within secondary lymphoid organs, thereby serving as a pivotal bridge between innate and adaptive immunity (Anderson and Simon, 2020). Within the TME, the functional fate of DCs is dictated by local signals, which can polarize them towards either an immunogenic phenotype that sparks an anti-tumor response or a tolerogenic state that promotes immune tolerance (Garris and Luke, 2020).

Experimental evidence suggests that the stress response and its associated glucocorticoid hormones are potent drivers of a tolerogenic shift in DCs, actively impairing DC-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Glucocorticoids have been shown in in vitro and animal studies to suppress DC activation, inhibit their migratory capacity, and even induce apoptosis (Chamorro et al., 2009). Furthermore, they are understood to transcriptionally reprogram DCs towards an immunosuppressive phenotype by upregulating genes for tolerogenic cytokines such as IL-10 while simultaneously downregulating genes essential for T cell activation, including those encoding co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86) and immunostimulatory cytokines (IL-12, IL-6, TNF) (Rea et al., 2000). This glucocorticoid-driven suppression is proposed to be compounded by stress-induced inhibition of IL-12 production in APCs, which is thought to skew T helper cell responses away from pro-inflammatory Th1 immunity and towards a Th2-dominant profile (Reiche et al., 2004).

The TME itself may amplify this stress-induced DC dysfunction. Preclinical evidence indicates that tumor-derived VEGF, a factor often upregulated in stress conditions, can further disrupt normal DC maturation, contributing to immune evasion (Gabrilovich et al., 1996). Interestingly, while most studies report stress-induced reductions in DC activation/maturation, cold-stress models show increased DC numbers but impaired function, underscoring that stress can differentially affect DC quantity versus quality (Kokolus et al., 2014). Consequently, this chronic stress-induced dysregulation is hypothesized to substantially reduce DCs' ability to present tumor antigens and prime cytotoxic T cells effectively (Fig. 2C). This failure to initiate a robust adaptive immune response suppresses anti-tumor surveillance and facilitates tumor immune evasion, providing a plausible pathway linking psychological stress and compromised cancer immunity. As with other stress-immune mechanisms, translating these detailed preclinical findings into validated human biomarkers and therapeutic strategies remains an essential next step.

2.3.8 Chronic stress-induced neutrophil dysregulation in cancer

Neutrophils constitute approximately 70% of circulating leukocytes and serve as a vital first line of innate defense, rapidly responding to and neutralizing pathogens (Wu et al., 2020). Within the TME, however, their role is complex and context-dependent, varying significantly across cancer types and disease stages (Colon-Echevarria et al., 2019). While their function in cancer is multifaceted, a significant gap exists in understanding how stress specifically modulates neutrophilic responses in cancer.

Emerging experimental evidence now indicates that stress is a regulator of neutrophil biology in cancer. A key mechanism proposed from preclinical work involves glucocorticoid signaling, which is understood to dysregulate neutrophil function and induce the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). In preclinical lung cancer models, this stress-induced NETosis has been shown to help establish a metastasis-promoting microenvironment (He et al., 2024). Experimental studies further indicate that stress hormones influence neutrophil egress from the bone marrow by downregulating endothelial expression of key adhesion molecules, including PECAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 (Cavalcanti et al., 2007). Concurrently, they are shown to delay neutrophil apoptosis by activating survival pathways, such as p38 MAPK and PI3K, and by upregulating anti-apoptotic genes, including myeloid cell leukemia 1 (Mcl1) and X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (XIAP) (Saffar et al., 2011).

Complementing these glucocorticoid-driven effects, catecholamines are also implicated in the pro-tumoral effects mediated by neutrophils. Norepinephrine, for instance, has been shown in experimental systems to stimulate neutrophils to release alarmin S100A8/A9 and activate myeloperoxidase (MPO) to produce oxidized lipids, a process that has been demonstrated in models to reactivate dormant tumor cells and fuel metastatic recurrence (Perego et al., 2020).

Collectively, these preclinical findings demonstrate that chronic stress not only increases neutrophil numbers but also actively reprograms their function towards a pro-tumoral phenotype (Fig. 2D). Through the combined actions of glucocorticoids and catecholamines, stress is proposed to promote neutrophil mobilization, enhance survival, and induce the release of factors that foster a pro-metastatic niche, thereby suggesting a novel mechanistic link between psychological stress and cancer progression through neutrophil dysregulation. The strength of this link and its direct relevance to human cancer pathophysiology, however, remain to be fully established through studies correlating psychological stress with neutrophil phenotypes and functions in patient cohorts. To date, correlative data linking psychological stress with neutrophil phenotypes or NET markers in cancer patients are sparse, so the translational weight of these mechanisms is still speculative.

3 Beyond the tumor: stress-induced systemic immune dysregulation

The influence of chronic stress is proposed to extend far beyond the TME, inducing profound systemic immune dysregulation and contributing to significant behavioral pathophysiology in cancer patients. This global influence is supported by observations in peripheral immune-related organs. The spleen serves as a key immunological reservoir and a prime example of how stress induces systemic dysregulation, characterized by complex, often contradictory alterations in immune cell populations and function.

Experimental models demonstrate that acute stress can induce splenic sympathetic nerve-mediated immunosuppression; for instance, it stimulates acetylcholine (Ach)-releasing ChAT + T cells to suppress macrophages via α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. In contrast, chronic stress leads to more sustained changes, including a reduction in peripheral CD4+ T cell numbers, a phenomenon observed in both restrained mice and correlated with anxiety in human patients, highlighting a critical role for these cells in stress-induced behavioral manifestations (Fan et al., 2019). Conversely, stress has also been shown to boost humoral immunity by expanding splenic plasma cell populations, yet it simultaneously exacerbates inflammatory responses and promotes B cell apoptosis upon immune challenge (Laukova et al., 2018).

Further evidence of this systemic dysregulation, largely derived from animal studies, includes altered B:T lymphocyte ratios, decreased splenocyte counts, reduced spleen size, and a shift in immune subsets towards an immunosuppressive phenotype. This phenotype is characterized by a reduction of NK cells and an increase in activated M2 macrophages (Gurfein et al., 2017; Lei et al., 2024; Maslanik et al., 2012). These apparently conflicting findings, stress-induced expansions of plasma cells alongside reduced splenocyte counts and increased M2 macrophages, suggest that stress reconfigures rather than uniformly suppresses immunity, complicating simple ‘pro-vs anti-immune’ categorizations. Although these findings were largely described in non-tumor-bearing animals, similar immune remodeling has been observed in stress-exposed cancer models, where the same suppressive and inflammatory signatures are associated with enhanced tumor progression and cancer-related behavioral alterations (Le et al., 2016; Lei et al., 2024; Thaker et al., 2006). Thus, while preclinical models provide a mechanistic foundation for stress-induced splenic reprogramming, the role of this systemic immune shift in driving human cancer progression and behavioral comorbidities remains an area for further investigation.

3.1 Tumor-associated innervation: implications for stress and systemic immunity

Preclinical research indicates that sympathetic signaling in the TME is profoundly influenced by chronic psychological stress, which induces sustained SNS activation (Sattler et al., 2025). Experimental evidence suggests that this state of stress facilitates and amplifies the sympathetic innervation of tumors, a process in which nerve fibers sprout into the TME in response to tumor-derived factors such as nerve growth factor (NGF) (Cole et al., 2015; Sattler et al., 2025). Consequently, stress-elevated NE levels are released directly into the TME, where preclinical studies show they engage adrenergic receptors on immune and tumor cells, thereby initiating a potent immunosuppressive cascade (Cole et al., 2015; Kyritsi et al., 2025; Sattler et al., 2025).

This neuroimmune axis, driven by stress, is proposed to promote the recruitment and activation of MDSCs and regulatory T cells, induce T cell exhaustion, upregulate checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, and drive the M2 Polarization of TAMs (Kyritsi et al., 2025; Sattler et al., 2025). This is understood to establish a feed-forward loop, where stress-promoted sympathetic activity creates an immunosuppressive niche that fuels tumor progression. Therefore, targeting this pathway with β-adrenergic blockade represents a promising, preclinically supported strategy to mitigate the detrimental effects of cancer immunity and enhance responses to immunotherapy (Kyritsi et al., 2025; Sattler et al., 2025). Consistent with emerging evidence that the nervous system is an active component of the tumor microenvironment, a study demonstrates that peripheral solid tumors, such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), harbor functional intratumoral neural circuits, including synapse-like structures and measurable electrical activity (Restaino et al., 2023). In the same study, malignant tissues exhibited greater neural activity than benign or normal counterparts, and both genetic ablation of sensory neurons and pharmacologic silencing attenuated tumor growth and metastasis (Restaino et al., 2023). These findings reinforce the concept that tumor-associated nerves actively contribute to disease progression and may represent actionable therapeutic targets in oncology. While this sympathetic-immune axis is mechanistically compelling in animal models, confirming its operation and therapeutic vulnerability in human cancers requires further clinical investigation that integrates neural imaging, TME analysis, and patient outcomes.

3.2 Chronic stress-induced myeloid mobilization from the bone marrow

Preclinical research establishes stress-induced remodeling of the bone marrow, the central hub of hematopoiesis. Experimental models show that signaling through β-adrenergic receptors enhances myelopoiesis and alters leukocyte trafficking, thereby accelerating the release of innate immune cells into the circulation. For instance, in repeated social defeat stress models, stress increases the proliferation of myeloid progenitors in the bone marrow, leading to elevated accumulation of neutrophils and monocytes in the blood and spleen (Heidt et al., 2014).

The mechanistic basis for this mobilization has been elucidated in preclinical studies. It involves stress-hormone-mediated reduction in CXCL12 expression in the bone marrow niche. CXCL12 is a key retention chemokine that typically anchors myeloid cells via CXCR4 binding (Heidt et al., 2014). The concurrent release of catecholamines and glucocorticoids has been demonstrated to directly promote leukocyte egress and alter cellular function (Powell et al., 2013; Wohleb et al., 2014). Paradoxically, chronic stress can induce glucocorticoid resistance in these newly mobilized myeloid cells. This is proposed to subvert their normal regulatory feedback, leading to unchecked pro-inflammatory activation (Stark et al., 2001) and priming the system for potential downstream consequences. These consequences, which include chronic inflammation, tissue damage, immune exhaustion, and dysregulated cytokine production, are implicated in promoting tumor progression and immune dysfunction (Grivennikov et al., 2010). While this bone marrow reprogramming axis is a well-defined mechanism in animal models, its contribution to immune dysfunction and tumor progression in human cancer patients, and its susceptibility to therapeutic intervention remain active areas of translational investigation.

3.3 The systemic inflammatory cascade and its impact on the central nervous system

As established, chronic stress creates a state of systemic immunosuppression and inflammation. According to the prevailing model, this peripheral inflammatory cascade does not remain confined to the tumor and circulation; it is proposed to directly target the central nervous system (CNS), a process recognized as neuroinflammation (Mittli, 2023; Süß et al., 2020). This interaction is understood to establish a feed-forward cycle in which stress-induced peripheral inflammation triggers neuroinflammation, which manifests as psychological distress (e.g., anxiety, anhedonia) (Kokkosis et al., 2024; Wohleb and Delpech, 2017). This distress, in turn, can perpetuate the activation of stress pathways, potentially further amplifying peripheral inflammation and tumor progression. Thus, psychological stress is increasingly viewed not as a passive side effect but as an active, biologically embedded component of cancer pathophysiology that directly impairs brain function. The specific neuroimmune mechanisms linking this systemic dysregulation to behavioral comorbidities are detailed in the following section.

4 Neuroimmune mechanisms of stress-induced behavioral comorbidities in cancer

4.1 The peripheral-to-central inflammatory cascade

Preclinical and clinical evidence indicate that the physiological stress response in cancer patients activates the innate immune system, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β) and stress hormone-mediated mobilization of myeloid cells from the bone marrow (Wohleb et al., 2013). This systemic inflammatory state is proposed to directly target the CNS (Mittli, 2023; Süß et al., 2020). Circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines can compromise the integrity of the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB), creating a proposed conduit for both inflammatory mediators and newly mobilized peripheral immune cells to enter the brain parenchyma (Huang et al., 2021; Rochfort and Cummins, 2015).

Within the brain, this concerted process is understood to activate resident microglia, the CNS's primary immune cells. The influx of peripheral signals and cells is thought to shift microglia from a homeostatic to a primed, hyperactivated state, lowering their threshold for inflammatory response (Frank et al., 2020). This microglial activation is posited to fuel a self-amplifying cycle of inflammation, characterized by elevated central pro-inflammatory cytokines that further disrupt neural homeostasis (Picard et al., 2021). This precise cascade, from stress-induced myelopoiesis and systemic inflammation to BBB disruption and microglial priming, provides the fundamental neuroimmune substrate for the development of behavioral disorders, supported by data from clinical and preclinical cancer contexts (Bortolato et al., 2017). While this cascade is strongly supported by preclinical data, particularly the timing and causality of BBB disruption and microglial priming, further integrative studies in cancer patients are required to combine psychological assessment, neuroimaging, and biomarker analysis.

4.2 Microglial-driven neuroinflammation and neural circuit dysfunction

Preclinical evidence indicates that activated microglia are the primary drivers of sustained neuroinflammation (Ransohoff and Brown, 2012), implicating them in the disruption of specific limbic-cortical circuits. In animal models, neuroinflammation in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), and hippocampus (HPC), synergizing with glucocorticoid signaling, is associated with dendritic atrophy, synaptic loss, and suppressed neurogenesis, leading to structural volume reduction and deficits in executive function and memory (Du Preez et al., 2021; Ledoux, 2000; McEwen, 2005; Woodburn et al., 2021). Conversely, in the amygdala and nucleus accumbens (NAc), stress can induce maladaptive hypertrophy. For example, experimental studies show that stress increases peripheral myeloid-derived matrix metalloprotease-8 (MMP8), which infiltrates the NAc to remodel the extracellular matrix and has been shown to be necessary and sufficient to drive social avoidance and anhedonia (Bessa et al., 2013; Cathomas et al., 2024). Collectively, this microglia-mediated region-specific remodeling, atrophy of control centers, and hypertrophy of threat/reward centers, is proposed to bias neural circuitry towards heightened emotional reactivity, impaired cognitive control, and disrupted reward processing. These changes are directly linked in preclinical cancer models to depressive and anxiety-like behaviors that mirror symptoms observed in patients (Du Preez et al., 2021; Santos and Pyter, 2018). While this region-specific pathophysiology is compellingly defined in experimental systems, assessing whether human cancer-related psychiatric symptoms arise from the precise microglial mechanism requires further neuroimmunological investigation in patient cohorts.

4.3 A convergent mechanism for neuropsychiatric symptoms

The microglia-neuroinflammation axis is increasingly proposed as a unifying mechanism for diverse cancer-related neuropsychiatric symptoms, ranging from major depression to cognitive impairment. Preclinical evidence supports this model as inhibiting microglial activation reverses depressive-like behaviors in animal models. (Kopschina Feltes et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2019). Similarly, in models of trauma-related disorders, stress-induced microglial activation and neuroinflammation are linked to pathophysiology such as impaired fear extinction, and anti-inflammatory strategies can prevent related behaviors (Freire et al., 2019; Torres-Rodríguez et al., 2023). In cancer, where systemic inflammation is often prevalent, stress-triggered microglial activation is thus a theoretically plausible key contributor to the overlapping spectrum of mood, cognitive, and trauma-related symptoms (McCaffrey et al., 2022). Therefore, targeting this axis represents a promising, mechanistically-informed strategy to preserve brain health. Interventions, whether pharmacological (e.g., microglial modulators, β-blockers) or behavioral (e.g., stress reduction), that dampen this peripheral-to-central inflammatory cascade hold significant potential to improve quality of life and potentially patient outcomes in cancer. However, realizing this potential requires bridging the gap between robust preclinical mechanisms and demonstrated clinical efficacy in oncology populations.

5 An integrative model: the Myeloid-CNS feed-forward loop in cancer pathogenesis

Synthesizing the evidence presented, this review proposes that the interplay between chronic stress and cancer is best understood not as a linear cascade but as a dynamically amplified circuit: the Myeloid-CNS Feed-Forward Loop (Fig. 1). This model extends beyond established concepts by integrating the critical, evidence-based mechanisms of “priming” and “sensitization”. First, preclinical evidence suggests that chronic stress systemically primes the myeloid compartment (Fig. 1B). Through β-adrenergic signaling, it can epigenetically and metabolically reprogram hematopoietic progenitors in the bone marrow and enhance the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs and TAMs in the periphery (Cao et al., 2021; Colon-Echevarria et al., 2020; Mohammadpour et al., 2021). These cells are theorized to be not merely more numerous but also hyper-responsive, poised to release elevated levels of inflammatory mediators. Second, this systemic inflammatory milieu is proposed to sensitize the CNS (Fig. 1A). The flood of cytokine (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6) from primed myeloid cells compromises the BBB and, crucially, shifts microglia from surveillance to a primed phenotype with a lowered activation threshold (Frank et al., 2020; McKim et al., 2018; Wohleb et al., 2012). This neuroimmune sensitization could then facilitate maladaptive structural plasticity in the Prefrontal Cortex (PFC), Hippocampus (HPC), and amygdala, creating a proposed biological substrate for anxiety and depression (Cathomas et al., 2024; Du Preez et al., 2021).

The novel synthesis lies in the loop's feed-forward nature. This subsequent stress signal would then encounter a primed myeloid system and a sensitized brain, potentially generating a more potent immunosuppressive and pro-tumoral response than the initial stressor. Therefore, it is hypothesized that a self-perpetuating cycle exists: stress leads to priming or sensitization, which, in turn, results in behavioral pathology. This pathology further intensifies the stress response and associated inflammatory/immune profiles and promotes tumor progression (Fig. 1C). This model generates testable hypotheses, such as whether biomarkers of myeloid priming predict susceptibility to cancer-related depression, and whether early interventions (e.g., β-blockers, cognitive-behavioral therapy) can “de-prime” this system and prevent loop amplification. It argues for adopting a prehabilitative psychoneuroimmunology approach in oncology, in which stabilizing this axis becomes a proactive component of initial cancer care. The validity and clinical utility of this integrative model await testing through the multidisciplinary, longitudinal studies it suggests.

6 Discussion

The immunosuppressive nature of the TME remains a paramount obstacle to achieving durable therapeutic efficacy in oncology. This review argues that the neurobiological changes induced by stress, including SNS-and HPA-driven myeloid suppression, dendritic cell dysfunction, impaired antigen presentation, and microglial activation, constitute a crucial yet often overlooked aspect of this immunosuppression. In addition to myeloid suppression and impaired antigen presentation, recent studies indicate that chronic stress can drive neutrophil dysfunction and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), mechanisms strongly associated with the creation of metastatic niches, tumor relapse, and cancer spread. This evidence broadens our understanding of the effects of stress, demonstrating that, in addition to myeloid suppression, dysregulated neutrophils act as essential mediators linking stress-induced inflammation to cancer progression. Beyond the TME, these pathways serve as a primary biological substrate for the development of behavioral disorders, thereby adversely affecting patient outcomes (Table 1). This psychoneuroimmune framework is strongly supported by extensive clinical and translational data (Table 1). Across a range of malignancies, stress-related factors have been consistently linked to immune remodeling, behavioral changes, and unfavorable clinical outcomes. For instance, in breast cancer, psychological stress correlates with increased intratumoral accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and poorer prognosis (Mundy-Bosse et al., 2011). Depression and anxiety have also been implicated in shaping the immune tumor microenvironment (Castro-Figueroa et al., 2021). In ovarian cancer, depressive symptoms are associated with elevated MMP-9 expression in tumor-associated macrophages (Lutgendorf et al., 2008), while stress hormones detected in patient ascites correlate with increased inflammatory cytokine levels and reduced T-cell function (Aquino-Acevedo et al., 2022). Additionally, molecular programs associated with stress have been implicated in chemoresistance in both breast and ovarian tumors (Pires et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022), and emotional distress has been linked to poorer responses to immune checkpoint blockade in NSCLC patients (Zeng et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings underscore the translational importance of stress-induced neuroimmune remodeling and further substantiate the proposed myeloid–CNS framework.

At the mechanistic level, sustained SNS/HPA axis activation elevates catecholamines and glucocorticoids that suppress adaptive anti-tumor immunity (e.g., impaired CD8+ T-cell effector programs) and promote the expansion/recruitment and functional polarization of immunosuppressive myeloid cells (MDSCs, TAMs, neutrophils), collectively fostering angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis (Cavalcanti et al., 2007; Xue Yan He et al., 2024; Mohammadpour et al., 2021; Mundy-Bosse et al., 2011b; Rivera-López et al., 2025; SenGupta et al., 2021). These pathways align with pre-clinical data demonstrating that stress-induced tumor progression and invasiveness are largely mediated by stress hormone signaling (He et al., 2024; Mohammadpour et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2015; Rivera-López et al., 2025).

Consequently, stress response mechanisms and neuroinflammation emerge as indispensable therapeutic targets for improving cancer care. Current treatment paradigms often fail to address a patient's predisposition to or experience of these behavioral comorbidities, thereby neglecting a significant factor that can influence overall survival and quality of life. The evidence presented in this review supports a paradigm shift: cancer treatment must continue evolving beyond focusing solely on tumor eradication to a more holistic model that concurrently prioritizes mental health and targets the psychoneuroimmune axis. This paradigm is supported by a growing body of interventional research. Clinical trials demonstrate that behavioral interventions such as cognitive-behavioral stress management (CBSM), mindfulness, and yoga can directly modulate stress physiology, including in those living with cancer. These approaches have been shown to reduce SNS and HPA axis hyperactivity, lower inflammation (e.g., reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and CRP), and improve cellular immune function (e.g., enhancing CD4+/CD8+ T-cell ratios and NK cell cytotoxicity), with some studies linking these changes to improved long-term outcomes in early-stage cancers, including prostate cancer (Antoni and Dhabhar, 2019; Chang et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2022). For instance, CBSM has demonstrated beneficial effects on T-cell populations, and resilience interventions can alter stress-related gene expression, confirming that modulating psychology can directly influence biology (Taylor et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2022). These findings further indicate that resilience may function not only as a psychosocial construct but also as a potential biological modifier capable of mitigating neuroendocrine dysregulation and inflammatory responses associated with chronic stress.

Complementing these behavioral strategies, pharmacological interventions offer a direct way to disrupt specific stress pathways. For example, non-selective β-blockers, such as propranolol, antagonize β2AR signaling, a key mechanism. β2AR signaling in MDSCs promotes their recruitment and immunosuppressive function via the cAMP-PKA pathway; blocking this signaling can restore NK cell function and slow tumor progression in preclinical and clinical settings (Chang et al., 2022). Clinical observations further underscore the importance of stress modulation in cancer therapy. Similarly, strategies to modulate glucocorticoid activity and to employ anti-inflammatory agents have shown potential to improve anti-tumor immunity by targeting the inflammatory sequelae of stress (Antoni and Dhabhar, 2019; Chang et al., 2022; Chen and Xie, 2018; Zhao et al., 2021). A particularly strong body of observational literature supports this concept, specifically regarding aspirin use and improved survival in breast, colon, and ovarian cancers, suggesting that its COX-inhibiting and antiplatelet functions may reduce metastasis-promoting inflammation. Prospective trials support this hypothesis; for instance, the Aspirin trial (NCT00578721) has investigated the role of aspirin in preventing recurrence in non-metastatic breast cancer, while a phase II trial (NCT03103152)is examining its effect on the TME in head and neck cancer. An additional area of great interest is the development of microglial modulators to manage cancer-related cognitive and mood symptoms by targeting the neuroinflammation (Zhao et al., 2021).

While chronic stress biology has been causally linked to tumor progression in preclinical models, estimating the magnitude of its effect in humans depends on the outcome considered. Epidemiologic evidence for stress effects on cancer incidence (which includes both initiation and early progression of precursor lesions) is mixed across stress definitions and cancer types, with recent systematic reviews/meta-analyses suggesting an overall association between stress-related conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety, PTSD, and some chronic stress exposures such as social isolation) and increased cancer risk, whereas other stressors show weaker or null effects (Jia et al., 2017; van Tuijl et al., 2023; Yang and Jiang, 2023). In general, prospective or registry-based studies with long follow-up periods and large sample sizes were more likely to demonstrate positive associations than retrospective studies or those with short follow-up periods. Further, results may differ across tumor types. A key source of inconsistency may be the timing of distress assessment: if stress physiology primarily accelerates the progression of existing precursor lesions to invasive disease, a mechanism strongly supported by preclinical data, then the interval between distress measurement and cancer diagnosis becomes critical. Studies assessing recent distress are more likely to capture this promotional window than those evaluating lifetime exposure, which potentially may help explain null findings in some cohorts. Relatedly, cumulative exposure to multiple forms of distress may confer greater risk than single or no exposures (Roberts et al., 2023; Shutta et al., 2021). For example, relatively consistent positive associations of depression have been observed for breast and ovarian cancers, while PTSD has been associated only with ovarian cancer risk (Roberts et al., 2023). It is possible that different tumor types may have varying sensitivity to SNS/HPA axis activation, depending on factors such as tumor innervation or the specific TME context of each cancer. These observations imply that the correlation between stress and cancer may be contingent upon tumor-specific biological characteristics, the timing and persistence of distress exposure, and the specific outcome assessed. Nonetheless, despite discrepancies in epidemiological findings concerning cancer incidence, the evidence connecting chronic stress to tumor progression and immune dysregulation remains considerably more consistent.

In addition, while rodent models (e.g., restraint stress, chronic social defeat, social isolation) provide experimental control and establish causal links between stress signaling and myeloid-driven immunosuppression, they only approximate the type, timescale, and complexity of stress scenarios experienced by patients with cancer. Rodent models typically rely on single, standardized stressors, which provide strong experimental control and allow causal inference, but do not capture the multifactorial and evolving nature of human psychological stress. In clinical contexts, distress is rarely a single, discrete exposure; instead, it is often multifactorial and dynamic, arising from diagnosis threat, uncertainty, symptom burden, treatment cycles (chemotherapy, surgery, immunotherapy), financial toxicity, caregiving demands, disrupted sleep/circadian rhythms, and social determinants of health, stressors that vary substantially across individuals and time. This contrasts with standardized animal paradigms that typically impose uniform intensity and timing and incompletely capture key human features such as cognitive appraisal, controllability, predictability, and meaning-making, which are central determinants of neuroendocrine and immune outputs. In addition, species differences in stress physiology must be considered (e.g., rodents are primarily corticosterone-dominant, whereas humans are cortisol-dominant), as well as neuroendocrine rhythms, housing temperature, and baseline immune set-points, which can influence immune readouts and tumor trajectories in preclinical systems. Another critical consideration is the divergent metabolic responses to chronic stress between species (Shutta et al., 2021). While mice typically lose weight under sustained stress (Cantú-Ruiz et al., 2025), humans often gain weight, particularly visceral adiposity (Wardle et al., 2011; Xiao et al., 2020). Although this divergence is largely driven by behavioral differences, such as stress-induced alterations in feeding behaviors and physical activity in both species, it nonetheless has substantial implications for translational comparability. Given that metabolic status and adiposity directly modulate immune function and the TME, the divergent metabolic responses to stress in mice and humans represent a critical translational barrier. Therefore, accounting for these interspecies differences is essential when interpreting preclinical findings and designing interventions intended for human populations.

Importantly, however, convergent clinical observations nonetheless support the biological relevance of these pathways: cancer patients exhibit high rates of anxiety/depression and distress related to disease/treatment demands, HPA-axis dysregulation is common in oncology populations, and distress/hormonal alterations have been linked to tumor-immune features and outcomes, including stress-hormone associations with inflammatory cytokines and attenuated T-cell function in patient tumor milieus (e.g., ascites), biobehavioral associations with myeloid pro-metastatic programs in human tumors, and poorer responses to immune checkpoint blockade in patients with elevated pretreatment emotional distress (Kanter et al., 2024; Lee and Singh, 2021; Ungvari et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2024). Collectively, these considerations underscore the need for integrative translational approaches, combining mechanistic animal studies with longitudinal patient cohorts that quantify distress (e.g., perceived stress, depression/anxiety), neuroendocrine outputs (diurnal cortisol slope, catecholamines), and tumor/immune profiling, to better map which components of stress biology are most clinically actionable.

6.1 Toward an integrated, stepped-care treatment model

The effectiveness of stress-targeted interventions may depend on timing across the cancer continuum, including diagnosis, perioperative periods, active treatment, recurrence, and survivorship, as distinct phases may present different neuroimmune and behavioral vulnerabilities. The evidence reviewed suggests that the most effective clinical translation may arise from integrated and personalized approaches that synergistically combine behavioral and pharmacological strategies within a stepped-care framework. This model begins with universal screening for distress and key resilience factors (e.g., social support, optimism, adaptive coping) using validated instruments such as the NCCN Distress Thermometer and Brief Resilience Scale (Chang et al., 2022; Graham-Wisener et al., 2021; Manigault et al., 2022; Nardella et al., 2025). Assessment of resilience can help stratify risk: patients with lower baseline resilience may represent a high-risk group for adaptive stress pathophysiology (Chang et al., 2022) and thus benefit from earlier, more intensive prophylactic support. Initial treatment would employ first-line, behavioral interventions (e.g., psychoeducation, mindfulness) to reduce basal stress physiology and systemic inflammation. For patients with moderate-to-severe symptoms, this foundation can be augmented with evidence-based psychotherapy (e.g., CBSM) combined with targeted pharmacotherapy, where behavioral pre-treatment may optimize the safety and efficacy of subsequent cancer therapeutic drugs.

Building on this mechanistic rationale outlined above, pharmacological agents can be strategically selected and sequenced based on dominant pathophysiology and disease phase to disrupt stress-driven immunosuppression across peripheral and central nodes of the stress–immune–cancer axis.

In the periphery, β-adrenergic blockade represents a particularly promising strategy to counteract catecholamine-mediated immunosuppression, particularly during high-risk phases such as perioperative stress or advanced disease. Early human evidence supports translational relevance: a phase I trial combining propranolol with pembrolizumab in metastatic melanoma demonstrated safety and a notable objective response rate (78%), with a recommended phase II dose of propranolol (30 mg BID) (Gandhi et al., 2021), and multiple ongoing interventional trials are evaluating β-blockers in combination with ICIs (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, durvalumab, tremelimumab) across melanoma, urothelial, and gastrointestinal malignancies (Carnet Le Provost et al., 2023). Beyond ICIs, β-blockade is also being tested to mitigate cytokine release syndrome in CAR-T cell therapy and has been evaluated in combination with chemotherapy, chemoradiation, and targeted agents (e.g., VEGF and EGFR inhibitors), with signals of improved progression-free and overall survival in selected contexts, albeit with heterogeneous outcomes (Cavalu et al., 2024; Massalee and Cao, 2023; Yıldız et al., 2025) (Table 3).Table 3 Summary of clinical trials involving beta-blockers in cancer treatment.

Centrally, microglial modulators may be leveraged, where approved agents are available, to directly target neuroinflammation in patients with prominent cancer-related cognitive or mood disturbances, a need that is most pronounced during active treatment and survivorship phases. For severe or refractory cases, this framework supports integrating specialized psychiatric care and considering novel agents within a coordinated model that includes expertise in oncology, psychiatry, and neurology. Collectively, this stepped, mechanism-based strategy integrates behavioral (top-down, psychophysiological regulation), peripheral pharmacological, and central pharmacological interventions to enable personalized, multinode disruption of the stress-immune-cancer axis, moving beyond unimodal therapeutic paradigms toward a model in which mental health and tumor biology are addressed concurrently.
