ABSTRACT

Remimazolam‐based general anesthesia provided stable hemodynamics and rapid, clear emergence in a patient with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease undergoing Zephyr endobronchial valve placement, suggesting that remimazolam may be a feasible anesthetic option for this procedure in patients with limited cardiopulmonary reserve.

1 Introduction

Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) with endobronchial one‐way valves is a minimally invasive treatment for patients with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) who remain symptomatic despite optimal therapy [1]. The Zephyr endobronchial valve (Pulmonx, California, USA) is deployed via flexible bronchoscopy to occlude target bronchi in emphysematous regions, reducing hyperinflation and redirecting airflow. This procedure has been shown to produce clinically significant improvements in lung function, exercise capacity, and quality of life [2, 3].

General anesthesia is typically used for this procedure as it suppresses airway reflexes, improves patient tolerance, and can shorten the procedure [4, 5, 6]. Total intravenous anesthesia with propofol is often preferred over volatile anesthesia because of its ability to provide reliable titratability during repeated airway manipulations and preserve hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction [7]. However, propofol can cause severe circulatory suppression [8], which poses a risk for patients with severe COPD who are prone to pulmonary hypertension (PH) and right ventricular dysfunction [9]. Particular caution is required during the induction of general anesthesia as the combination of anesthetic‐induced hypotension and increased pulmonary vascular resistance due to positive‐pressure ventilation may acutely worsen the pulmonary‐to‐systemic pressure ratio, potentially leading to a pulmonary hypertensive crisis [10].

In contrast, remimazolam, a novel short‐acting benzodiazepine, causes less circulatory depression and allows for a rapid emergence following antagonism with flumazenil compared to propofol [11]. This suggests that remimazolam may be a suitable alternative to propofol for Zephyr endobronchial valve placement in this patient population. Here, we report a single case of a patient with severe COPD who successfully underwent Zephyr endobronchial valve placement under remimazolam‐based general anesthesia.

2 Case History/Examination

A 54‐year‐old man (161 cm, 74 kg) with severe COPD was scheduled for Zephyr endobronchial valve placement under general anesthesia. Despite comprehensive medical management, including home oxygen therapy (1 L/min at rest, 3 L/min with exertion), optimal medication, and a structured pulmonary rehabilitation program, his dyspnea on exertion persisted. His only other significant comorbidity was well‐controlled hypertension.

Preoperative evaluation revealed very severe airflow limitation with hyperinflation on pulmonary function testing: forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity ratio (FEV1/FVC) 25.9% and percent predicted vital capacity (%VC) 66.2%. Chest computed tomography demonstrated marked emphysematous changes. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a normal left ventricular ejection fraction (60%) but a mildly enlarged right ventricle with reduced function (right ventricular fractional area change 27%) and a borderline elevated estimated pulmonary artery pressure (tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient 27 mmHg). Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis on room air revealed compensated respiratory acidosis (pH 7.402, PaCO2 52.6 mmHg, PaO2 65.9 mmHg, HCO3 − 32.1 mmol/L, and base excess +6.3 mmol/L).

3 Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

No premedication was administered. After applying standard monitors, including an electrocardiogram, pulse oximetry, and non‐invasive blood pressure, a FloTrac sensor (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) was placed in the left radial artery to monitor arterial pressure‐based cardiac output. Anesthesia was then induced with a bolus of remimazolam (0.1 mg/kg) and a continuous infusion of remifentanil (0.5 μg/kg/min). Tracheal intubation was facilitated with rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) using an 8.5‐mm internal‐diameter endotracheal tube (ETT). Although no vasoactive agents were administered during induction, the lowest mean arterial pressure (MAP) was 66 mmHg, and cardiopulmonary conditions remained stable throughout the induction period. Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of remimazolam (0.6–1.0 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.3–0.5 μg/kg/min), titrated to maintain a bispectral index (BIS) between 40 and 60. Rocuronium was incrementally administered to keep the train‐of‐four (TOF) count at one or less, with the stimulating electrode placed across the ulnar nerve to ensure reliable assessment [12]. Hemodynamics remained stable throughout the procedure; MAP was maintained at or above 65 mmHg with a low‐dose noradrenaline infusion (0.05–0.08 μg/kg/min), and the cardiac index remained above 2.2 L/min/m2. Mechanical ventilation was set with an FiO2 of 0.6, a tidal volume of approximately 6 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 10 breaths/min, and a positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH2O, with an inspiratory‐to‐expiratory ratio of 1:3. To minimize dynamic hyperinflation, a degree of permissive hypercapnia was accepted. These settings maintained SpO2 in the high 90s, and the absence of auto‐PEEP was confirmed by capnography and flow–volume loops. No problematic coughing or inadvertent movement occurred throughout the procedure.

The surgical procedure was performed under flexible bronchoscopy without complications. Based on preoperative fissure analysis and Chartis collateral ventilation assessment, three Zephyr valves were successfully deployed in the left upper lobe.

Following the final valve placement, the ETT was exchanged for a size 4 supraglottic airway (i‐gel; Intersurgical, Wokingham, UK) to minimize coughing during emergence. Infusions of remimazolam and remifentanil were then discontinued. After 10 min, upon confirming signs of awakening and a TOF count of 1, flumazenil (0.2 mg) and sugammadex (400 mg) were administered. Immediately before i‐gel removal, PaCO2 was already elevated to 59.6 mmHg, reflecting the patient's baseline chronic hypercapnia and the permissive hypercapnia accepted during mechanical ventilation (Table 1). Within 1 min after administration of flumazenil and sugammadex, the patient opened his eyes and followed commands, and the i‐gel was removed without coughing. Immediately after i‐gel removal, ABG analysis showed respiratory acidosis (pH 7.285, PaCO2 69.2 mmHg). Despite this, the patient maintained regular spontaneous breathing at 10–12 breaths/min and satisfactory oxygenation without assisted ventilation. The respiratory acidosis subsequently improved over time (Table 1). Because pain after Zephyr endobronchial valve placement is generally minimal, fentanyl was not administered; instead, acetaminophen 1 g was administered before completing the procedure. The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit on 3 L/min oxygen via face mask for close monitoring due to his severe COPD and high risk of pneumothorax. Oxygenation remained satisfactory, and PaCO2 further decreased to 56.2 mmHg on ICU admission; supplemental oxygen was subsequently reduced to 1 L/min. Re‐sedation and re‐curarization did not occur. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no pneumothorax or respiratory failure. The patient was transferred to the ward on postoperative day (POD) 4 to begin rehabilitation and was discharged on POD 15.

4 Conclusion and Results

Remimazolam‐based general anesthesia was associated with stable hemodynamics, particularly during induction, and rapid, clear emergence in a patient with severe COPD undergoing Zephyr endobronchial valve placement. This single‐case observation suggests that remimazolam may be a feasible anesthetic option for high‐risk patients with severe COPD undergoing this procedure. Prospective comparative studies are required to evaluate its safety and effectiveness relative to propofol and to define optimal anesthetic practice.

5 Discussion

This case demonstrates the successful use of remimazolam for general anesthesia in a patient with severe COPD undergoing Zephyr endobronchial valve placement. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of this procedure performed under remimazolam‐based general anesthesia. For patients with severe COPD and limited cardiopulmonary reserve, anesthetic management that ensures stable hemodynamics, particularly during induction, and timely, clear emergence is paramount.

Patients with severe COPD often present with PH and right heart dysfunction [9], which predisposes them to hemodynamic instability. During induction, anesthetic‐induced hypotension and increases in pulmonary vascular resistance caused by positive‐pressure ventilation may worsen the pulmonary‐to‐systemic pressure ratio and precipitate a pulmonary hypertensive crisis. Conversely, an overly light anesthetic depth to preserve blood pressure is also undesirable as adequate depth is necessary to suppress airway reflexes, blunt the stress‐related increase in oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production [4, 13], and limit the rise in pulmonary vascular resistance. Therefore, careful agent selection and titration are essential. Hypotension is the most common anesthesia‐related event during BLVR [5]. While propofol is the conventional anesthetic, hypotension is a frequent adverse effect [8]. In contrast, remimazolam produces minimal cardiovascular depression and is associated with a lower incidence of hypotension after anesthesia induction [14, 15], and intraoperative hypotension [11, 16, 17, 18]. These findings from the literature provided the rationale for selecting remimazolam in this patient. In this case, the lowest MAP during induction was 66 mmHg without vasoactive support, and intraoperative MAP was maintained at or above 65 mmHg with low‐dose noradrenaline (0.05–0.08 μg/kg/min). Overall, the hemodynamic stability observed in this case is consistent with previous reports and suggests that remimazolam may be a feasible option for Zephyr endobronchial valve placement in high‐risk patients with severe COPD.

Patients with severe COPD are at high risk of postoperative respiratory complications, making a prompt and clear emergence desirable. Comparative studies suggest that the rapid metabolism of remimazolam and its specific antagonism by flumazenil facilitate fast and reliable emergence with a lower risk of respiratory depression compared to propofol [11, 16, 19], without increasing the risk of postoperative delirium [17, 18, 20, 21]. However, re‐sedation after flumazenil remains possible [19]. In this case, emergence was rapid and smooth, without re‐sedation, consistent with the potential benefits reported in previous studies.

Zephyr endobronchial valve placement involves sustained airway stimulation, and vigorous coughing at emergence may dislodge newly deployed valves or increase the risk of pneumothorax. Propofol is well‐known for its ability to attenuate airway reflexes and reduce coughing at emergence compared with inhalational anesthetics [22, 23]. Given the patient's high‐risk profile and the fact that the effect of remimazolam on emergence coughing has not been fully established, we proactively exchanged the ETT for a supraglottic airway before emergence to minimize this risk [24].

While muscle relaxation is crucial for suppressing intraoperative coughing during BLVR, its complete reversal is equally important to prevent postoperative respiratory complications [25, 26]. In terms of interactions, the potentiating effect of remimazolam on rocuronium‐induced neuromuscular block is weaker than that of sevoflurane and comparable to that of propofol [27]. Furthermore, remimazolam does not appear to inhibit the antagonistic effect of sugammadex on rocuronium‐induced neuromuscular blockade [28]. In this case, neuromuscular blockade was successfully reversed with sugammadex, and no residual blockade was observed, supporting the feasibility of rocuronium use during remimazolam‐based anesthesia for BLVR with appropriate neuromuscular monitoring and reversal.

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single patient and therefore cannot establish the superiority of remimazolam over propofol for Zephyr endobronchial valve placement. Second, although this patient had severe COPD with chronic hypercapnia, right ventricular dysfunction was mild, pulmonary hypertension was borderline, and his overall cardiac function was not profoundly impaired. Our findings therefore may not be generalizable to patients with more advanced pulmonary hypertension or severe right heart failure. Third, the transient respiratory acidosis immediately after i‐gel removal was likely multifactorial, with contributions from baseline chronic hypercapnia, permissive hypercapnia during mechanical ventilation, and transient residual effects of remimazolam and remifentanil during emergence; however, the relative contribution of each factor was not assessed. The short‐acting properties of remimazolam and remifentanil, together with flumazenil reversal of remimazolam, may have facilitated the prompt recovery of ventilation, although this possibility remains speculative. Further prospective comparative studies are needed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of remimazolam relative to propofol and establish optimal anesthetic management strategies.
