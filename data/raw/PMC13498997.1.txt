Key Points

Question

What factors influence the implementation and outcomes of a mobile integrated health (MIH) posthospital discharge program for patients with heart failure?

Findings

In this qualitative study of 20 patients and 25 stakeholders nested within the MIH intervention group of the Using Mobile Integrated Health and Telehealth to Support Transitions of Care Among Heart Failure Patients trial, adoption and implementation were facilitated by high patient satisfaction, leadership engagement, interdisciplinary coordination, and patient trust, but limited by reimbursement and regulatory constraints.

Meaning

These findings suggest that MIH programs are valued by patients and health systems but require supportive policy and payment models to achieve sustainability and scale.

This qualitative study examines factors associated with the implementation of a mobile integrated health program for patients with heart failure in an urban setting, by interviewing stakeholders and patients participating in a randomized clinical trial of mobile integrated health.

Importance

Mobile integrated health (MIH) is a promising model for improving transitions of care for patients with heart failure (HF). National implementation remains limited due to insufficient understanding of barriers and facilitators to adoption and sustainability.

Objective

To identify factors associated with the implementation of an MIH program for patients with HF in an urban setting, using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework.

Design, Setting, and Participants

This qualitative study was embedded in the Using Mobile Integrated Health and Telehealth to Support Transitions of Care Among Heart Failure Patients (MIGHTy-Heart) randomized clinical trial conducted from January 2021 to September 2024 at 2 large urban health systems in New York, New York. Qualitative data were generated through semistructured interviews with patients and stakeholders (clinicians, paramedics, caregivers, and program leaders). Data were analyzed from January 2022 through August 2023.

Exposure

The MIH intervention included home visits from community paramedics, ongoing nurse care coordination, and facilitated telehealth consultations.

Main Outcomes and Measures

Five RE-AIM domains were assessed using directed content analysis of interview data. Quantitative reach was measured by comparing adults with HF offered access to MIH to all adults with HF receiving care at the health systems during the study period, and effectiveness data were summarized descriptively from the parent MIGHTy-Heart trial. Adoption was measured by comparing facilitators and barriers to adoption. Implementation was assessed through service utilization and thematic analysis. Maintenance included willingness to recommend MIH and institutional continuation plans.

Results

A total of 73 343 patients (51 420 [70%] aged ≥65 years; 39 979 [55%] male) were treated for HF across both health systems during the trial period, including 1005 who were enrolled in the trial and randomized to MIH, among whom 414 received at least 1 MIH visit; 20 patients and 25 stakeholders were interviewed for qualitative results. Regarding reach, MIH participants vs all patients with HF were similar with respect to age and sex, but had greater racial and ethnic diversity (MIH group: 50% Black or African American, 27% Hispanic or Latino, and 25% White; all HF patients: 9% Black or African American, 4% Hispanic or Latino, and 42% White). Regarding effectiveness, there was no statistically significant decrease in 30-day all-cause readmissions or Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire scores among MIH participants vs the comparator group (mean difference, 1.83; 95% CI, −0.75 to 4.40; P = .16). Adoption was promoted by institutional alignment, and implementation was supported by leadership engagement, interdisciplinary coordination, and patient trust. Barriers included misaligned reimbursement and regulatory constraints. Sustainability plans reflected MIH’s perceived value to patients and staff.

Conclusions and Relevance

Despite the primary trial showing no difference in 30-day readmissions or Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire scores between MIH and the transitions of care coordinator control group, this qualitative study found that MIH was well received and aligned with institutional goals. Sustaining and scaling MIH may require supportive reimbursement models and policy reforms.

Introduction

Mobile integrated health (MIH) is an emerging model of health care delivery that provides patient-centered, nonemergency services in the home or community setting.1 MIH is delivered by interdisciplinary teams, including nurse care coordinators, community paramedics, and emergency medical technicians. In the US, there are more than 150 MIH programs in operation.2 Compared with home health or visiting nurse services and hospital-at-home programs, MIH differs in the type and breadth of care by offering on-demand, acute or chronic, and interdisciplinary care that is often integrated with telemedicine.1,3

MIH programs have demonstrated potential to reduce patient transports to emergency departments (EDs), 30-day hospital readmissions, and overall health care costs while also achieving high levels of patient and clinician satisfaction.1 However, MIH programs vary substantially in clinical protocols and their use of telehealth, clinician training, care coordination models, and postvisit follow-up.1,3 As more organizations seek to initiate or expand access to MIH programs, sharing knowledge about implementation facilitators and barriers and promoting a standardized approach to evaluating the effectiveness of programs and which components of the interventions contribute to effectiveness could help improve clinical care. To date, limited work has reconciled the discrepancy that often emerges between patient and clinician enthusiasm for MIH programs and the inconsistent effects on readmissions observed in clinical trials. Addressing this gap requires an integrated implementation-effectiveness analysis capable of identifying which components of MIH generate value, for whom, and under what delivery conditions.

We conducted a qualitative study embedded in the Using Mobile Integrated Health and Telehealth to Support Transitions of Care among Heart Failure Patients (MIGHTy-Heart) trial4 to examine the implementation of an MIH program for patients discharged after an admission for heart failure (HF) at 2 large urban health systems using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework5 to identify predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors relevant to successful deployment and sustainability of MIH.

Methods

This qualitative study was approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York institutional review board (protocol No. 20-08-329-380). All participants provided informed consent, including permission to record and publish deidentified quotes (eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1). This qualitative study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline. For complete details on our methods, see eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1.

MIGHTy-Heart Trial

This qualitative study was embedded in the MIGHTy-Heart trial.4 MIGHTy Heart was a randomized clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04662541) comparing the effectiveness of MIH vs a transitions of care nurse coordinator intervention on 30-day hospital readmissions and patient-reported health status among adults with HF. The methods and the primary outcome results have been reported previously,4,6 and the focus of this analysis is on the MIH group and the evaluation of MIH.

Theoretical Framework

We used the RE-AIM framework constructs to comprehensively evaluate the MIH intervention across 5 key dimensions. Dimensions were assessed using qualitative data sources augmented with quantitative descriptive data where available.

Reach was defined as the extent to which our target population accessed the MIH intervention. We assessed reach by comparing demographic characteristics of several cohorts of patients: (1) participants enrolled in the trial vs all patients approached to determine the reach of the trial; (2) participants randomized to receive MIH in the trial vs all patients hospitalized with HF across the 2 participating health systems during the study period to determine the reach of access to the MIH intervention, and (3) participants who received a community paramedicine visit as part of the MIH intervention vs all patients hospitalized with HF across the 2 participating health systems during the study period to determine the reach of receipt of the MIH intervention. Data sources included electronic health records (EHRs), MIH program metrics, and patient surveys. Race and ethnicity were obtained through self-report and categorized as Asian; Black or African American; multiple races, other (eg, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander), or missing; and White, as well as Hispanic or Latino and not Hispanic or Latino ethnicity.

Effectiveness evaluated the patient-centered outcomes associated with the MIH intervention. Specifically, we measured 30-day health status and hospital readmissions among participants randomized to MIH compared with those randomized to receive care from a transitions of care coordinator in the MIGHTy-Heart trial. Effectiveness was evaluated using EHRs and patient surveys.

Adoption examined the uptake of MIH from multiple stakeholder perspectives, including patients, clinicians, and organizational leadership. Implementation focused on identifying facilitators and barriers encountered during MIH program delivery. Maintenance assessed factors that would support the sustainability of MIH programming in the future. Adoption, implementation, and maintenance were evaluated qualitatively through patient and stakeholder interviews, providing insights into adoption patterns, how the intervention was executed in practice settings, and long-term viability considerations, respectively.

Qualitative Data Sources and Analysis

Qualitative data sources included interviews with 2 groups of key stakeholders: MIGHTy-Heart patient participants and health care professional stakeholders, including physicians and nurses with expertise in HF, MIH community paramedics, MIH program directors or executive leaders in digital innovation or transformation, members of the stakeholder advisory board for the trial, clinicians involved in the day-to-day operations of the participating MIH programs, and regional and national leaders and experts in MIH. National MIH leaders were intentionally included to provide contextual variation in regulatory environments, reimbursement models, and scope-of-practice frameworks.

A semistructured interview guide was created based on the RE-AIM framework and tailored to patient or stakeholder participants (eAppendix 3 in Supplement 1). Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes or concepts emerged after 3 consecutive interviews. Interviews lasted between 30 and 60 minutes and were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Interviews were analyzed by 4 trained qualitative researchers (M.R.T., M.E., L.S.T., M.G.) using qualitative analysis software (Dedoose version 8.0). A directed content analysis method underpinned the study.7 The 5 RE-AIM dimensions formed the basis of the coding scheme. Subthemes were added inductively based on emerging concepts in the data. A minimum of 2 reviewers (M.R.T., M.E., L.S.T., M.G.) coded each transcript. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Quantitative Data Sources and Analysis

Quantitative data sources for descriptive purposes were EHRs, MIH program surveys, and patient surveys. EHRs provided data on participants’ health care utilization, specifically 30-day all-cause readmissions. MIH program surveys completed by community paramedics after each home visit measured MIH program utilization. Patient surveys provided data on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and health status using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire.8 We report descriptive statistics of demographic characteristics to evaluate reach. We evaluated effectiveness by comparing 30-day health status and readmission rates between the 2 comparator groups using weighted logistic regression models, as reported elsewhere.6 We computed descriptive statistics to determine the adoption of the MIH program.

Results

Qualitative Interview Participants

A total of 73 343 patients (51 420 [70%] aged ≥65 years; 39 979 [55%] male) were treated for HF across both health systems during the trial period, including 1005 who were enrolled in the trial and randomized to MIH, among whom 414 received at least 1 MIH visit. The 20 patient participants (mean [SD] age, 62 [14] years; 13 [65%] female) who completed qualitative interviews were evenly distributed across the 2 health systems in which MIGHTy-Heart was recruiting (10 participants at each site). Among these, 12 (60%) were Black, 8 (40%) identified as Hispanic, and 3 (15%) were White. Nearly half reported not having enough financial resources (9 patients [45%]), and 8 patients (40%) had a high school education or less. All resided in 4 of the 5 boroughs in New York: 8 patients (40%) from Manhattan, 6 patients (30%) from Brooklyn, 5 patients (25%) from Queens, and 1 patient (5%) from Staten Island.

The 25 stakeholder participants who completed qualitative interviews included 6 health care professionals (4 cardiologists and 2 nurses), 8 MIH staff members involved in delivering the MIH intervention (4 paramedics, 1 dispatcher, and 3 nurse care coordinators), 2 caregivers of patients with HF, and 9 MIH program directors. Participants worked in 4 US states: New York, Pennsylvania, Texas, and Massachusetts.

Effectiveness

The primary trial results have been published elsewhere.6 In brief, there was no significant adjusted difference in 30-day health status or all-cause readmission rates between the MIH and TOCC groups. There was a significant age × treatment interaction, with younger participants in the MIH group demonstrating larger improvements in health status.

Adoption

The key subthemes relating to adoption, implementation, and maintenance of MIH are summarized in the Figure and described in more detail with illustrative quotes in the eTable in Supplement 1. In qualitative interviews, major facilitators to adoption included patients’ desires for closer monitoring and care in the home, clinicians’ desires for better remote patient management, the presence of key partners to support initial pilot testing, and the COVID-19 pandemic enabling flexibility. Participants also reported that financial and regulatory considerations were 2 additional barriers to adoption. Many MIH directors discussed the challenges in demonstrating financial value to institutional leaders, given the limited revenue generated by MIH, and that the specific metrics that MIH improves may not align with the institution’s financial strategies. One director reported: “The type of model we had worked better as a grow-quickly model, but because there wasn’t a great reimbursement model, there would have to be a focus on trying to narrow it to certain populations. The model wasn’t really well designed for being extremely selective on the population. There wasn’t enough risk. There weren’t enough capitated patients.”

Implementation

In interviews, the primary predisposing factors for implementation that were discussed included emergency medical services (EMS) skills and knowledge, the increasing value of digital health, clinician beliefs about types of patients who benefit most from MIH, and strong institutional support. The participants described how the comprehensiveness and quality of care provided during MIH visits, interdisciplinary team integration, patient trust in community paramedics, and telehealth functionality enabled MIH implementation. One patient stated, “If anybody was to go through what I went through that day [an MIH visit], they wouldn’t be going to the hospital as much.” However, they also reported that some patients experienced reticence and communication challenges, and some clinicians experienced workflow and logistical issues with the MIH team. Operating MIH programs were frequently logistically fraught because of the need to carefully and tightly manage clinician time; allocate limited resources, such as paramedics and ambulances, effectively; and implement at a time when other institutional initiatives can help ease rather than complicate logistic challenges. Some participants noted that these workflow and logistic issues were amplified when there was a lack of initial institutional investment and support when launching the program, which made it more difficult to demonstrate program success and garner further institutional support. One MIH director explained, “For a long time, it had been run with bubble gum and duct tape, trying to find doctors who would answer a call. It was run with per diem dollars and moonlighting.”

Maintenance

During interviews, participants described how the ability of MIH programs to demonstrate value and financial sustainability, embrace future innovations and improvements, and show high patient satisfaction with MIH reinforced successful maintenance. Participants also noted several barriers to sustained maintenance of MIH, including challenges expanding program capacity and accessibility to meet patients’ needs and managing the dynamics of interdisciplinary care teams. One clinician stated, “Relationships between the heart failure group and the emergency room doctors are probably going to be beneficial.” Participants also emphasized that limited reimbursement and scope of practice because of the regulatory and health policy landscapes were another significant barrier to maintenance. One director explained, “EMS is still in the situation of being paid only by the federal government for transportation. Now, EMS is doing medical care, they’re making diagnoses…these folks can contribute a whole lot more than just driving somebody somewhere.”

Discussion

This qualitative study embedded within the MIGHTy-Heart trial revealed numerous strengths of MIH, including its ability to reach diverse patient populations. Although MIH did not reduce 30-day readmissions or improve overall health status compared with TOCC in the primary trial, participants consistently described meaningful benefits related to high patient, clinician, and caregiver satisfaction. These differences highlight a divergence between traditional clinical metrics and the dimensions of value experienced by patients and clinicians. Through qualitative interviews with stakeholders and key leaders, we identified program components perceived as most effective in implementation. Exploratory subgroup analyses that we previously reported6 showed effect modification by age, with younger participants benefiting more, which were reinforced by qualitative interviews describing key subgroups as benefitting the most, suggesting that effectiveness varied across patient subgroups. These findings align with emerging evidence on the role of patient characteristics, such as frailty, in determining MIH benefit.1 Understanding which factors drive effectiveness in specific populations can inform the development of targeted interventions optimized for those who stand to benefit most.

There is a particularly urgent need for postdischarge support among patients with HF who become trapped in cycles of repeated hospitalizations,9 a population that is increasing rather than decreasing nationally.10 MIH can meet patients’ persistent clinical and social needs that may result in frequent rehospitalizations. This finding is consistent with another systematic review of MIH programs that also reported high patient and clinician satisfaction rates and low ED transport rates among older adults receiving MIH.1 Thus, MIH may be well positioned to help address persistent high rehospitalization rates among patients with HF.

In our study, stakeholders from multiple states (New York, Pennsylvania, Texas, and Massachusetts) described how MIH programs were driven by the specific clinical and social needs of their patient populations. Although core program elements remained similar, programs were modified through iterative, adaptive cycles to align with community needs. This also included alignment with the unique regulatory contexts and financial support in each site. A 2023 survey of MIH programs nationally showed that unsustained resources, staffing, and funding and challenges in demonstrating return on investment were the top reasons that MIH programs were discontinued in the US.2 Multiple interview participants agreed that MIH is most compatible with value-based care payment models rather than fee-for-service models. Prior studies have demonstrated that MIH could be cost effective,11,12,13 but interview participants pointed out that this could vary depending on the size and expense of a particular MIH program. Despite momentum within the US health care system to move toward value-based care, it remains elusive in certain settings,14,15 which tempers enthusiasm for expensive programs with limited returns on investment like MIH.

In this context, we found that a key indicator of successful MIH implementation was the ability to select appropriate outcome metrics that were demonstrably improved by MIH and that aligned with the goals and values of key decision-makers within institutions. For example, MIH was appealing to some decision-makers who were primarily interested in reducing ED overcrowding, which is directly improved by MIH (which reduces ED transport rates1,16). Beyond perceived value, preliminary institutional planning discussions within both health systems indicated continued interest in MIH integration into hospital-at-home programs and population health initiatives,13,17,18 although funding decisions are contingent on regulatory and reimbursement developments.

Beyond institutional support, regulatory constraints also partially contribute to the financial constraints that leaders of MIH programs face. Currently, paramedic reimbursement is generally contingent on transporting patients to EDs due to a national law that dates back to the original formation of Medicare in the 1960s.19 Additionally, in most states, the paramedic scope of practice is legally defined in state laws or regulations, which dictate the types of point-of-care testing and interventions that can be conducted in the home by paramedics.20 Because these regulations are typically highly restrictive, this further limits the ability of traditional MIH programs to diagnose and treat patients in the home. Despite these challenges, some interview participants discussed ongoing efforts within multiple professional organizations to lobby for more favorable laws, both on the state and federal levels, that would allow MIH to better support patients in the home.

Taken together, our findings suggest that selecting outcome metrics that align with institutional financial priorities (eg, ED avoidance rather than readmissions alone), securing robust institutional investment and operational infrastructure before launch, anticipating regulatory and scope-of-practice constraints as a function of state-based contexts, and building interdisciplinary relationships proactively between emergency medicine and subspeciality clinicians were key drivers of MIH program success. MIH programs frequently reduce ED transports,1 but their financial viability is fundamentally constrained by Medicare’s transport-based EMS reimbursement structure, which limits staffing levels and clinical scope, ultimately shaping program effectiveness and sustainability. Our findings suggest that durable scale will require explicit reimbursement mechanisms for MIH. Until then, identifying the highest-risk populations and tailoring programs to these groups is likely the most optimal use of this finite resource. In fact, in the primary trial, the patients with the most advanced HF (ie, New York Heart Association class IV) were excluded precisely because their care teams did not want to risk losing access to MIH through randomization. This integrated view underscores that MIH success depends not only on clinical execution but also on alignment across financial, regulatory, and organizational domains.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include that the data originated from participants enrolled in a randomized clinical trial conducted in a single city, and interviews represent the views of those who agreed to be interviewed, introducing potential selection bias toward those with more favorable views of MIH. Thus, the implementation data and perspectives described may not represent the breadth of perspectives when MIH is implemented in other settings, particularly rural settings. Patient interviews may also be subject to social desirability bias, with participants underreporting negative experiences. Additionally, only 2 caregivers participated, limiting insight into caregiver perspectives. Furthermore, these findings reflect a single MIH program design, and one that excluded patients with advanced HF; other MIH models serving other patient populations may encounter different barriers and facilitators.

Conclusions

In this qualitative study, a model of community-based health care delivery for patients discharged with HF was well-received and aligned with institutional priorities, but its sustainability was limited by misaligned reimbursement structures and regulatory constraints on the scope of practice of EMS. Successful implementation of MIH must be adapted to patient and clinician needs, with tracked metrics that align with institutional financial priorities. Future work should prioritize identifying patient subgroups most likely to benefit, defining the core MIH components that drive outcomes, and testing reimbursement models capable of supporting sustained delivery.
