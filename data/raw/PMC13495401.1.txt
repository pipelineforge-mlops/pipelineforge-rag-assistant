Background

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for evidence on treatment efficacy but face limitations: restrictive eligibility criteria exclude real-world populations, head-to-head comparisons between approved regimens are rare, and absolute effectiveness in unselected cohorts often differs from trial results. High-quality cancer registries address these gaps by documenting treatment reality and quantifying effectiveness outside trial settings. We describe how the breadth and depth of prospective, longitudinal oncology registry data enable advanced research complementing RCTs.

Materials and methods

The iOMEDICO oncologist and hematologist network currently operates nine prospective, multicenter registry platforms across major cancer types in Germany. The registries employ regulatory-grade electronic data capture with audit trails, continuous data management, and consecutive patient enrollment. Beyond demographics and clinical variables, platforms systematically collect comorbidities, comprehensive biomarker data, complete treatment pathways, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).

Results

More than 55 000 patients have been documented so far across >400 sites representing diverse care settings. Our registry data enable patient-centered research in diverse applications: (i) characterizations of patient populations, treatment patterns, and outcomes to evaluate the current standards of care, identify unmet needs, and follow developments over time; (ii) predictive and prognostic model developments; (iii) comparative effectiveness research such as target trial emulation for head-to-head treatment comparisons; (iv) translational research into biomarker prevalence; and (v) evaluation of PROs.

Conclusion

Well-designed prospective, longitudinal registries collecting broad and deep real-world data complement RCTs, inform Health Technology Assessments, and fill knowledge gaps by creating treatment transparency, addressing evidence gaps, and providing realistic outcome expectations for the heterogeneous populations in routine care.

Introduction

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for establishing treatment efficacy, yet numerous questions concerning optimal patient care cannot be answered by RCTs alone. Prospectively collected, high-quality registry data address these gaps across two fundamental domains:

Firstly, registries describe how patients are treated in routine practice. They create transparency regarding treatment reality: which patients receive which therapies? Are all guideline-recommended diagnostic tests (like biomarker testing, imaging) carried out to enable optimal treatment selection? How rapidly are newly approved therapies incorporated into clinical practice? This real-world evidence (RWE) fills gaps that RCTs, by design, cannot inform.

Secondly, registries quantify treatment effectiveness outside trial settings. RCTs face three critical limitations: (i) restrictive eligibility criteria often systematically exclude patients with comorbidities, poor performance status, or advanced age, populations commonly encountered in routine practice, limiting external validity and potentially generalizability of trial results,1 (ii) RCTs with patients with cancer usually focus on one line of treatment and rarely compare all available treatment options head-to-head, leaving critical evidence gaps regarding optimal sequencing and choice among approved regimens, especially in the incurable, advanced-stage setting,2,3 (iii) The efficacy-effectiveness gap: absolute benefit observed in highly selected trial populations (efficacy) often differs from effectiveness observed in unselected real-world cohorts.

Addressing both domains requires cancer registries that balance breadth (large, representative populations of sufficient size followed longitudinally across multiple sites ensuring generalizability) with depth [granular medical data on patient characteristics including comorbidities, diagnostics such as biomarker tests and imaging, clinician- and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) as well as details on all types of treatments]. Additional multilayered data validation procedures, including electronic plausibility checks, regular queries, and audit trails, ensure high-quality real-world data (RWD) for research.4,5

In this study, we describe the iOMEDICO network of prospective, longitudinal oncology registry platforms in Germany, demonstrating how the breadth and depth of RWD enable advanced research. This registry infrastructure, spanning treatment documentation, comparative effectiveness research, prognostic modeling, translational research, PRO integration, and Health Technology Assessment (HTA) supporting data, complements RCT findings and addresses critical evidence gaps.

Materials and methods

The iOMEDICO network of oncologists and hematologists working in hospital and office-based practices in Germany established its prospective, longitudinal cancer registry platforms in 2006 to generate regulatory-grade RWD including PROs in representative samples of patients. All platforms are prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) RWD platform with detailed methodologies published previously.

Unique features of the platforms:• Clinician-led governance: medical oncologists and hematologists in all relevant tasks.

• Multicentric representation: data collection covering hospital- and office-based practices, representing the full spectrum of German health-care sectors.

• Prospective design: patients are enrolled at treatment initiation and followed longitudinally until death or the end of the specified follow-up.

• Data capture: a fully electronic, structured data capture system fulfilling Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and EMA regulatory standards with implemented real-time plausibility and completeness checks, audit trails, and mapping to international Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium standards with continuous updates to meet evolving data collection requirements. All sites are trained to ensure inter-site standardization of documentation.

• Quality assurance: continuous, sufficiently staffed centralized data management (monthly query schedules) and on-site data verification of all informed consents and key variables based on a data validation plan, minimizes inconsistencies, loss to follow-up (∼10%), and missing data (on average <10% per variable).

• Informed consent framework: patient consent enables the collection of clinician-reported decision-making factors, PROs, biomarker samples, and linkage with external databases (e.g. biobanks).

Beyond standard demographic and clinical variables, iOMEDICO registries systematically collect:• Comorbidity and risk factors: Charlson Comorbidity Index and cancer-specific risk factors enable replication of pivotal trial inclusion/exclusion criteria and control for potential confounders in causal analyses.

• Biomarker and cytogenetic data: comprehensive data on molecular or cytogenetic testing including test results.

• Treatment pathways: information on decision making, details on all types of treatments including start and stop dates, types of drugs or procedures used, modifications, and reasons thereof.

• Effectiveness and safety: outcomes per treatment line, such as best response, date(s) of progression, and date of death, and information on discontinuation of treatments or deaths due to toxicities.

• PROs: validated instruments chosen specifically for each cancer type capture health-related quality of life (HRQoL), time-to-deterioration, and symptom burden.

Research enabled by this RWD

Real-world patient characteristics and treatment patterns

The registry platforms systematically document the full spectrum of patients treated in routine clinical practice including treatment sequencing and effectiveness.6,7 For example, by retrospectively applying pivotal trial inclusion/exclusion criteria to registry cohorts, we quantify the proportion of real-world patients who would have been excluded from registration trials and compare their outcomes to potentially trial-eligible patients [e.g. for renal cell carcinoma (RCC), non-small-cell lung cancer,8,9 and hepatobiliary cancer (unpublished)]. Furthermore, these data and the fitting methodology also allow for advanced analytics like the impact of reduced starting doses of targeted therapies in breast cancer.10

These analyses demonstrate that ∼40%-46% of real-world patients would be excluded from pivotal trials across multiple tumor types, underscoring the importance of RWE to fill this gap and the necessity of RWE for understanding treatment effectiveness in unselected populations.

Prognostic models and risk stratification

Registries enable the development and validation of predictive and prognostic models such as risk stratification tools that reflect real-world patient populations. Using registry data with comprehensive baseline characteristics, treatment patterns, and long-term outcomes, we can build clinically relevant risk scores that support treatment decision making in routine practice (e.g. for colorectal or pancreatic cancer).11,12

Comparative effectiveness research and target trial emulation

When RCT data are unavailable for head-to-head comparisons between approved treatment options, registries can enable target trial emulation using causal inference methods such as inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) or propensity score matching. These methods allow rigorous comparative effectiveness research across one or multiple treatment lines while controlling for confounding. We used these methods for head-to-head comparisons of: first-line treatment strategies in RCC, of palbociclib versus ribociclib in breast cancer, and of treatment sequences in pancreatic cancer.13, 14, 15 The validity of these analyses rests on several registry-specific features: prospective enrollment at treatment initiation enables time-zero alignment and avoids immortal time bias; systematic collection of clinical confounders (including performance status, comorbidity, biomarker profiles, and prior treatment lines) supports the exchangeability assumption underlying IPTW and propensity score approaches.16

Translational research

Registries that are prospectively built like ours enable comprehensive translational research by storing leftover biomaterials for future analyses and then linking central laboratory testing results with detailed clinical data, treatment patterns, and outcomes. This approach allows systematic assessment of real-world prevalence of molecular markers, concordance between local and central testing, and the clinical impact of marker-guided treatment decisions in routine practice (e.g. as shown in our paper on gastric cancer17).

PROs and clinical data integration

Our registries systematically integrate longitudinal collection of PROs with comprehensive clinical and treatment data, enabling assessment of HRQoL, symptom burden, and other PROs over the course of treatment in routine care. As an example, linking PROs with clinical data has allowed us to investigate the association between HRQoL and disease progression, long-term toxicity of early breast cancer treatments, and HRQoL differences between pre- and postmenopausal patients with breast cancer.18, 19, 20

In addition, methodologies to address missing PRO data were explored: a machine learning-augmented inverse probability weighting approach was applied to investigate the extent of bias introduced by missing questionnaires.21 This methodological work was cited in the SISAQoL-IMI Konssortium, underscoring the relevance of registry-based research for advancing methods and standards for handling missing PRO data. Furthermore, these longitudinally collected PROs can be used to validate academic research that used simulated, synthetic data.

Registry data for HTA

In HTA, registry data play an increasingly important role in describing unmet patient needs and addressing evidence gaps. Registries strengthen HTA submissions by defining comparator therapies based on RWD rather than expert opinion, estimating target population sizes, and serving as external controls for single-arm trials.22,23 Beyond HTA, registry data support pharmaceutical drug development and trial planning: real-world distributions across disease stages, biomarker subgroups, and comorbidity profiles inform patient population selection, eligibility criteria, and recruitment estimates.

Moreover, causal machine learning methods, such as causal forests, can be applied to registry data (potentially combined with early-phase clinical trial data) to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects and identify patient subgroups that are most likely benefit from a given therapy. Such analyses may inform clinical trial enrichment strategies by guiding the selection of target populations for confirmatory phase III trials, thereby increasing the probability of demonstrating clinically meaningful treatment effects in pivotal studies.

Discussion

Our published work using the iOMEDICO registry platforms demonstrates that RWE generation requires both breadth and depth. Breadth ensures generalizability across heterogeneous patient populations, care settings, and geographic regions; depth enables sophisticated research methodologies including target trial emulation, PRO analysis, and translational research.

The examples presented illustrate the possibilities with prospective, longitudinal, high-quality registry data. These registries create transparency regarding patients’ treatment and discover areas for improvement, thereby serving quality assurance purposes. They demonstrate the importance of complementing RCT data with RWD to estimate absolute effectiveness in routine populations, whereas the relative treatment effects established in RCTs remain unchallenged. Moreover, besides informing HTAs, they enable critical research on head-to-head comparisons between approved regimens, treatment sequences across multiple lines, thereby addressing and filling important evidence gaps. These insights should continuously be integrated back into the registry infrastructure, ensuring that the platforms remain state of the art as oncology practice and analytical methods evolve.

Registry data are not only valuable for filling gaps left by RCTs but also for addressing limitations of RCTs themselves, especially issues of the control arm. RCTs are designed as static experiments in a dynamic clinical environment: treatment standards, supportive care, diagnostic precision, and patient management all evolve continuously. When trial recruitment or follow-up extends over several years, this creates temporal confounding that can distort results in ways randomization cannot prevent. Standard-of-care drift (when the control arm treatment becomes outdated during the recruitment or follow-up period) is a recognized challenge, further complicated by placebo-controlled designs, which HTA bodies may reject as insufficient evidence of added benefit over an active standard of care. An evaluation of RCTs leading to FDA approval of anticancer drugs found that 17% of approvals were based on trials with suboptimal control arms,24 and another study found 11.1% of phase III oncology trial control arms were suboptimal, either from the outset or because the standard of care changed during recruitment.25 However, drift extends beyond treatment choice: improvements in supportive care, imaging technology, and multidisciplinary management can improve outcomes in both arms over time, inflating or obscuring true treatment effects, particularly when results are compared with historical controls or early-phase results. Prospective, longitudinal registry data collected continuously across calendar time can document both phenomena and provide essential context for interpreting trial results. For rare tumor types or settings, in which early-phase efficacy data are so compelling that randomization to standard of care raises ethical concerns, registry-derived external control arms are increasingly accepted by regulatory agencies and HTA bodies as a rigorous alternative.22,26

Limitations

Residual confounding remains an inherent limitation of all observational analyses. Most of our registries exclusively enroll systemically treated patients; the few individuals receiving no anticancer therapy after diagnosis are not captured, limiting inferences about the total diagnosed population. Because data are collected in real-time, outcome data for recently approved therapies may remain immature until sufficient follow-up. Tumor response assessment follows routine clinical practice rather than strict RECIST criteria, which may introduce variability across sites and over time. Missing data (particularly for PROs) are not fully avoidable: questionnaire noncompletion may correlate with disease severity or treatment burden, potentially biasing estimates of HRQoL despite careful statistical handling. Despite comprehensive variable collection and the use of causal inference methods to address confounding by indication and immortal time bias, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (such as patient preferences, socioeconomic status, center-level treatment philosophies, or documentation practices) cannot be fully excluded in comparative effectiveness analyses. Therefore, registry findings will always complement but will not replace RCTs for definitive efficacy assessment.

Future directions

Current focus of further developing the registry platforms includes the integration of digital health technologies (wearables and electronic PRO applications), comprehensive genomic profiling, and artificial intelligence-based outcome prediction.

Conclusion

Oncology registries collecting both broad and deep RWD are essential complements to RCTs. By creating transparency about treatment reality and enabling advanced research methodologies, well-designed registries address critical evidence gaps and provide realistic outcome expectations for heterogeneous patient populations in routine care. As the oncology evidence ecosystem evolves, suitable RWE from prospective, longitudinal registries will play an increasingly important role in regulatory decisions, clinical guideline development, HTA, and personalized treatment selection.
