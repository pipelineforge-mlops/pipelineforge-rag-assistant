Dietary oils are energy sources for the growth and reproduction of poultry. Soybean oil (SO) is widely used but competes with the human food supply. In this study, black soldier fly maggot oil (BSFMO) was used as a sustainable alternative to SO for improving breeder hen productivity, chick quality, and immunity. A total of 360 39-week-old Black Yao Chicken 2A breeding hens with an average body weight of 2567 ± 66 g and an egg-laying rate of 70% were randomly allocated to three dietary treatments (n = 120 per group): 2% SO, 2% SO–BSFMO blend (SMO), and 2% BSFMO. After a 5-week feeding trial, 200 eggs per group were incubated, and 120 hatched chicks per group were randomly chosen and reared for 6 weeks. Compared with the SO group, BSFMO significantly increased laying rate, egg weight, egg size, yolk weight, yolk color, and feed conversion efficiency (P < 0.05). The concentrations of follicle‑stimulating hormone, estradiol, triglyceride, and high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol in plasma were increased, while low‑density lipoprotein cholesterol was decreased (P < 0.05). BSFMO also enhanced plasma immunoglobulin A, G, M; interleukin‑2; total antioxidant capacity; superoxide dismutase; and glutathione peroxidase and decreased malondialdehyde (P < 0.05). Chicks from the BSFMO group showed higher quality, growth performance, immune status, hepatic mRNA expression of insulin‑like growth factors (IGF1, IGF2), and epigenetic regulatory genes (DNMT1, DNMT3A) (P < 0.05). In conclusion, maternal feeding with BSFMO improved the productivity and immunity of breeder hens, chick quality, and representative epigenetic gene mRNA expressions of offspring. As an alternative lipid source to soybean oil in animal nutrition, BSFMO can be used in poultry farming for safe and sustainable poultry production and can reduce food competition.

Introduction

Nutrients are essential for animal organisms, as they support growth and enhance disease resistance. In poultry, energy-rich feed sources are essential for their growth and reproduction (Aye-Cho et al., 2024). Edible oils are frequently incorporated into poultry feed, which is an effective approach to optimally fulfill energy requirements with a small feed volume because lipids have high caloric values compared to carbohydrates and protein (Ravindran et al., 2016). Beyond energy supply, dietary oils also provide essential fatty acids, which improve lipid metabolism in chickens (Ehr et al., 2017; Skřivan et al., 2018), facilitate the transport of fat-soluble vitamins, and participate in the hormonal production process (Diarra, 2020; Yunbing and David Julian, 2021). The work of Al-Salhie et al. (2017) showed that pumpkin seed oil increased serum reproductive hormones in Japanese quail. Similarly, the studies of Tchoffo et al. (2017) reported that ginger rhizomes (Zingiber officinale) essential oil increases reproductive hormones such as estradiol (E2), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH) in the serum of Japanese quails. The fatty acids in a hen's diet influence the fatty acid composition of the egg yolk and, consequently, affect hatching and chick quality (Vilchez et al., 1990). In mammals, dietary fats promote the expression of epigenetic regulatory genes such as DNMT1 and DNMT3A (Peng et al., 2021; Korsmo et al., 2022).

The study reported that soybean oil has a high energy value compared to other vegetable oils (Fascina et al., 2009). Thus, soybean oil is generally used due to its availability and nutritional profile. Studies have shown that soybean oil in hens' diets increases egg production, nutritional value, and egg quality (Bragg et al., 1973; Dänicke et al., 2000; Dong et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2021). However, some studies reported that soybean oil in laying hens' diet increases serum malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and oxidative stress levels, and negatively affects their reproductive lifespan (Pérez-Bonilla et al., 2011; Gao et al., 2022). Furthermore, soybean oil is also commonly used in human nutrition, and this leads to food competition due to the rapid growth of the population. Research on alternative energy feed sources is already underway among poultry nutritionists.

Black soldier fly maggot oil (BSFMO) has been known as an oil with a fatty acid profile almost similar to soybean oils (Kim et al., 2022). Many studies have already shown that BSFM oil could replace soybean oil in the diet of broiler chickens (Achille et al., 2017; Schiavone et al., 2018; Cullere et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2020a; Chen et al., 2022) and laying hens (Heuel et al., 2021; Patterson et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2022; Khan et al., 2024). Our previous work showed that BSFMO is an energy source that can help chickens achieve successful hatching (Nadiedjoa et al., 2025). BSFMO is very rich in medium-chain fatty acids (Awad et al., 2020), which are the most efficient energy sources (Rubin et al., 2000; Dabbou et al., 2021). The energy from the medium-chain fatty acids of BSFMO will promote vitellogenesis in the liver, which will reduce the mobilization of body reserves and allow the hen to maintain a better body weight for laying big eggs and high weight. Additionally, lauric acid from BSFMO acts as an antimicrobial agent that improves digestive tract sanitation. A healthy intestine will lead to better absorption of essential nutrients such as calcium, zinc, and vitamins for egg laying. As a precursor of steroid hormone, lauric acid improves the level of estradiol for the regulation of the follicular hierarchy. Also, BSFMO contains a high total content of phenolic compounds and possesses antioxidant activity (Rattana and Supachet, 2024) and can alleviate oxidative stress. BSFMO could then be used as an energy source and an alternative to feed costs in poultry, for breeding hens nutrition to improve their reproductive function, chick quality, and the expression of epigenetic genes, compared to SO. However, few studies have examined the effect of maternal hen nutrition with black soldier fly larvae oil (BSFMO) on hen reproduction and epigenetics in the offspring (Nadiedjoa et al., 2026). Thus, we aimed to determine the impact of BSFMO on the productivity of breeder hens, as well as on chick quality, performance, and representative epigenetic gene expression in chickens.

Materiel and methods

Animal ethics

Following clearance from Shandong Agricultural University's Animal Care and Use Committee (No. 2001002), the study protocols were carried out in compliance with the Guidelines for Experimental Animals set by the Ministry of Science and Technology (Beijing, China).

Experiment 1

The experiment was conducted at Lihua Chicken Farm in Shandong, China. The study was performed on breeder hens (Black Yao Chicken 2A) at 39 weeks of age. In total, 360 breeder hens with an average body weight of 2567 ± 66 g and a laying rate of 70% were randomly divided into three experimental groups of 120 hens each, formed by 8 replications of 15 hens. The hens were housed in battery cages measuring 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm, with each cage accommodating two hens. The first group was fed a diet containing 2% soybean oil (SO); the second group was fed a diet containing 2% soybean oil and maggot oil (SMO); the third group was fed a diet containing 2% black soldier fly maggot oil (BSFMO). The diets were prepared weekly, stored in a dry place, and distributed by measurement once a day in the morning, ensuring adequate quantities of feed according to the production requirements of laying breeder hens. The hens were exposed to 16 hours of light and 8 hours of darkness per day and underwent artificial insemination. Drinking water for the hens was available at all times, and the experiment lasted five weeks.

In compliance with the nutritional criteria for chicken's GB/T 39235-2020 (Chinese national standard, 2020), the diets were formulated based on AOAC (AOAC Method, 2019) while maintaining a consistent energy-protein ratio. Data collection

Production performance parameters

Before the start of the experiment, the hens' initial weight was recorded, and their weight was measured weekly. Every day at 8:00 AM, 10:00 AM, and 2:00 PM, eggs were collected and counted in each treatment group. A high-precision digital scale with 0.1 g accuracy (XT 6200C, Precisa, Switzerland) was used to weigh the eggs. The following formulas 1-4 were used to determine the laying rate, egg weight, egg mass, and feed conversion ratio.(1) LayingRate=TotalnumberofeggsTotalnumberofhensX100

(2) AverageEggWeight=TotalweightofeggsTotalnumberofeggs

(3) EggMass=Layingrate(%)XAverageeggweight100

(4) FeedConversionRatio=FeedintakeEggmass

Egg quality parameters

48 eggs were chosen at random from each group during the fifth week of the experiment, and their quality was assessed. After measuring the longitudinal and transverse diameters at the base with a caliper, the shape index for egg size was calculated using formula 5 below. The eggshell strength tester (EFG-0503, Robotmation, Japan) was used to determine the eggshell breaking strength. The eggshell thickness tester (ETG-1061, Robotmation, Japan) was used to measure the thickness of the eggshells. A multifunctional automatic egg quality detector (EMT-5200, Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure albumen height, yolk color, and Haugh units. The egg yolk was removed and then weighed with a balance. Finally, the eggshells were cleaned, dried at 25°C for 24 hours, and weighed.(5) Eggshapeindix=Eggwidth(mm)EggLength(mm)X100

Biochemical, antioxidant activity, immunity, and reproductive hormone parameters in the plasma

Eight hens from each group were randomly chosen, with one hen per repetition at the end of the fifth week of the experiment. Blood samples were drawn from the wing vein and placed in anticoagulant tubes. After centrifuging for ten minutes at 3500 rpm, the plasma was extracted and kept for testing at −20°C.

Biochemical parameters like triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (THO), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total protein (TP), glucose (GLU), urea (Urea), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and albumin (ALB) were measured using the Hitachi l-7020 automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Oxidative stress indicators, such as total antioxidant capacity (TAC), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX), malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), were measured using a commercial kit from Nanjing Jiancheng Biological Engineering, China.

ELISA kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Biological Engineering, China, were used to measure plasma antibodies such as immunoglobulin (IgA, IgG, IgM), interleukin (IL-2, IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α). Similarly, ELISA kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Biological Engineering, China, were used to measure the concentrations of reproductive hormones in the plasma, including E2, FSH, and LH.

Experiment 2

The experiment was conducted at Shandong Agricultural University's Animal Science experimental farm in China. From each hen group, 200 eggs were randomly selected for incubation in 8 repetitions of 25, making a total of 600 eggs. The automatic-turning incubator was used to incubate the eggs. For eighteen days, the temperature and humidity were fixed at 37.8°C and 60%, respectively. Candling was performed on the 18th day of incubation, and infertile eggs were removed. The embryonated eggs were transferred to the hatchers, and the temperature and humidity were adjusted to 37.5°C and 70%, respectively. After hatching, the hatching parameters were recorded, and 24 chicks from each group were randomly selected for chick quality. 120 mixed-sex chicks and in equal numbers were also randomly selected from each group, with 8 repetitions of 15 chicks for rearing. The chicks were placed in cages measuring 95 cm × 75 cm × 60 cm, with each cage housing 15 chicks. The temperature was set at 32°C and gradually reduced to 23 ± 2°C with a humidity of 65 ± 3%. The lighting was provided 24 hours a day during the first week and then gradually reduced to 16 hours of light and 8 hours of darkness until the end of the experiment. The room and the rearing equipment (drinkers and feeders) were washed and disinfected before the experiment. From the first to the fifth day of rearing, the chicks received vitamins in their drinking water. On the fifth and twenty-first days, the chicks were given the Newcastle disease vaccine. Every morning, the drinking line were washed before giving fresh water to the chicks. During the starter and growth stages, hens were fed a commercial feed that was bought from Antimp Solar Feeds Co., Ltd. and Golden Rooster Hatching Co., Ltd. (Tai'an, China). Water and feed were provided ad libitum, and the experiment lasted six weeks.

Incubation data collection

Formulas 6-8 were used to determine the fertility rate, embryonic death rate, and hatchability rate following hatching. A centimeter-graded ruler was used to measure the chicks' lengths from the beak to the tips of their legs, and a high-precision digital scale with an accuracy of 0.1 g (XT 6200C, Precisa, Switzerland) was used to weigh the chicks. As per the protocol of Tona et al. (2003), the TONA score was applied as a qualitative assessment for the chicks.(6) FertilityRate=TotalnumberofeggscontainingembryosTotalnumberofeggsincubateX100

(7) EmbryonicmortalityRate=TotalnumberofdeadembryosTotalnumberoffertilityeggsX100

(8) HatchingRate=TotalnumberofchicksTotalnumberoffertilityeggsX100

Production performance and plasma immunity of chicks

Feed intake was measured by subtracting the amount of feed that was given each day from the amount that was not consumed. Weekly measurements of the chicks' weights made it possible to calculate their weight gain. The ratio of feed intake to weight growth was used to calculate feed conversion ratios. Eight chickens from each group were randomly chosen, one per repetition at three and six weeks of age, and blood samples were taken from the wing vein in anticoagulant tubes and centrifuged for ten minutes at 3500 rpm. After obtaining the plasma, antibody testing was conducted. ELISA kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Biological Engineering in China were used to measure plasma antibodies, including immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM), interleukins (IL-2, IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α).

RNA extraction and analysis by real-time quantitative PCR

At the end of the experiment, eight chickens from each group were randomly selected, one per replicate, and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The liver samples of the chickens from each group were collected and placed in liquid nitrogen. After samples were stored at −80°C for the identification of growth genes and epigenetic regulator genes expression. The procedure outlined by Wang et al. (2024) was followed for RNA extraction and quantification using quantitative real-time (QRT-PCR). Trizol (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA) was used to extract total RNA, and a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, Germany) was used to evaluate its quality. Commercial kits (Roche, Switzerland) were used to perform reverse transcription. cDNA was amplified using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX) (Roche, Switzerland). Based on available sequences, the primers for quantitative RT-PCR were designed using the NCBI Primer BLAST software. The real-time PCR reaction was carried out using the Q5 Real-Time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA). Using the comparative CT (2ΔΔCT) method, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was utilized as a reference gene to normalize and compute the differences found in the samples. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 (633) Software, San Diego, CA, USA. One-way ANOVA and the Tukey test were chosen for comparisons between the different groups. The analysis was done in a linear model whose equation is as follows: Yik=μ+αi+ ϵik. Yik denotes the observed value of the dependent variable; μ denotes the overall mean; αi denotes the effect of the ith level of maternal feeding (α1 = 2% BSFMO; α3 = 2% SMO; α2 = 2% SO); and εik denotes the random error. The results were presented as means with standard error (Mean ± SEM). A significant criterion was defined as P < 0.05.

Discussion

BSFMO enhanced the productivity, egg quality, immunity, and antioxidant capacity of breeder hens

In poultry production, performance parameters are of crucial importance for the profitability and sustainability of farming. In this study, hens’ production performance parameters of all groups were similar during the first two weeks of the experiment (weeks 40 and 41). Thus, that was the time of the feed adaptation. However, from the third to the fifth week of experiment (weeks 42, 43, and 44), hens fed with BSFMO laid eggs with higher weights compared to the eggs of hens fed with 2% SO. No significant difference was observed in body weight, laying rate, egg mass, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio. But, the cumulative results over the entire experimental period showed that hens fed with BSFMO had a higher laying rate compared to the groups of hens fed with soybean oil or a mixture oil (80.10% versus 76.91% and 75.49%). BSFMO contains more saturated fatty acids compared to SO. Saturated fatty acids are stable and provide more energy. Therefore, feed containing 2% BSFMO was able to supply the necessary energy to the hens, allowing them to meet their maintenance and production needs compared to hens fed with SO. Furthermore, compared to SO, BSFMO is very rich in medium-chain fatty acids (Ewald et al., 2020), which are the most efficient energy sources because they are easily oxidized (Rubin et al., 2000; Dabbou et al., 2021). This is why, in the present study, the cumulative feed conversion results of hens in the BSFMO group were better compared to the other groups. The results of the present study were corroborated by those of Anas et al. (2024), which also demonstrated that BSFMO enhances the laying rate and egg weight in laying hens. Soybean oil and crude palm oil are both plant-based, in contrast to BSFMO, which is of animal origin. However, the results of the present study were contradictory to the results of Patterson et al. (2021) and Kim et al. (2022), who found no significant differences between the treatment groups for production performance parameters when they replaced soybean oil with BSFMO in the diets of laying hens at 3% and 4.5%, respectively. This difference could be due to the inclusion level of oil, which was higher compared to the oil level in the present study; excessive dietary lipid increases metabolic stress, hinders intestinal nutrient utilization, and impairs hen performance beyond the optimal supplementation level. It could be hypothesized that 2% may represents a moderate effective inclusion level, while supplementation above 3% exceeds the optimal range and might weaken its beneficial impacts. Therefore, gradient dosage trials are necessary to systematically verify this dose-response relationship.

Nutrients are an essential factor for the production and quality of hen eggs. Edible oils often contribute to the improvement of the eggs' quality. In this study, no significant differences were observed regarding egg shape index, albumen height, haugh units, eggshell strength, eggshell thickness, or eggshell weight for each oil. The significant difference was observed only for egg size, yolk weight, and yolk color. Thus, the values of the average height and diameter of eggs from the BSFMO group were improved. Therefore, BSFM oil led to the production of large eggs with intensely colored yolks and increased yolk weight in breeder hens compared to SO. Changing the fatty acid content of the egg leads to the growth of the egg yolk and thus increases the height and diameter of the egg (Nys and Sauveur, 2004). BSFMO is thought to affect the fatty acid profile compared to SO. This ultimately suggests that the BSFMO contributed to increasing egg size by affecting the fatty acids in the egg yolks. The results of this study were similar to the work of Anas et al. (2024), who compared BSFMO and palm oil. The color of egg yolk is often influenced by the carotenoid pigments present in the feed ingredients (Kojima et al., 2022). Carotenoids are pigments found in many living organisms, including plants and some animals. Maggots, as insect larvae, can potentially contain them in variable concentrations, depending on the feed sources, such as colorful fruits and vegetables used for their nutrition. In the present study, BSFMO increased the egg yolk color of breeder hens compared to soybean oil, which could be attributed to their varying levels of carotenoid concentration. According to the work of Cruz et al. (2023), BSFMO has a carotenoid level ranging from 2.05 to 4.59 mg/kg. According to Benbouriche et al. (2024), the carotenoids in soybean oil are eliminated during refining, which explains why BSFMO improved the color of the yolk over SO. The results of the present study corroborate those of Patterson et al. (2021) and Kim et al. (2022), who also demonstrated that adding 1.5% to 4.5% BSFMO to the diet of hens improved egg yolk color compared to SO.

Biochemical parameters provide valuable information about the health and can help diagnose various diseases. They are therefore used to assess organ function and detect potential abnormalities. Indeed, transaminases (ALT, AST) are the main indicators used to assess liver function, followed by total proteins (TP) and albumin (ALB). In the present study, except for AST, the plasma levels of ALT, TP, and ALB were not statistically different between the study groups of hens. This suggests that all these oils had a common effect on liver function. Furthermore, no signs of morbidity were observed throughout the entire experimental period; this indicates that none of the oils were toxic to the chickens' livers. Kim et al. (2020a) also found similar results following the incorporation of BSFMO, corn oil, and coconut oil, each at 5%, into the diet of broiler chickens. Plasma glucose and urea allow the diagnosis of blood sugar levels and kidney function, respectively. The results of this study no longer showed a significant difference between the different groups for these parameters. This indicates that BSFMO can replace SO in the diet of breeding hens without harmful effects. The results of this study were corroborated by the findings of Schiavone et al. (2018) and Dabbou et al. (2021) in broiler chickens. BSFMO did not cause a change in total cholesterol, but it did lead to an increase in HDL cholesterol while decreasing LDL. Indeed, a high HDL level is a good indicator of health, whereas a high LDL level is a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases. Thus, the decrease in plasma LDL-cholesterol concentration in the groups of hens fed with BSFMO indicates that this oil reduces the risk of cardiovascular diseases in hens compared to SO. However, the increasing of triglyceride levels in the groups fed with BSFM oil indicates that this oil is the best energy source compared to soybean oil. When Aprianto et al. (2023) replaced palm oil with BSFMO in the diet of broiler chickens, they also did not find any significant difference between the groups in total cholesterol.

In this study, the levels of FSH and E2 were increased in hens that received a diet containing BSFMO. FSH, which is responsible for the development and maturation of follicles in the ovaries, would have an impact on egg size. This is why, in this study, hens fed with 2% of BSFM oil produced eggs of larger size and weight compared to the soybean oil group. The estradiol level also increases with BSFMO, which will promote ovarian maturation in hens and contribute to the proper functioning of the reproductive system. The LH levels of hens in all groups were statistically similar. LH triggers ovulation; this suggests that the follicular hierarchy was stable in all groups of hens. Thus, BSFMO improved the production of reproductive hormones compared to soybean oil without negative effects on follicular hierarchy. The results of this study corroborate those of Attivi et al. (2023), which showed that maggots of non-defatted black soldier flies increase reproductive hormones in laying hens. BSFMO is rich in medium-chain fatty acids, saturated fatty acids and bioactive lipid components, which can improve hepatic lipid metabolism, regulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, and thereby promote the synthesis and secretion of reproductive hormones. Certain lipid sources possess the inherent potential to modulate and enhance reproductive hormone secretion. In line with this view, Ipçak et al. (2024) showed that the inclusion of encapsulated fennel essential oil (Foeniculum vulgare Mill.) at a rate of 350 mg/kg in the diet of laying hens led to an increase in reproductive hormone concentrations such as FSH, LH, and E2. Similarly, Liu et al. (2021) demonstrated that supplementation with encapsulated Bacillus subtilis combined with essential oils improves reproductive performance in broiler breeders.

The BSFMO increased the levels of IgA, IgG, IgM, and IL-2 in breeder hens in this study. This would have positively impacted the lymphoid cells, which are responsible for producing these various antibodies essential for immune function in poultry. The work of Sypniewski et al. (2020) also showed that BSFMO improved plasma immunity in turkeys compared to SO. The current study is corroborated by the work of Chen et al. (2022), which showed that plasma levels of IgA, IL-2, and TNF-α increased with the substitution of soybean oil by BSFMO in broiler chickens. However, further studies will still be necessary to review the effect of BSFMO on the lymphoid cells of laying hens. Nevertheless, Schiavone et al. (2018) found no significant effect on the number of leukocytes and the heterophile/lymphocyte ratio when they substituted SO with BSFMO in broiler chickens.

In this study, BSFMO increased the levels of T-AOC, GSH-PX, and SOD, and then decreased MDA in the breeder hens compared to SO. This suggests that BSFMO may contain a substance capable of stabilizing free radicals by donating an electron or by acting through the activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), which can synthesize antioxidant enzymes. Thus, according to Rattana and Supachet (2024), BSFMO contains a high content of phenolic compounds, which are known for their antioxidant activities. Indeed, the results of this study corroborate those of Chen et al. (2022), who also showed that substituting SO with BSFMO improved the antioxidant status of broiler chickens. Kim et al. (2020b) also suggested that BSFMO could enhance the levels of total antioxidant capacity. However, it has been shown by numerous studies that soybean oil increases the serum MDA levels and oxidative stress in laying hens (Pérez-Bonilla et al., 2011; Gao et al., 2022). So, BSFMO is the best oil and is a superior oil that can address the oxidative stress problem in chickens compared to SO.

Maternal feeding with BSFMO improved the quality, production performance, and immunity of the offspring chicks

Vilchez et al. (1990) suggested that the composition of dietary fatty acids in hens influences egg yolk fatty acids, which affects hatchability and chick quality. In this study, the hatch rate did not differ between the study groups based on parental nutrition with BSFMO and SO. But the weights and lengths of the chicks were improved for the chicks in the BSFMO group. This was due to the improvement in the egg weights of the hens by BSFMO, as previously mentioned in this study. It has been shown that egg weight has a positive effect on the weight and length of chicks (Javid et al., 2017), which are indicators of chick quality. This was corroborated by the Tona Score data in the present study, and the chicks from parents fed with BSFMO had better scores and therefore were quality compared to those from the other groups. Furthermore, chicks from parents fed with BSFMO had the best growth performance compared to chicks from the other groups. The quality of chicks is an indicator of the future performance of chickens (Decuypere and Bruggeman, 2007; Willemsen et al., 2008). So, it is the best quality of chicks from the BSFMO group that explains their better growth performance.

Chickens' growth is mainly regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-growth axis, which consists of essential compounds including growth hormone, growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), and insulin-like growth factors IGF1 and IGF2 (Renaville et al., 2002). In this study, chicks from the hens fed with BSFMO had a higher level of IGF1 and IGF2 mRNA expression compared to chicks that were fed with SO. These results are in agreement with the chick growth performance results shown above. The work of Saxena et al. (2020) showed that a high-energy diet led to better growth performance in broiler chickens with an upregulation of the expression of IGF1, IGF2, IGFR, and GH genes. The best feed conversion shown in this study by chicks from parents fed with BSFMO would have promoted the production of energy, which stimulated IGF production in these chicks.

Maternal feeding with BSFMO increased the expression level of DNMT1 and DNMT3A in the liver of chicks in this study. The results of this study are consistent with several previous studies. The work of Peng et al. (2021) showed that maternal feeding with high fats led to global hypermethylation of the liver in the offspring. Similarly, it has been noted that maternal nutrition with fats leads to DNA hypermethylation in both the liver and brain of mouse offspring, resulting in an increased mRNA expression level of DNMT1 and DNMT3A (Korsmo et al., 2022). The increase in the expression level of DNMT1 and DNMT3A from BSFMO chicks should have been due to its saturated fatty acid content. Saturated fatty acids are part of the composition of cell membranes, activate NF-kB, and thus influence the regulation of these epigenetic markers. DNMT1 and DNMT3A are representative epigenetic genes involved in embryonic development and cell differentiation. In this sense, the growth performance of chicks from hens fed with BSFMO was better compared to that of other chicks. Naturally, the effects on epigenetics need further verification by detecting other genes or corresponding protein levels.

This study showed that BSFMO in the diet of breeder hens improves the plasma immunity of their chicks. In poultry, IgG can be deposited in the yolk and absorbed into the chick's circulatory system. IgA is deposited in the albumen, which the chick ingests during its formation, acting locally in the respiratory and intestinal tracts. This suggests that BSFMO chicks received an antibody transfer from their parents. Since the immunity of parents fed with BSFMO was stronger compared to the other groups, it would make sense that the immunity of their offspring would also be improved. Further studies will even be necessary to better understand all the mechanisms, especially at the gene level.

Conclusion

Black soldier fly maggot oil is a sustainable alternative to soybean oil for breeder hens. Rich in medium-chain fatty acids and lauric acid, BSFMO improves laying rate, egg quality and feed conversion, enhances reproductive hormone secretion, lipid metabolism, immunity and antioxidant capacity in hens. Maternal BSFMO feeding also boosts chick quality, growth and early immunity, accompanied by upregulated hepatic expression of IGF1/IGF2 and representative epigenetic genes (DNMT1, DNMT3A). Collectively, BSFMO enhances breeder productivity and offspring performance likely via epigenetic regulation, offering a safe and eco-friendly strategy to reduce food-feed competition and promote sustainable poultry production. Further research is needed to assess the impact of BSFMO on the protein level of epigenetic genes for more clarification.
