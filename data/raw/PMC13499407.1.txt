Background

Personal protective equipment (PPE) restricts heat dissipation from the body, and many healthcare workers experience heat-stress-related physical discomfort, including dizziness, headaches, fatigue, and excessive sweating, during clinical duties. Such thermal strain may compromise not only an individual’s well-being but also work performance and patient safety. Despite these concerns, evidence from routine clinical practice, particularly for PPE commonly used in nursing care, remains limited.

Objective

This study aimed to investigate the effects of wearing PPE during simulated nursing care on physiological and psychological responses and to compare gowns and aprons.

Methods

This study included 11 young women in simulated nursing care wearing PPE in three conditions: control, aprons, and gowns. The ear canal temperature, skin temperature, sweat rate, and subjective responses were evaluated.

Results

Changes in ear canal temperature did not differ significantly among PPE conditions. PPE type and time interactions to changes in forearm skin temperature were observed (F(26,260) = 19.2, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.66). The forearm skin temperature during nursing care was significantly higher in the gown condition than in the control and apron conditions in multiple comparisons. The sweat rate during simulated nursing care was significantly higher in the gown condition than in the control condition (p < 0.01, r = 0.91). The gown condition was associated with higher thermal sensations (p < 0.05, r = 0.87), more statistically significant thermal discomfort (p < 0.05, r = 0.87), and a stronger sweat sensation (p < 0.05, r = 0.87) than the control condition.

Conclusions

The simulated nursing care performed while wearing PPE for 20 min in this study did not produce enough heat load to affect the core temperature. However, the thermal sensation and discomfort subjectively increased with the heightened sweat rate in the gown condition. The study findings reinforce the need for evidence-based guidelines aimed at protecting nurses from heat stress associated with the use of protective clothing. Furthermore, this study provides quantitative evidence indicating that PPE type influences the magnitude of these responses, underscoring the necessity of considering PPE type when developing practical recommendations.

1 Background

The health and well-being of healthcare workers are essential for maintaining safe and high-quality healthcare delivery and for sustaining healthcare systems. During infectious disease outbreaks, healthcare workers must continue working despite increased occupational risks. Therefore, improving their working environment and protecting their physical well-being have become an urgent global priority.

Healthcare workers must wear personal protective equipment (PPE) such as gloves, gowns, masks, goggles, and face shields to prevent infectious diseases. During the COVID-19 pandemic, they had increased opportunities to wear PPE while performing medical procedures. Yang et al. (2024) report that nurses and physicians wore PPE on an average of 4.0 days per week during the pandemic, with an average of 6.2 h of PPE worn per shift.

Although PPE is indispensable for infection control, its use imposes considerable physiological burdens on healthcare workers. Previous reports indicated the appearance of various physical disorders due to the wearing of PPE in the medical field. Headaches, fatigue, profuse sweating, and dizziness were reported in a high proportion of subjective symptoms when wearing PPE to work during the COVID-19 pandemic, and more than half of the cases reported heat and discomfort (Unoki et al., 2020; Davey et al., 2021; Messeri et al., 2021; Hunt et al., 2023). Further, Ong et al. (2020) revealed that 81.3% of healthcare workers who wore N95 masks and goggles to work during the pandemic experienced headaches for <60 min of wear, and the use of N95 masks and goggles for >4 h per day was associated with headache occurrence. These findings suggest that PPE use increases heat stress and may compromise the physical well-being of healthcare workers.

Despite these risks, internationally standardized guidelines for managing heat stress during PPE use in healthcare settings are lacking. Protecting healthcare workers from heat strain is a critical responsibility of healthcare management and is essential for preparing for future emerging infectious diseases. Establishing evidence-based guidelines, therefore, requires foundational scientific data.

Previous experimental studies have investigated thermoregulation and thermal perception during PPE use. For example, Coca et al. (2017) compared the physiological response of 60 min of walking on a treadmill at 3 METs with PPE conditions, including face shields and gowns as well as goggles and full-body protective clothing (coveralls) The group wearing PPE (coveralls) had significantly higher rectal temperature and more significant sweating than those wearing a gown.

Chaudhari et al. (2023) investigated the physiological and psychological responses to 2 h of walking on a treadmill wearing PPE (coveralls). They revealed that the stomach temperature after 2 h of walking on the treadmill was significantly higher in the group wearing PPE than in the group not wearing PPE. These studies indicate that PPE increases core body temperature and induces heat strain, and that the magnitude of the effect varies by PPE type.

However, important gaps remain. Most previous studies were conducted using treadmills and bicycle ergometers (Hostler et al., 2009; Morley et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2013; Coca et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2023; Chaudhari et al., 2023; Wibowo et al., 2024). It is not a simulation of activities, such as care and treatment performed by healthcare workers and could entail increased physical activity levels. Further, the PPE worn is frequently a coverall-type protective suit, and it is not necessarily PPE that is frequently worn in medical settings. The room temperature was over 32 °C (Coca et al., 2017) or 35 °C (Kiiza et al., 2021), slightly higher than in general medical settings in developed countries, including Japan. Consequently, foundational data specific to real-world healthcare practice are insufficient for developing guidelines for PPE use that are tailored to clinical settings.

This study constitutes the first stage of a research program aimed at establishing evidence to protect the well-being of healthcare workers during PPE use, with a focus on comparing two PPE types—gowns and aprons—that are commonly used in clinical settings. Nurses account for 59% of healthcare workers (World Health Organization [WHO], 2020); hence, they are most frequently required to wear PPE. Ensuring nurses’ well-being is therefore directly linked to healthcare system resilience. Accordingly, the present study focused on nursing activities. This study aimed to determine the physiological and psychological responses to the use of different PPE types (gowns vs. aprons) during nursing care simulations. Our study findings may provide fundamental data to guide the development of PPE-wearing guidelines in medical settings.

2 Methods

2.1 Study design

This study employed an open-label randomized crossover design. The trial was performed following the recommendations of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT 2025). Each participant underwent experiments under three PPE conditions, such as apron (wearing masks, gloves, and aprons), gown (wearing masks, gloves, and gowns), and control conditions (wearing masks and gloves), on different days and at the same time of day. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the six possible sequences of the three clothing conditions to balance potential order effects using a random number table. A minimum 1-day washout period was implemented between PPE conditions to reduce potential carryover effects.

2.2 Participants

The participants included nursing female college students. The eligibility criteria were the absence of underlying medical conditions. All participants were females because nursing professionals are predominantly female. The exclusion criteria were those who were pregnant or may be pregnant. To determine the sample size, we referred to a previous crossover study investigating the physiological effects of wearing PPE (Zand et al., 2024), which reported a moderate effect size for skin temperature (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.40, corresponding to Cohen’s f of approximately 0.20). Assuming α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, a repeated-measures ANOVA would require 10–12 participants.

The study participants were instructed to refrain from drinking alcohol and performing strenuous exercise the day before and on the day of the experiment, to finish eating at least 2 h before the start of the experiment, to refrain from consuming caffeine on the day of the experiment, and to sleep for at least 6 h on the night before the experiment.

Three experiments were conducted within the same menstrual cycle, either during the follicular or luteal phases, excluding the menstrual phase. The menstrual cycle was determined using participants’ self-reported information. Participants were monitored for heat-related symptoms, including dizziness, headache, nausea, or discomfort, during all experimental sessions.

2.3 Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted in the artificial climate chamber from mid-June to mid-July 2024. The air temperature and relative humidity were controlled at 26 °C and 50%, respectively. Participants wore similar tank top underwear, ankle-length socks, nursing uniform, and nurse shoes. In this study, the aprons and gowns were made of polyester, the masks were made of polyester and non-woven fabric, and the gloves were made of vinyl (Supplementary File 1).

Fig. 1 presents the experimental protocol. The participants changed into nursing uniforms and were attached to various sensors with surgical tape after arriving at the laboratory. The activity meter was attached to the waist of the nursing uniform. After 30 min of rest by sitting in the climate chamber, they wore PPE and provided simulated nursing care for 20 min. After the care, the participants took off their PPE and rested in a sitting position for another 30 min. The control conditions were performed using the same procedure as the others, except for wearing and undressing PPE.Fig. 1 Participant flow diagram.

Fig 1 dummy alt text

Participants performed simulated routine nursing care in the following order: diaper change (4 min), bed sheet changing (7 min), bed bathing and clothing changes (7 min), and bedside environment cleaning (2 min). To ensure consistent physical workload across conditions, they conducted nursing care while following a video-guided protocol. Further, to ensure smooth task performance, participants watched the video and practiced on a different day prior to the experiment. An activity monitor (Active style Pro HJA-750C, OMRON Corp., Japan) was used to measure the activity intensity. The average value of activity intensity for 20 min during simulated nursing care was used as a representative value for the analysis.

Blinding of participants, intervention providers, and outcome assessors was not feasible because the clothing conditions were visibly different. However, objective physiological measurements were used, and all experimental procedures and environmental conditions were standardized to minimize measurement bias. Whenever possible, data analysis was conducted using coded datasets without condition labels.

2.4 Outcome measures

2.4.1 Primary outcome

An earplug-type sensor (LT-2N-13, Gram Corporation, Japan) was used to measure ear canal temperature. The skin temperature was monitored at seven skin surfaces (forehead, abdomen, forearm, back of the hand, thigh, lower leg, and back of the foot) with a thermistor (ITPO10-12, NIKKISO-YSI CO., Ltd., Japan). Both ear canal and skin temperatures were monitored over time from rest to 30 min after the nursing care simulation, and the data was recorded in a data logger (LT-8A, Gram Corporation, Japan) every 10 s. The equation of Hardy-DuBois was used to calculate the mean skin temperature (Hardy et al., 1938). The ear canal and skin temperature analysis at each measurement site in an average of 5-min intervals was used as a representative value.

Amount of sweating was measured on the forehead using a wearable device throughout the experiment (SKW-1000, SKINOS Co., Ltd, Japan), and the data was recorded every 10 s. The average value of every 5 min was used as a representative value for the analysis.

2.4.2 Secondary outcomes

Subjective responses, including thermal, discomfort, and sweating sensations, were assessed. The thermal sensation was rated on a seven-point scale: “very cold,” “cold,” “slightly cool,” “neutral,” “slightly warm,” “hot,” and “very hot.” Thermal discomfort was rated on a four-point scale: “comfortable,” “slightly uncomfortable,” “uncomfortable,” and “very uncomfortable.” The sweating sensation was assessed on a four-point scale: “no sweating,” “slight sweating,” “sweating,” and “excessive sweating.” Subjective responses were assessed 15 and 20 min after the start of rest, immediately after nursing care (before PPE undressing), after PPE undressing, and 15 min and 30 min after nursing care.

2.5 Statistical analysis

The ear canal and skin temperature changes were reviewed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. A multiple comparison test was performed using Bonferroni correction when interactions were observed. Effect sizes for ANOVA were calculated using partial eta squared (partial η²). As the sweating rate and subjective response data were not normally distributed, Friedman’s test was used to compare the three PPE conditions. The effect size for Friedman’s test was expressed as Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W). When significant differences were discovered, post hoc comparisons were performed employing the Bonferroni correction, and effect sizes were calculated as r (|Z|/√N). Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software (ver. 29).

2.6 Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the Declaration of Hlsinki. The purpose and content of the experiment were fully explained to the research participants in writing and orally, and consent to participate in the experiment was obtained in writing. The author’s institution approved this study (Approval number: 20232018). This study was registered at the UMIN as an intervention study (UMIN000054105).

3 Results

3.1 Participant flow

Participant recruitment was completed prior to starting the experiments. Twelve individuals applied, and one declined participation before randomization. All 11 participants completed all three experimental periods, with no participants lost to follow-up or excluded after randomization (Fig. 2). All outcome measurements were conducted during the laboratory sessions with no additional follow-up period. No serious adverse events occurred during the trial. All interventions were delivered as planned, and no deviations from the protocol occurred.Fig. 2 Experiment procedure.

3.2 Basic attributes of the participants

The participants’ mean (standard deviation [SD]) age was 23.2 (2.1) yr, with an average height of 160.0 (2.7) cm, weight of 51.3 (5.6) kg, body mass index of 20.0 (2.3), muscle mass of 35.8 (2.1) kg, and body fat percentage of 25.1% (6.2%).

3.3 The artificial climate chamber temperature, relative humidity, and activity intensity

The average temperature (SD) of chamber temperature was 26.3 (0.2) °C for all three conditions, and the relative humidity was 47.9(1.8) %, 47.5 (1.3)%, and 47.6 (1.4) % for the control, apron, and gown conditions, respectively. The mean activity intensity (SD) during simulated nursing care was 2.5 (0.4) METs for all controls, aprons, and gowns. The three conditions demonstrated no significant difference in terms of temperature, humidity, or activity intensity.

4 Discussion

4.1 Physiological responses to simulated nursing care while wearing PPE

The ear canal temperature remained between 36.0 and 36.3 °C during the experiment. No significant interaction was found between time and PPE type in ear canal temperature. These results indicate that the implementation of 20 min of simulated nursing care while wearing PPE in this study did not greatly affect the heat strain on the whole body. Previous studies (Jiang et al., 2023; Chaudhari et al., 2023) have revealed that activities while wearing PPE significantly increase tympanic and intragastric temperature. These previous studies performed exercise on treadmills or bicycle ergometers, and the activity level and duration were more significant than in the present study, which may be one of the reasons for the difference in results. Moreover, the PPE used during the last study was coverall-type protective clothing. Meanwhile, the PPE utilized in the present study was an apron and gown made of polyethylene, which is frequently used in clinical settings in Japan. Compared to the previous study, the smaller area covered and the lower thermal resistance value of the material may have contributed to the difference in results.

The skin temperature of the forehead, abdomen, and thighs decreased aftercare, whereas the temperature of the forearms, back of the hand, lower leg, and back of the feet increased. Except for the forehead, the skin temperature decreased in the trunk or near it. Further, the skin temperature increased in the peripheral part of the limbs. The trunk, such as the chest and back, generally sweats more than the peripheral areas, including the upper arms and forearms (Smith and Havenith, 2011). Sweating occurs due to activity in the abdomen and thighs, and the evaporation of sweat decreases the skin temperature by the heat of vaporization. Conversely, the peripheral parts of the back of the hands and feet have a huge ratio of surface area to volume. They are suitable for precise control of heat dissipation due to their high efficiency of heat dissipation (Cotter and Taylor, 2005). Nursing care activity may increase the blood flow of the peripheral areas skin; thus, the skin temperature increases. The decrease in skin temperature on the forehead may be caused by the heat of vaporization due to sweat evaporation, as in the abdomen and thighs, because the forehead is one of the places where sweating occurs most as a thermoregulatory response (Smith and Havenith, 2011). The forearm skin temperature was more significantly increased in the gown condition than in the other two conditions, especially during care. Although the forearm represents a relatively small proportion of the total body surface area, changes in the forearm skin temperature also affect overall body temperature. A significant interaction between PPE type and time was observed in mean skin temperature. The participant covered their arms with a polyethylene-sleeved gown and wore gloves over the gown sleeves in the case of gown conditions. Therefore, the ventilation in the forearm was blocked, and the heat dissipation was thought to be hindered, thereby increasing skin temperature. The gown and apron used in this study were made of the same material (polyethylene) from the same manufacturer; thus, the gown having a larger clothing area on the body than the apron was associated with the difference in the change in skin temperature in the forearm.

The amount of sweating in the forehead was significantly higher in the gown condition than in the control condition, and there was no significant difference between the apron condition and the control. Kiiza et al. (2021) compared physiological responses when performing simulated medical procedures (average duration: 69 min, estimated activity level: 3.0 METs), including peripheral venous catheterization, central venous catheterization, and endotracheal intubation, whereas wearing PPE (coveralls) in a hot environment with a room temperature of 35 °C and a controlled environment with a room temperature of 20 °C. This previous study revealed that the sweat rate was significantly greater in the heat condition than in the control condition. Compared with the study by Kiiza et al. (2021), the room temperature conditions in our study were lower, and the gowns worn were less thermally permeable. However, the present study also showed the same prior results, with the gown condition having higher sweating than the other conditions.

In this study, the skin temperature findings and sweat rate indicate that the degree of heat strain experienced by nurses differs depending on whether PPE is worn and on the PPE type (apron or gown), even during the same nursing care activity. In contrast, no significant changes in ear canal temperature were observed, suggesting that the 20-min simulated nursing care performed under study conditions did not impose a thermal load sufficient to affect core body temperature.

These findings suggest that the polyethylene aprons and gowns examined may be tolerated without substantial heat strain during relatively short nursing care activities conducted in an environment with an ambient temperature of approximately 25 °C. However, this study evaluated only a 20 min PPE-wearing condition and could not determine the effects of prolonged PPE use. Therefore, further studies should investigate the physiological effects of extended PPE use under conditions that more closely reflect actual clinical practice. Such evidence will be important for establishing recommendations regarding safe PPE-wearing durations and appropriate rest intervals for healthcare workers and for minimizing PPE-associated heat strain.

4.2 Subjective responses to simulated nursing care while wearing PPE

Immediately after simulated nursing care and after PPE undressing, significant differences in thermal sensation and thermal discomfort were found between the three conditions. The gown condition was significantly higher than control conditions, indicating that the participants were aware of the hot and thermal discomfort. Furthermore, one of the factors that most significantly affects the thermal sensation of the whole body is the thermal sensation and skin temperature of the back and upper arms (Maruyama and Tamura, 1989). The forearm of the participant was covered with the sleeve of the gown, which inhibited heat dissipation and increased the skin temperature, in the gown conditions. This increase in forearm skin temperature may have increased the perception of heat in the forearm, resulting in a greater perception of heat throughout the body. Gagge et al. (1967). demonstrate that heat-induced discomfort is associated with the degree of hyperthermic sweating. In this experiment, the sweat rate on the forehead for 20 min of simulated nursing care was significantly higher under the gown condition than under the control and apron conditions. These results indicate the increase in the sweat rate during simulated nursing care as a factor in the increase in thermal discomfort in gown conditions.

Chaudhari et al. (2023) demonstrated that the PPE-wearing group experienced significantly greater thermal sensation and discomfort than the non-wearing group. Many studies evaluating the psychological effects of PPE wearing, including this study, assessed exercise-induced heat strain using equipment such as treadmills. The activity intensity by simulated nursing care set in our study was 2.5 METs, which was lower than that of the previous research, and the time was shorter at 20 min. Still, the results of thermal sensation and discomfort were comparable with those of the last study. The findings suggested that nurses perform their daily nursing duties while being aware of the heat and thermal discomfort when wearing gowns to care for patients with infectious diseases. Although further research is needed on physiological and psychological responses to PPE under diverse conditions, this study provides evidence supporting the need to develop strategies to reduce heat strain from PPE use during nurses’ daily tasks and to establish PPE-wearing guidelines.

4.3 Limitations

This study had several limitations. The results were obtained from a female-only sample in a laboratory setting, and caution is necessary when applying them broadly to clinical situations. This study used an earplug-type sensor to estimate core body temperature. Still, it may have not estimated eardrum temperature as accurately as the infrared sensor utilized in previous studies. The local sweat rate was only recorded at the forehead in this study, which limited the ability to assess heat strain on the entire body. Further studies may be warranted to measure and estimate the participants’ body weight as well as the weight of their clothing before and after care to assess total body fluid loss. Additionally, blinding was not feasible due to the visible differences in clothing, which may have influenced subjective responses. It was believed that by more closely simulating clinical settings, it was feasible to assess the actual heat strain experienced by nurses. However, it should be acknowledged that the types of PPE, content, and duration of care differ significantly in real-world settings.

In light of these considerations, several issues require further investigation. First, this study did not assess cognitive load or task performance; thus, future studies should examine how PPE-related heat strain affects cognitive function and work performance in real-world clinical settings. In addition, factors such as the material properties of gowns and aprons, as well as differences in mask fit and sealing characteristics, should be considered when evaluating heat strain associated with PPE use. Investigating these factors may provide valuable foundational evidence for the development of evidence-based guidelines regarding PPE use in healthcare settings.

5 Conclusions

The following results summarize the physiological and psychological responses of wearing PPE during simulated nursing care, highlighting the differences between the PPE types.1. Changes in ear canal temperature during nursing care were not significantly different between PPE types. However, forearm skin temperature was significantly higher in the gown condition than in the control condition. The same result was obtained for the mean skin temperature. Further, the sweat rate during nursing care was higher in the gown condition than in the control condition.

2. The thermal sensation and discomfort during nursing care were significantly greater in the gown condition than in the control condition. In the present study, the 20-min simulated nursing care with PPE use did not produce enough heat load to affect the core temperature. However, in the gown condition, the thermal sensation and discomfort subjectively increased with a higher sweat rate.

Our study findings reinforce the need for evidence-based guidelines aimed at protecting nurses from heat stress associated with the use of protective clothing. Furthermore, this study provides quantitative evidence indicating that the type of PPE (aprons and gown) influences the magnitude of these responses, underscoring the necessity of considering the PPE type when developing practical recommendations. Although wearing PPE for 20 min under the thermal conditions examined increased sweating and subjective thermal responses, no significant change in ear canal temperature was observed. These findings suggest that PPE use for 20 min under the conditions examined did not impose excessive heat strain. Nevertheless, further studies are required to evaluate the effects of prolonged PPE use and establish evidence-based recommendations regarding safe PPE-wearing durations and appropriate rest periods.
