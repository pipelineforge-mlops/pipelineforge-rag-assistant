Background & objective

The challenge of applying knowledge of Medical Device Related Pressure Injuries (MDRPIs) to clinical practice persists worldwide. This study examined the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) of nurses dealing with MDRPIs in two disparate organizational ecosystems, India and Europe. This was done to better understand the systemic factors that contribute to gaps in nursing competency as a result of disparate organizational ecosystems.

Methods

The study used a 20-point validated KAP scale with a sample of 334 registered nurses located in Europe and in India. Gaps within the KAP scale were analyzed through domain-specific disparities with demographic variables as covariates.

Results

Nurses in Europe scored substantially higher on the KAP scale (17.61 ± 2.14) compared to the Indian nurses (10.94 ± 2.85; p < 0.005). The largest gap was in Clinical Practice (2.64 ± 1.44 vs. 5.30 ± 1.03; p < 0.005). Subgroup analyses showed that the performance gaps were unrelated to the demographic variables in question. Interestingly, the Indian cohort had a higher percentage of postgraduate nurses, thus indicating that higher levels of education do not necessarily correlate to higher levels of Clinical Practice.

Conclusion

The management of MDRPIs is a product of the nursing environment as determined by the organizational structures of the health system, rather than the capability of the individual nurse. Closing the gap in competency requires movement away from higher levels of Academic Qualifications, and a concentration on the nursing Clinical Environment through the development and standardization of institutional clinical protocols.

Introduction

Medical device-related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) are a growing issue of serious concern in the modern medical practice sector that negatively impacts patient outcomes because it increases the length of stay in the hospital and increases treatment costs. These injuries reduce.

the safety of patients and prolong their hospitalisation, increase morbidity, and cost significant amounts of money [1]. The MDRPIs occur due to sustained mechanical shear and pressure, especially by medical devices, endotracheal tubes, nasal cannulas, catheters, cervical collars, etc., This causes local tissue damage. In contrast to classical pressure ulcers, which typically involve the development of bony protrusions due to immobilisation, MDRPIs replicate the device shape [2, 3]. They can occur in other parts of the body, such as the nose, lips, ears, and mucous membranes. The MDRPI incidence has also been on the rise alongside growing dependence on medical equipment, particularly in the intensive care setting, so there is a systemic need to prevent and control MDRPI in the nursing profession [4].

The trend of MDRPIs has also increased along with the development of medical technology and the intricacy of patient care. Patients having critical illnesses often need to be equipped with multiple devices and kept in this condition, which often increases the risk of such injuries [5, 6]. Among the contributory factors are immobility, decreased tissue perfusion, the deterioration of sensory perception, and moisture, which contribute to the MDRPI development. Such injuries put the well-being and life of the patients at risk and are linked to an extended period of hospitalisation, high cost of healthcare, and high chances of contraction and morbidity. In appreciating these implications, the prevention of MDRPIs has been preferred as a key goal in practice across the world.

Evidence-based prevention of MDRPI is offered by contemporary guidelines issued by the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP), European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (EPUAP), and Pan Pacific Pressure Injury Alliance (PPPIA). Such guidelines include periodic skin testing, repositioning of the devices, and the use of protective dressings. The implementation of these standards is, however, successful on the basis of nursing expertise, clinical judgment, institutional support, and allocation of sufficient resources. MDRPIs are a specific type of pressure injury that is caused by medical equipment that can be used either to diagnose or as a source of treatment. These injuries are usually at the point where contact is achieved between the device and the skin or mucosa, as per the shape or contour of the device. In the NPIAP, MDRPIs are classified as pressure injuries due to the application of devices that are specifically designed and used for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes, whereby the tissue damage is according to the pattern of the device [7].

The result of these injuries is tissue ischemia, inflammation, and ultimately necrosis due to the sustained mechanical force, which subjects the soft tissues to bony prominences and breaks the microcirculation. MDRPIs are therefore mostly harder to prevent or detect early, since medical machines cannot be taken off or modified regularly, particularly in the case of critical care. As a continuation of TURK-eye, a series of studies in Europe has offered an extensive study of the epidemiology as well as preventive aspects of MDRPIs. In the case of adults in the ICU, a rate of 32.5% prevalence had been reported with a prospective cohort study of 172 patients in the ICU, showing an MDRPI incidence of 48.8% [5, 8–10]. Injuries of the mucosa were also common, and a prolonged hospital stay proved to be one of the risk factors. In this context, nurses are of prime importance in the prevention and early detection of MDRPIs.

MDRPIs are still a big problem in both Indian and European healthcare settings, especially in intensive care units (ICUs) where patients depend on a lot of different invasive and non-invasive devices. A cross-sectional observational study with 160 ICU patients in a tertiary care hospital found that 10.6% of them had MDRPI [11]. Point prevalence research among 146 ICU patients revealed a higher prevalence rate of 19.2%, with oxygen masks and endotracheal tubes found as the main causes of pressure injuries [8].

Early risk identification is one of the most important nursing interventions. Regular skin inspections in the areas and under medical equipment are crucial in the early detection of the early stages of pressure injuries [12]. Prevention-based nursing interventions comprise routine device repositioning, application of pressure-relieving surfaces, and proper device sizing and positioning [5, 13]. Although MDRPIs have been recognised all over the world as a preventable condition, there remain severe gaps in terms of knowledge, research, and practice of preventing and acting on the problem. Research demonstrates that there are no uniform procedures, inconsistencies in the use of clinical guidelines, and training of nurses in different health systems [14]. Although European healthcare systems usually have quite developed guidelines and organised nurse-education programs, the differences in MDRPI prevention are still evident because of dissimilarity in healthcare policies, institutional concerns, and resource distribution. Conversely, India is facing other problems, including large patient-to-nurse ratios, poor wound-care education, and the unavailability of state-of-the-art wound-management equipment. Furthermore, the literature that compares nursing practice in relation to MDRPI prevention in the context of both high-income countries and resource-intensive healthcare lacks sufficient scholarly findings. The proposed research attempts to fill these gaps through a comparative examination of the nursing practice in the prevention and management of MDRPI in the European and Indian settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional survey, comparative design was used to assess differences between medical device-related pressure injury (MDRPI) prevention competencies following Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (supplementary file 1). This study was carried out simultaneously in the tertiary care centres. The India cohort was collected from the facilities in Kattankulathur, Tamil Nadu, and the European cohort was sampled specifically from the tertiary centre in Dublin, Ireland. To facilitate readability, this latter group is, for the purpose of this manuscript, called the European cohort in general.

Participants and sampling

A multi-stage sampling method was used, and 334 registered nurses (with 167 registered nurses per cohort) participated. To identify clinical areas with a high use of medical devices, a purposive sampling was first conducted, targeting the general intensive care units, the surgical wards, and the medical specialty units. These units were then sampled by stratified random sampling for proportional representation of institutional type and clinical specialty. The inclusion criteria were: (i) Age 20–50 years, (ii) Current clinical duties related to medical device application or management, and (iii) employment within the specified regions. Exclusion criteria: (i) nurses involved in strictly administrative or non-clinical work, and (ii) questionnaires for which responses were incomplete were eliminated before analysis, providing a final sample of 334.

Variables and measurement

The main finding was the ‘Knowledge, Attitude and Practice (KAP) score about MDRPI prevention. Domain-specific scores for Knowledge, Clinical Practice and Awareness were additional secondary outcomes. Data on potential confounders (age, gender, and educational qualification) were obtained. The information was gathered with a 20-item instrument that was created based upon the 2019 NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA clinical practice guidelines. There was good internal consistency (α = 0.76) across the measured domains of the instrument. The Awareness domain was created to measure institutional practices and not ethical inclinations.

Scoring algorithm

There was a dichotomous scoring for the instrument, with 1 point to be awarded for evidence-based responses and 0 points for incorrect or suboptimal responses. The maximum possible scores were 8 for Knowledge, 6 for Clinical Practice, and 6 for Awareness, and a total KAP score of a maximum of 20. This was defined a priori as: 17–20 (Excellent knowledge and practice), 13–16 (Good knowledge, minor gaps), 9–12 (Moderate knowledge and practice), and < 9 (Poor knowledge and practice). Using available literature, a clinically significant difference was defined as a difference ≥ 2.0 on the composite KAP score. The detailed scoring of each question is explained in Supplementary File 2.

Bias mitigation

The recruitment procedures and electronic survey administration were uniform for both groups to reduce the potential for selection and information biases. To minimize response variation due to item interpretation, a uniform set of instructions was given before the assessment.

Study size

The statistical power was computed using an a priori method to apply the Fleiss formulation for comparing two independent proportions. A baseline of 163 participants in each cohort (total N = 326) was determined as a minimum requirement with a two-sided alpha (α) of 0.05, 80% statistical power, and knowledge of an adequate MDRPI of 0.55 (India) and 0.70 (Europe) aimed at detecting a 15% effect size. The minimum required statistical power thresholds were fully met, as the ultimate recruitment effort resulted in 167 individuals per cohort (N = 334).

Statistical methods

The SPSS 27 software was used to analyze the data. The data were analysed using complete case analysis since the questionnaires that were not completed were not considered for the initial screening process. Since the data were not parametric, comparisons between the groups were made using Mann-Whitney U tests, and the rank-biserial correlation (r) was reported as an effect size. Multivariable analysis was performed to eliminate the influence of other factors such as age, gender, and educational qualification on KAP scores, and isolate the geographic area effect. A p-value < 0.05 was considered the level of statistical significance.

Ethics and data protection

Both the study was ethics approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, SRM Institute of Science and Technology, India (2628/IEC/2025) and Griffith College Research Ethics Committee, Ireland (2025/0001132). All participant informed consented electronically. In the European cohort, the collection and transfer of data occurred in accordance with the standard cross-border data protection rules, using secure digital systems.

Results

Baseline demographic characteristics

The total number of registered nurses included in this study was 334, and India and Europe had equal numbers of 167 registered nurses each. There were no significant differences between the two regional cohorts with respect to age distribution (p = 0.354) or gender (p = 0.170) as shown in Table 1. Nurses aged 31–40 years were the largest age group in both cohorts, and most of the participants from both cohorts were female. A significant difference in education levels was found as part of the baseline analysis. A surprising finding was the large number of post graduate (PG) qualified nurses in the Indian cohort (55.1%, n = 92) than in the European cohort (47.3%, n = 79; p = 0.002).

Primary outcomes and domain disparities

Although the Indian group had a higher percentage of advanced academic degrees, Medical Device-Related Pressure Injury (MDRPI) management skills were significantly lower among them. Comparing the two regional health care systems showed that there are significant macro-level differences. Table 2 shows that the Total KAP scores in the European cohort were significantly higher than the Indian cohort (17.61 ± 2.14 vs. 10.94 ± 2.85). Figure 1 provides a graphical comparison of the mean scores across the four outcome domains. This absolute difference resulted in a large effect size (Rank-Biserial r = 0.693, p = 0.004) and a median difference of 6.50 points. On an analysis of each domain individually, the Knowledge score of the European nurse study group was significantly higher than that of the Indian nurse study group (6.81 ± 1.13 vs. 5.27 ± 1.51; r = 0.552, p = 0.022). The highest discrepancy occurred in the Clinical Practice domain. The mean practice score for European nurses was 5.30 ± 1.03, while for the Indian nurses, it was 2.64 ± 1.44. This lack in clinical execution gave the highest r (ES = + 0.724, p = 0.005) and median difference (+ 2.60 points). The same effect was seen in the Awareness and Perception Domain, with the European cohort having significantly higher scores (5.49 ± 0.93) compared to the Indian cohort (3.02 ± 1.47; r = 0.681, p = 0.012).

Subgroup and within-country analyses

Given the possibility of confounding by individual demographic factors, notably the preponderance of education-related competency gaps (pre-existing imbalance), extensive subgroup (Table 3) and within-country analyses (Table 4a and 4b) were conducted. A heatmap indicating the size of the difference in means across demographic subgroups (Europe-India) was created to help visualize the subgroup comparisons (Fig. 2). Notably, there was a very weak and non-significant relationship between individual characteristics and competency in either of the regional ecosystems. European nurses who received only an undergraduate (UG) degree did significantly better than Indian nurses with a Postgraduate (PG) degree in Total Kaplan (17.48 ± 2.16 vs. 11.08 ± 2.75; p < 0.005). Additionally, there was no variation according to age (p = 0.428), gender (p = 0.590), or educational qualification (p = 0.228) in the Indian group. The youngest group of Indian nurses (20–30 years: 10.70 ± 2.95) exhibited performance essentially identical to the “oldest and most senior” group of nurses (41–50 years: 10.95 ± 2.90). The same pattern of micro-level homogeneity occurred in the European cohort, as there were no statistical differences for age (p = 0.392), gender (p = 0.969), or education (p = 0.672). This validates that there are macro-level environmental difference factors that account for the general regional differences, in addition to the individual factors of the nurses themselves.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study shows that there is a wide gap between the nurse cohorts in Europe and India with respect to knowledge, clinical practice, and awareness about management of MDRPI. The data clearly reveal that in all the domains assessed, European nurses have higher scores, thereby generating large effect sizes. Importantly, there was no evidence of any of these differences being explained by any one demographic factor such as age, gender, or level of education when we performed rigorous analyses within the various subgroups and within the different countries. Rather, the overall divergence indicates that MDRPI competency is a more environmental type of phenotype; that is, a competency largely shaped by factors at systems levels rather than just by individual ability.

Influence of educational qualifications and clinical tenure

One of the most significant conclusions of this investigation was that there was a lack of correlation between academic achievement and clinical proficiency. The Indian cohort had significantly higher scores for Knowledge and Practice in the negative direction, while they had significantly higher scores for post-graduation level. This is consistent with previous empirical studies which indicated that there are often non-specific, measurable units of wound management in the general curriculum level of advanced nursing programs [15, 16]. Although advanced academic achievement is a necessary but insufficient foundation for specialized MDRPI knowledge, greater academic achievement does not mean that the knowledge has been focused upon through explicit integration [17–19].

Also, scores for all age groups have remained unchanged over time, suggesting that clinical understanding of MDRPI prevention does not automatically improve as time is spent at the bedside. The knowledge needs to be actively taught, as device-related pressure injuries are a complex phenomenon, depending on mechanical forces specific to the modern medical equipment [20, 21]. It is noteworthy that older nurses who had learned from others in India were not different from younger nurses, but did not perform as well as newly graduated nurses from Europe, indicating that passive experiential learning had failed and the need for ongoing, formal professional learning [19, 22].

Systemic barriers to knowledge translation

There is a large gap in knowledge between these cohorts, and this gap is much greater in the domain of Clinical Practice. Acquiring theoretical knowledge of MDRPI pathophysiology is not enough unless there is a clinical environment that is conducive to taking preventive measures [23]. The extremely low levels of practical execution among Indian nurses could be attributed to the established constraints against health workers in a resource-limited setting. The standards for international guidelines include regular change in position and the application of Several prophylactic dressing protocols; but proper implementation of these requires adequate nurse-to-patient ratios, easy access to specially formulated barrier products, and manageable patient loads. The inflated practice gap may reflect a clinical reality in which nurses are becoming increasingly frustrated because of the lack of clinical resources.

Organizational determinants of safety culture

Lastly, in the Awareness and Perception domain, there will be different hypotheses as to institutional safety cultures. High scores across Europe could be due to policies developed and implemented that are well embedded across hospital governance and severe pressure injuries being classified as reportable incidents. This institutional accountability that starts from the top increases clinical vigilance. However, Indian lower awareness scores probably could be attributed to the lack of institutional measures implemented for tissue viability, routine clinical audits, and explicit attention to tissue viability by leaders. Awareness is of course decreasing when there is no awareness on the macro level, that is, when there is no policy prioritization [18, 20, 22].

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of this study is that it employs inter- and intra-group analyses to control for baseline demographics and for macro-systemic effects. The cross-sectional, self-report study design makes causal inferences impossible, however. Most importantly, no investigator-specific covariates are available for these analyses (e.g., specific years in clinical practice, exact departmental assignment, previous training in wound care), and therefore fully adjusted analyses of these micro-environmental factors are not possible. In addition, hypotheses around institutional resources and specific curricula were inferential since these were not quantified objectively. Further large longitudinal studies that include direct observation of the clinical state are needed.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional comparative study is clear-cut and clearly shows a huge gap between the medical device related pressure injury prevention competency among Indian nurses and Europe. A significant European superiority prevailed in all domains (knowledge and clinical practice and institutional awareness) and resulted in a relevant performance difference. Importantly, there was no overlap across these huge differences when analyses were conducted within demographic subgroups (including male and female age, and across general levels of education) suggesting that all of these differences were independent of individual demographic factors. The surprising educational dichotomy, in which the Indian group includes a much larger number of postgraduates and a much smaller number of competent students, and this discrepancy is particularly pronounced in the competencies assessed, suggests that the move from general academic learning to experiential learning relies more on chance than on skill.

Rather, the best MDRPI management is revealed as a product of the system and an environmental phenotype, shaped by a strong institutional safety culture and effective and consistent delivery of support materials for prophylaxis. The large difference between theory and practice within the resource-restricted cohort highlights the need for theoretical knowledge of the pathophysiology of events that is not yet supported by an enabling clinical architectural framework.

The health care systems need a fundamental transformation to adequately control preventable device-related harm. This means clearly integrating targeted, measurable MDRPI modules into straight-through education and creating mandatory reporting structures on the patient safety level from above, as well as easy access to specialized barrier products. In the end, it requires refocusing from the “blame game” and moving to an environment that is structured in a way that supports and enables nursing professionals to act according to international guidelines proactively in order to minimize the incidence of these preventable injuries.
