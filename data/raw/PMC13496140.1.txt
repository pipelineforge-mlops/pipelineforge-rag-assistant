Purpose

Discrepancies in adherence classification across assessment tools pose a challenge in clinical practice. This study compared three medication adherence assessment tools—the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), the Single-Item Self-Adherence Assessment (SISA), and the Omani-validated Medication Adherence Universal Questionnaire (MAUQ-ar-OM)—with respect to their agreement, their association with glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and their identification of behavioral and belief-related barriers in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).

Patients and Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the endocrinology clinic of a tertiary-care academic hospital in Oman, among Arabic-speaking Omani adults (≥18 years) with T2DM who were receiving oral hypoglycemic agents, either alone or in combination with insulin. Adherence was classified into three levels (high, moderate, and low). In a predefined subgroup of 98 participants, the MAUQ-ar-OM was used to identify adherence barriers across four domains: positive attitudes toward healthcare and medication, lack of discipline, aversion toward medication, and active coping with health problems.

Results

A total of 233 patients were included, of whom 50.7% were female. The median age was 58 years and the median HbA1c was 8.8%. Adherence classification varied markedly across tools: high adherence was reported in 88.8% of patients by the SISA versus 46.6% by the MMAS-8. Agreement between MMAS-8 and SISA was slight-to-fair beyond chance (quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ = 0.248; p<0.01). Lower MMAS-8 scores were associated with higher HbA1c values (p<0.01). Within the MAUQ-ar-OM, the lack-of-discipline domain emerged as a barrier most closely aligned with adherence behavior (p=0.237, p=0.019).

Conclusion

In this cross -sectional study, MMAS-8 showed a stronger association with HbA1c and may help identify patients with poorer glycemic control, whereas SISA offered a rapid assessment but appeared to classify a higher proportion of patients as highly adherent. The MAUQ-ar-OM identified lack of discipline as a potentially modifiable adherence barrier. Because this was a cross-sectional study using self-reported measures, prospective studies incorporating objective adherence measures are needed before concluding that any single tool is superior. A combined approach that integrates a brief screening tool with a comprehensive assessment may warrant further evaluation to optimize diabetes care.

Introduction

Medication adherence, defined as the extent to which a patient’s medication-taking behavior corresponds with the prescribed regimen, is an essential determinant of optimal clinical outcomes across chronic conditions.1–3 Non-adherence refers to a patient’s failure to follow prescribed medical or treatment regimens, and may involve forgetting to take medications, omitting doses, or neglecting recommended lifestyle changes. It can be intentional or unintentional and represents a major barrier to the management of chronic diseases and the attainment of optimal health outcomes.1 Non-adherence is frequently associated with adverse clinical consequences, including increased hospitalization rates, disease complications, and higher mortality.1–3

Adherence is conventionally defined as taking at least 80% of prescribed doses; however, this threshold may vary according to the specific disease and medication.4 The diversity of methods and tools used to measure adherence, and the variability in their acceptable cut-offs, makes it difficult to compare findings across studies.5,6

Psychosocial factors strongly influence medication non-adherence in chronic disease. Key predictors include psychological distress, substance use, negative attitudes toward medication, limited social support, and socioeconomic factors such as lower income, lower educational attainment, and the absence of an established dietary routine.7–10 For example, disease-related distress, including diabetes-related distress, has been shown to negatively affect adherence.7,9,11 Adherence is also closely linked to mental health, quality of life, and disease-related perceptions, including depression and patients’ beliefs about their illness.12 Poor perceived health and mental well-being are frequently associated with higher rates of non-adherence and, consequently, treatment failure.7,11 Additional factors—such as a poor patient–physician relationship, inadequate health literacy, and limited social support—have likewise been associated with poor adherence, particularly in chronic conditions such as diabetes.13

Diabetes is a chronic condition that requires lifelong management and patient education to prevent complications.14 Globally, the disease affects a large and growing population,15 and in Oman it represents a considerable public health challenge. In this context, evaluating and managing medication non-adherence is essential for effective disease control. Substantial evidence supports the association between good adherence and improved glycemic control, a reduced risk of complications, and lower mortality.16

Despite its clinical importance, reliably assessing adherence remains a significant challenge in both practice and research, as different tools often yield divergent results.17 Adherence has been evaluated using a range of methods, from comprehensive questionnaires to simple single-item assessments.18,19 Among the most widely used instruments are the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), the Single-Item Self-Adherence Assessment (SISA), and the more recent Medication Adherence Universal Questionnaire (MAUQ).18–20 These three instruments were selected because they represent complementary approaches to adherence assessment: MMAS-8 is a widely used multidimensional self-report scale that categorizes adherence and explores common medication-taking behaviors; SISA is an ultra-brief pragmatic screening question suitable for routine clinical workflows; and MAUQ-ar-OM is a locally validated Arabic instrument designed to capture behavioral and belief-related adherence domains in Omani patients. Although each instrument has been evaluated separately, limited evidence directly compares their agreement and their associations with clinical markers such as HbA1c in Arabic-speaking T2DM populations. This gap is clinically important because discordant adherence classification may affect which patients are identified for counseling or adherence-support interventions.18–21

Studies have highlighted the lack of consensus on the most appropriate method for measuring adherence, with different tools frequently producing variable results. This underscores the need for standardized, validated, and contextually appropriate methods to ensure reliable assessment and comparability across studies and populations.22 Limited data exists regarding the level of agreement between these tools and their associations with clinical outcomes such as glycemic control, despite their widespread use—particularly in Arabic-speaking populations. Given the central role of adherence in the management of T2DM and its potential impact on outcomes such as HbA1c, understanding the strengths, limitations, and interrelationships of these instruments is essential. We hypothesized that the three tools would not be interchangeable, that the multidimensional MMAS-8 would show a stronger association with HbA1c than the single-item SISA, and that MAUQ-ar-OM domains would provide complementary behavioral information rather than simply reproduce MMAS-8 or SISA adherence categories. Therefore, this study aimed to (i) evaluate the level of agreement between three adherence assessment tools (the MMAS-8, the SISA, and the Omani-validated MAUQ-ar-OM); (ii) explore the relationship between these tools and HbA1c in patients with T2DM; and (iii) identify behavioral and belief-related barriers to adherence assessed by the MAUQ-ar-OM, categorized according to the adherence levels defined by the MMAS-8 and the SISA.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the endocrinology clinic of Sultan Qaboos University Hospital (SQUH) between October 1, 2024, and January 31, 2025. SQUH is a tertiary-care academic teaching hospital with an estimated bed capacity of 500 that offers a wide range of outpatient services across medical specialties, from pediatrics to geriatrics. The endocrinology clinic provides dedicated diabetes-care services twice weekly. Most patients are scheduled for follow-up every three months; the clinic also accommodates prescription refills for stable patients or those unable to attend in person, allowing medication renewal without a full consultation. In addition, the hospital supports diabetes management through a specialized Diabetes Education Clinic, led by a diabetes specialist nurse, where patients receive individualized counseling based on their specific health needs.

Study Population

Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM who were receiving oral hypoglycemic agents (OHAs), either alone or in combination with insulin. Only Arabic-speaking Omani patients capable of providing informed consent were included. Patients with severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric conditions that could hinder their ability to provide informed consent or to complete the questionnaire accurately were excluded.

Consent and Instruments Administration

Informed consent was obtained from all participants through an in-person interview during clinic visits. The MMAS-8 and MAUQ-ar-OM were administered face-to-face by trained study personnel using standardized wording. The SISA was administered by telephone because it is as a brief single-item screening assessment and was feasible for patients who could not complete all tools during the clinic encounter. The timing between face-to-face and telephone assessments was within one month. HbA1c was abstracted from the most recent clinically available laboratory result nearest to the adherence assessment, within a three-month interval.

Data Collection and Adherence Assessment Tools

The following data were collected: patient demographics (age and sex); comorbidities (eg, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease); baseline microvascular complications (diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy); and diabetes history (duration of T2DM, most recent HbA1c, number of OHAs, and use of insulin). The three adherence assessment tools are described below.

MMAS-8

MMAS-8, a validated self-reported tool designed to evaluate medication-taking behavior, comprises seven yes/no items and one 5-point Likert-scale item that address common barriers to adherence, such as forgetfulness, side effects, and the perceived inconvenience of daily medication use. Each item was scored according to the official MMAS-8 coding instructions: items 1–7 were coded as “No = 1” and “Yes = 0”, with item 5 reverse-coded; item 8 (Likert scale) was standardized by dividing its score by 4 to yield a unified scoring range across the scale.20,21

The total MMAS-8 score ranges from 0 to 8 and categorizes adherence into three levels: High adherence: MMAS-8 score = 8.

Moderate adherence: MMAS-8 score ≥6 and <8.

Low adherence: MMAS-8 score <6.

Permission to use the scale and its coding was obtained through a licensing agreement (MMAR, LLC; https://www.moriskyscale.com; © 2007 Donald E. Morisky).

SISA

In SISA, participants were asked by telephone: “In the past month, how often did you take your medications as prescribed?”18 Responses were categorized into three levels: High adherence: “I always take my medications as prescribed”.

Moderate adherence: “I partially take my medications as prescribed”.

Low adherence: “I am not adhering to taking my medications as prescribed”.

Any response indicating less-than-fully adherent behavior (eg, taking medications approximately 75% of the time or less) was flagged as non-adherence. This single-item tool provides a simple categorical indicator of adherence (high, moderate, or low) without the need for a lengthy questionnaire.

MAUQ-ar-OM

MAUQ is a validated, open-access, self-reported instrument19 comprising 16 items with a maximum total score of 112 points, grouped into four domains: Positive Attitudes toward Healthcare and Medication (PAM), maximum 28 points.

Lack of Discipline (LD), maximum 28 points.

Aversion Toward Medication (ATM), maximum 28 points.

Active Coping with Health Problems (ACHP), maximum 28 points.

Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), with reverse coding applied to negatively worded items. The MAUQ-ar-OM, the Arabic version validated in Oman, was validated in a recent study at the same institution.23 However, the present analysis differs from that validation study in objective, focusing on the comparative assessment of MMAS-8, SISA, and MAUQ-ar-OM adherence classifications and their relationship with HbA1c rather than on translation or psychometric validation.

Further agreement and sensitivity analyses were performed to identify cut-off points for categorizing adherence into three levels (high, moderate, and low) based on associations with relevant clinical outcomes.

Sample Size

The sample size was aligned with the study objectives. A non-probability convenience sampling technique was used, based on the availability of patients during the study period. For the correlation analyses between adherence scores and HbA1c, the main sample of 233 participants provided approximately 80% power at a two-sided α=0.05 (Fisher z transformation) to detect correlations of about ρ=0.18 or larger, whereas the predefined MAUQ-ar-OM subgroup of 98 participants provided approximately 80% power to detect correlations of about ρ=0.28 or larger; subgroup analyses were therefore considered exploratory and potentially underpowered for weak associations. For the agreement analysis, the sample was considered sufficient to estimate chance-corrected agreement between the MMAS-8 and the SISA with clinically interpretable precision, although the skewed distribution of SISA categories was recognized as a limitation affecting κ estimates.24

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as the mean and standard deviation (for normally distributed data) or the median and interquartile range (IQR; for non-normally distributed data). Associations or differences between adherence levels and continuous outcomes were examined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. For categorical outcomes, the Pearson χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test was used, depending on the data characteristics. To account for potential confounding, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed with HbA1c as the dependent variable. Separate models were constructed for the MMAS-8 and the SISA after adjustment for age, duration of diabetes, body mass index, insulin use, insulin dose, and total number of medications. Weighted Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic was used to evaluate the agreement between adherence assessment methods, distinguishing observed agreement from that expected by chance.25 Percent of observed agreement and κ value were reported with their 95% confidence interval and the corresponding p-values were interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The interpretation of κ followed standard categories: poor (κ<0.01), slight (κ=0.01–0.20), fair (κ=0.21–0.40), moderate (κ=0.41–0.60), substantial (κ=0.61–0.80), and almost perfect (κ=0.81–1.00).26 Because the adherence categories were ordinal and were assessed in the same participants, weighted κ is the preferred agreement statistic. Sensitivity analysis was performed to identify cut-off points for categorizing adherence into three levels (high, moderate, and low) that best aligned with clinical outcomes. Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used to assess associations between continuous adherence scores (the MAUQ total and domain scores) and the MMAS-8 total adherence score or HbA1c. All analyses were performed using STATA version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Adherence by MMAS-8

When adherence was assessed using the MMAS-8 (Table 1), most patients were classified as highly adherent (n=104, 46.6%) or moderately adherent (n=92, 41.3%), while a smaller proportion (n=27, 12.1%) were classified as having low adherence. Older age was associated with low adherence (median 60 vs 55 vs 59 years; p=0.035). Higher HbA1c values were also associated with lower adherence categories (p<0.01), indicating that better adherence was accompanied by better glycemic control. Further, multivariable linear regression analysis showed that adherence remained independently associated with higher HbA1c (β = 0.745, 95% CI: 0.194–1.296; p<0.01).

Adherence by the SISA

Stratification of adherence by the SISA is presented in Table 2. The majority of patients were classified as highly adherent (n=198, 88.8%), while only 16 (7.2%) and 9 (4.0%) were classified as moderately and poorly adherent, respectively. The only variable significantly associated with adherence category was the duration of diabetes; patients with a duration of 11–15 years were more likely to exhibit low adherence (55.6% vs 18.8% vs 14.7%; p<0.01). However, this was not apparent when performing multivariable linear regression, suggesting that the SISA did not independently predict glycemic control.

Agreement Between the MMAS-8 and the SISA

The agreement between the MMAS-8 and the SISA is presented in Table 3. The overall observed agreement between the two instruments was 84.4%. However, the quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ demonstrated only slight-to-fair agreement beyond chance (κ = 0.248; 95% CI: 0.138–0.358; p<0.01). Discussion

This cross-sectional study of adults with T2DM provides a comparative evaluation of three validated medication adherence assessment tools—the MMAS-8, the SISA, and the MAUQ-ar-OM, and explores their agreement and association with HbA1c as a primary clinical outcome. The findings reveal substantial discrepancies in adherence classification across these tools, with slight-to-fair agreement between the MMAS-8 and the SISA. Notably, Lower MMAS-8 scores were associated with higher HbA1c, suggesting that MMAS-8 scores were more closely aligned with glycemic control than SISA classifications in this sample. However, because this was a cross-sectional study using self-reported adherence measures and no objective adherence gold standard, these findings should not be interpreted as proving the superiority or predictive validity of the MMAS-8. The MAUQ-ar-OM findings, particularly the weak but statistically significant correlation between the LD domain and MMAS-8 score, suggest a potential behavioral dimension for future study rather than definitive evidence that discipline-focused interventions would improve outcomes. While the SISA offers a rapid, easy-to-administer assessment suited to busy clinical settings, its simpler structure may overestimate adherence relative to more comprehensive tools.

Our findings demonstrate that adherence estimates vary substantially with the assessment tool used. The SISA classified a higher proportion of patients as highly adherent than the MMAS-8, a discrepancy consistent with prior studies reporting higher adherence rates with single-item measures or visual analogue scales relative to multi-item scales such as the MMAS-8.17–19 This pattern is attributable to inherent differences in assessment approach: whereas the MMAS-8 and the MAUQ-ar-OM probe specific barriers and beliefs—such as forgetfulness, intentional dose-skipping, and negative attitudes toward medication—single-item tools such as the SISA rely on a general self-assessment that may fail to capture these dimensions of adherence behavior.17,27,28 Furthermore, social desirability bias and patients’ tendency to over-report adherence in brief assessments may contribute to this overestimation.

The overall observed agreement between the MMAS-8 and the SISA was 84.4%; however, the quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ indicated only slight-to-fair agreement beyond chance (κ = 0.248; 95% CI: 0.138–0.358; p<0.01). This apparent discrepancy reflects the well-recognized kappa paradox, whereby high observed agreement may coexist with a low κ coefficient when one response category predominates, resulting in markedly imbalanced marginal distributions.26 In the present study, most participants were classified as having high adherence by both instruments, increasing the agreement expected by chance and consequently reducing the κ value. This finding aligns with previous research showing that different adherence measures often yield inconsistent classifications of patient behavior, particularly among patients with low adherence.17–19 Such inconsistencies may lead to misinterpretation of true adherence behavior, with implications for clinical decision-making and targeted intervention. Single-item tools such as the SISA should therefore not be considered interchangeable with more comprehensive, structured instruments, especially where accuracy is critical, as in clinical audits and research. Accordingly, the choice of adherence measure should be guided by the clinical setting, the patient population, time constraints, and the specific information required.29

With respect to clinical outcomes, lower MMAS-8 scores were associated with higher HbA1c, reinforcing the documented relationship between poor adherence and suboptimal glycemic control.20,21 Studies across diverse populations have shown that lower MMAS-8 scores predict worse glycemic control, even after adjustment for demographic and clinical variables.30–32 By contrast, this association was less apparent with the SISA, suggesting that the MMAS-8 captures a broader range of behavioral and belief-related dimensions and therefore offers greater clinical relevance, whereas the SISA provides a simpler, quicker assessment that may lack sensitivity and tend to overestimate adherence.

The subgroup analysis using the MAUQ-ar-OM revealed meaningful behavioral trends, although no statistically significant differences were detected across the adherence categories defined by the MMAS-8 or the SISA. Specifically, the LD domain showed a trend toward differentiating adherence levels and a significant correlation with the total adherence score, suggesting that interventions targeting self-discipline may be particularly beneficial in this population.19 This observation is consistent with literature emphasizing self-regulation, habit formation, and behavioral consistency as important determinants of long-term medication adherence in chronic diseases such as T2DM.33,34 Behavioral factors—such as forgetfulness, poor time management, and competing priorities—frequently contribute to non-adherence, often unintentionally. Structured interventions, including reminder systems and habit-building strategies, have demonstrated potential to improve adherence.35,36 In addition, belief-based domains captured by the MAUQ—such as attitudes toward healthcare, perceived medication burden, and aversion toward medication—are recognized as influential determinants of adherence behavior.19,37 These findings support the need for multifaceted, behaviorally informed interventions that address not only knowledge and access but also psychological readiness, which is often shaped by cultural and familial expectations. Such approaches may be beneficial for improving clinical outcomes in patients with T2DM.

Future work should prioritize prospective, longitudinal studies to evaluate how different adherence tools predict long-term clinical outcomes, including reductions in HbA1c, the development of complications, hospitalizations, and mortality. There is also a need to develop and validate standardized cut-off points for the MAUQ to classify adherence as high, moderate, or low, thereby enhancing comparability across studies. Qualitative studies, including patient interviews and focus groups, are essential to explore patients’ perspectives on adherence barriers and facilitators. Integrating a validated, practical screening tool with a comprehensive instrument such as the MAUQ in clinical practice may balance efficiency with behavioral insight. Embedding adherence assessment within electronic health record systems could enable routine, automated monitoring and facilitate the identification of at-risk patients, and machine-learning approaches could further enhance personalized care by linking adherence data with clinical risk-prediction models.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of multiple validated tools, a well-characterized cohort from a tertiary-care center, and the integration of both behavior- and belief-based assessments. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporality, causality, or predictive validity for future glycemic outcomes. Second, adherence was assessed only using self-reported instruments; no objective adherence measure, such as pharmacy refill records, pill counts, electronic medication monitoring, or biochemical confirmation, was available for validation. Third, convenience sampling at a single tertiary-care academic center may have introduced selection bias and may limit generalizability to the broader population of patients with T2DM in Oman, including those managed in primary care or non-tertiary settings. Fourth, different administration modes were used: MMAS-8 and MAUQ-ar-OM were administered face to face, whereas SISA was administered by telephone. This may have introduced differential recall, interviewer, or social-desirability bias and may have affected comparability between instruments. Fifth, although several clinical covariates were collected, residual confounding remains possible even after adjusted analyses. Sixth, the MAUQ-ar-OM subgroup was relatively small (n=98), and analyses of weak correlations or subgroup differences should be considered exploratory. Finally, although the MAUQ-ar-OM was validated locally, it remains a relatively new adaptation requiring additional psychometric and external validation across diverse Omani and Arabic-speaking populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights substantial discrepancies in adherence classification across three across three self-reported adherence assessment tools in Omani adult patients with T2DM. The clinical relevance of the MMAS-8 showed a stronger cross-sectional association with HbA1c than the SISA, suggesting potential utility for identifying patients who may require further adherence assessment; prospective confirmation is needed before this can be regarded as established. By contrast, the SISA appeared to overestimate adherence and showed no association with HbA1c in this sample. The behavioral barriers identified by the MAUQ-ar-OM, particularly lack of discipline, merit further investigation as a possible target for behaviorally informed adherence support. Prospective, adequately powered, and ideally multicenter studies incorporating objective adherence measures are needed before any single tool can be recommended as clinically superior or before combined assessment can be shown to improve diabetes outcomes.
