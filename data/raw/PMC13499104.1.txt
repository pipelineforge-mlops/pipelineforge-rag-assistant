Pediatric intraorbital wooden foreign body mimicking an orbital mass: Delayed diagnosis and imaging considerations on MRI

Wooden intraorbital foreign bodies (IOFBs) are uncommon in children and may present a significant diagnostic challenge because their imaging appearance can mimic orbital neoplasms or inflammatory lesions, particularly when the trauma history is absent or unrecognized. We report the case of a 10-year-old boy presenting with progressive left orbital swelling and pain. Initial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a rim-enhancing extraconal lesion with a central non-enhancing component and was initially interpreted as an orbital neoplasm, including a possible orbital schwannoma. No trauma history or external wound was identified, and the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up. Five months later, the patient re-presented with worsening swelling, purulent discharge, and a draining sinus tract. Repeat MRI demonstrated persistent thick peripheral enhancement with more conspicuous internal signal voids and interval development of a draining sinus tract, raising suspicion for retained wooden foreign bodies with an orbital abscess. Surgical exploration confirmed multiple retained wooden fragments within the extraconal orbital compartment. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of wooden IOFBs on MRI and emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for occult foreign bodies in children presenting with atypical orbital lesions, particularly when the clinical history is unclear. Careful sequence-by-sequence MRI interpretation and clinicoradiologic correlation are essential to facilitate timely diagnosis and prevent infectious complications.

Introduction

Ocular trauma remains an important cause of visual morbidity worldwide, and retained intraorbital foreign bodies (IOFBs) represent a potentially serious complication [1]. Although relatively uncommon, retained IOFBs are clinically significant causes of orbital pathology [2]. Organic foreign bodies, particularly wood, are diagnostically challenging because their imaging appearance may mimic neoplastic or inflammatory orbital lesions, especially when trauma history is absent or forgotten [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Computed tomography (CT) is the preferred first-line imaging modality for evaluating orbital trauma because of excellent ability to detect metallic, glass and other inorganic foreign bodies [[7], [8], [9]]. However, wooden foreign bodies may be radiolucent or demonstrate attenuation similar to orbital air or fat, making their detection difficult, particularly during the acute stage [[7], [8], [9]]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), after metallic foreign bodies have been excluded, may better demonstrate retained wooden IOFBs because wood typically appears hypointense on both T1- and T2-weighted images relative to orbital fat [9,10].

Pediatric cases present additional diagnostic challenges because children may not recall, recognize, or report minor trauma [[10], [11], [12]]. Consequently, organic IOFBs may remain silent for prolonged periods, resulting in delayed presentation and diagnosis [13,14]. Delayed recognition may lead to serious complications, including orbital abscess formation, sinus tract development, and prolonged morbidity. Although several reports have described missed intraorbital foreign bodies and their imaging features, most involve adult patients [2]. Pediatric cases remain uncommon in published work, even though children may be more likely to withhold or be unaware of a traumatic event, lowering clinical suspicion for a retained foreign body. Additionally, concerns regarding radiation exposure often lead to MRI being performed instead of CT in this population. As a result, familiarity with MRI appearance and pitfalls becomes especially critical in pediatric evaluations. In this context, we present the case of a pediatric wooden intraorbital foreign body initially interpreted as a possible orbital schwannoma, on MRI, underscoring the importance of considering retained organic foreign bodies in children with atypical orbital lesion and no clear history of penetrating injury.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old boy presented with a 3-month history of progressive swelling and pain in the left eye. On examination, visual acuity was 6/12 in the left eye with associated proptosis, conjunctival chemosis, and eyelid edema. No external wound or trauma history was initially identified. Contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain and orbits was performed (Fig. 1) as the lesion was clinically considered non-traumatic in origin. The initial MRI examination included axial, sagittal, and coronal T1-weighted, T2-weighted, STIR, and post-contrast sequences. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and ADC mapping were not obtained. Imaging demonstrated a well-defined fusiform left extraconal lesion measuring approximately 3 × 1.9 × 2.5 cm with irregular thick peripheral enhancement and a central non-enhancing component. The lesion was predominantly iso- to hypointense relative to the extraocular muscles on T1-weighted images and mildly hyperintense on T2-weighted images with a persistent markedly hypointense central component on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences. Mild proptosis was present. Based on these imaging findings, an orbital neoplasm, including orbital schwannoma, was considered in the differential diagnosis.

The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up for approximately 5 months and later re-presented with worsening swelling, purulent discharge, and a draining sinus tract in the inferior eyelid. Repeat MRI (Fig. 2) was performed. Compared with the initial examination, the lesion showed persistent thick peripheral enhancement with more conspicuous internal signal voids and interval development of a draining sinus tract. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics were initiated, and surgical orbital exploration through an anterior lower eyelid transcutaneous approach revealed frank abscess formation with a draining sinus tract in the extraconal space. Multiple small fragments of wooden material were identified within the abscess cavity (Fig. 3). The abscess was drained, the foreign bodies were carefully removed, and the cavity was irrigated with vancomycin. The abscess cavity did not extend to the orbital apex. Histopathological examination demonstrated plant material associated with suppurative granulomatous inflammation, without evidence of neoplasia or fungal elements. Oral Augmentin was continued postoperatively, and the drainage tube was removed after 48 hours. Following surgery, proptosis resolved and visual acuity improved. At 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of residual foreign bodies or recurrent infection.Fig. 3 Photograph—retrieved foreign bodies. Discussion

Wooden IOFBs remain diagnostically challenging because of variable imaging appearances [2,3]. Diagnosis is particularly difficult in pediatric patients, as a history of trauma is often absent, forgotten, or underestimated [3,11,12]. Consequently, retained wooden foreign bodies may present as atypical orbital masses and lead to delayed diagnosis.

In the present case, the initial MRI demonstrated a well-defined fusiform orbital lesion with thick peripheral enhancement, a central non-enhancing component, and associated proptosis. These findings raised suspicion for an orbital neoplasm, including schwannoma. However, retrospective review revealed persistent irregular T1- and T2-hypointense internal components that did not enhance, as well as subsequent sinus tract formation, which were more suggestive of a retained wooden foreign body with secondary infection.

Retained wooden IOFBs pose substantial diagnostic and clinical challenges [15]. CT is the primary imaging modality for evaluating orbital trauma because of its ability to detect metallic, glass, and other inorganic foreign bodies [7]. However, wooden fragments are frequently missed because of their radiolucent nature and attenuation characteristics that may resemble orbital air or fat [8,9,16]. This limitation is particularly important when the history of penetrating injury is unclear or unavailable.

MRI, while contraindicated in the presence of metallic fragments, can be highly valuable once metallic injury is excluded [8,9]. Wooden foreign bodies typically appear hypointense on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, which may facilitate their detection [10,12]. Nevertheless, associated inflammatory changes, abscess formation, and granulation tissue may dominate the imaging appearance and obscure the retained fragment. Ho et al. [9] reported that wooden foreign bodies commonly appear as irregular hypointense structures surrounded by hyperintense inflammatory tissue and rim enhancement related to abscess formation [9]. Similarly, Nagae et al. [8] emphasized that persistent signal voids associated with peripheral enhancement strongly suggest retained wooden fragments, particularly when CT findings are inconclusive [8].

Although DWI was not performed in the present case, it may provide additional diagnostic information in selected orbital inflammatory lesions because abscess cavities often demonstrate restricted diffusion due to viscous purulent material [17]. Inclusion of DWI sequences in orbital MRI protocols may therefore be helpful when evaluating atypical orbital masses or suspected orbital infection.

MRI may also play a complementary role in detecting occult orbital or intraocular foreign bodies, particularly when CT or ultrasonography findings are negative or equivocal. A recent report in BMC Ophthalmology highlighted a case in which MRI was pivotal in identifying a retained intraocular foreign body that was not clearly detected on initial imaging, ultimately leading to appropriate diagnosis and management [18]. Although CT remains the first-line modality due to its sensitivity for metallic foreign bodies and safety considerations, MRI may provide added value in selected cases, especially when non-radiopaque materials are suspected or when clinical suspicion persists despite negative CT findings.

Wooden IOFBs may remain clinically silent for prolonged periods before presenting with complications such as orbital cellulitis, abscess or fistula formation [13,14]. The porous organic structure of wood promotes microbial colonization and proliferation, predisposing patients to recurrent infection [14,19]. Reported complications include orbital cellulitis, abscess formation, panophthalmitis, chronic draining sinus tracts, and intracranial extension [9,19,20]. In addition, fragmentation during surgical removal has been associated with an increased risk of persistent infection and recurrent inflammatory complications [21].

In the present case, delayed recognition of the retained IOFB resulted in abscess and sinus tract formation. Complete surgical removal of the wooden fragments and drainage of the abscess led to an uncomplicated postoperative recovery without recurrence. MRI played an important complementary role by demonstrating persistent hypointense intralesional components, inflammatory changes and the sinus tract, thereby facilitating definitive surgical management.

Conclusion

Although CT remains the preferred first-line imaging modality for suspected orbital trauma, MRI provides valuable complementary information when retained IOFBs are suspected despite an atypical clinical presentation. Recognition of characteristic hypointense intralesional components together with careful clinicoradiologic correlation can facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate surgical management, thereby preventing delayed diagnosis and potentially serious infectious complications.
