Background

Provider shortages and lack of culturally responsive care limit mental health services to multicultural populations worldwide. We examined the effectiveness of a psychoeducational intervention aimed at building community capacity to address depression and anxiety among racial, ethnic, and linguistic minoritized adults.

Methods

Strong Minds-Strong Communities was a six-month, multicenter, longitudinal, randomized trial conducted in 37 community-based organizations and clinics in two U.S. sites (Massachusetts and North Carolina). English-, Spanish-, Mandarin-, or Cantonese-speaking adults, 18+, with moderate to severe depression or anxiety symptoms were eligible. Participants were randomized (1:1) to a psychoeducational intervention provided by community health workers or a usual care condition receiving a U.S. National Institutes of Health booklet about anxiety and depression. Both conditions included referrals for social determinants of health needs. Primary outcomes were changes from baseline at months six and 12 in self-reported depression and anxiety symptoms (HSCL-25), functioning (WHODAS 2.0), and perceived quality of care. Prespecified subgroup analyses tested race, ethnicity, language, and site outcome differences. The study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04092777).

Findings

From September 25, 2019, to March 3, 2023, we enrolled 1044 participants (524 intervention, 520 usual care). Between baseline and month six, intervention participants reported greater improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms (standardized effect size, 0·38), functioning (standardized effect size, 0·28), and perceived quality of care (standardized effect size, 0·47). These greater improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms, functioning, and perceived quality of care attenuated but remained significant six months post-intervention (standardized effect size, 0·28, 0·21, and 0·33, respectively).

Interpretation

The intervention shows important promise for clinically decreasing depression and anxiety symptoms and improving functioning. The trial included supportive training and supervision to enhance organizational readiness, community engagement, and testing of program effectiveness for building community capacity to tackle mental health care shortages. Findings highlight the importance of testing differences by language and disaggregating ethnic groups.

INTRODUCTION

Immigration has challenged public health around the world. Many counties lack sufficient resources to serve healthcare needs, including a workforce to address linguistic, ethnic, racial, and cultural differences of new arrivals.1 The influx of immigrants seeking better life opportunities, protection from violence, or fleeing war and torture has forced U.S., European, and Asian countries to recognize the need to serve multicultural populations,1 often in need of mental health care.2 According to the WHO 2022 Mental Health Report, nearly one billion people globally are living with a mental health disorder.3 A 2021 meta-analysis of migrant workers reported the prevalence of depression and anxiety to be 38·99% and 27·31%, respectively.4 The WHO reports severe gaps in service coverage, with depression and anxiety progressively identified as a leading threat to population health in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).3 This is partly due to those in need being mostly undetected and untreated, leading to loss of productivity, premature death, and loss of quality of life.5 For depression, receipt rates of minimally adequate treatment range from 23% in high-income countries to as low as 3% in LMICs.3

Most ethnic, racial, linguistic, and immigrant populations are exposed to a range of social determinants that place them at increased risk for mental health issues, including housing instability, racism and discrimination, acculturative stress, language barriers, limited social networks, and a lack of home country connection.6 Although culturally responsive healthcare (defined as intentionally adapting services and approaches to be “rooted in respect and appreciation for the role of culture in the individual’s and family’s beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors”7) can help improve mental health symptoms at the population level, many randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of mental health interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) are not culturally adapted or inclusive of diverse communities.8

Approximately 47·8 million immigrants (14·3%) are living in the U.S.,9 many of whom come from countries where perceptions of mental disorders differ significantly from those of the clinicians responsible for their care.10 Ethnic and racial minoritized groups confront discrimination, with migrants exposed to anti-immigrant hostilities that question their belonging in the country of reception while struggling with the loss of family ties.11 Clinicians are required to provide “culturally responsive care.”12 Yet doing so is difficult because addressing diverse cultural backgrounds requires specific training and time.12

Even in a high-income country, millions of U.S. adults with past-year mental illness received no or inadequate care, with lack of insurance coverage and unaffordable costs creating challenges.13 Given structural and systemic constraints, racial, ethnic, and linguistic minoritized groups are more likely to receive poorer quality services.13 This reflects the mental health system’s lack of culturally responsive care (care that sees the patient as the expert on their conditions and focuses on their definition of the problem and living circumstances)14 to address language and social determinants and fulfill the needs of 67·8 million individuals who spoke a language other than English at home.15 Most U.S. adults with limited English proficiency speak either Spanish (63%) or Chinese (7%).16 Individuals of Latinx and Asian descent together account for 84% of those in the U.S. with limited English proficiency, but only 18% of those proficient in English.17 Inequities also emerge due to a shortage of trained providers, the absence of outreach to identify needs, and a lack of socio-culturally and linguistically adapted evidence-based practices.13 Although Medicaid expansions through the Affordable Care Act in the U.S. could support addressing these mental health challenges, the treatment gap between Whites and racial and ethnic minoritized groups, many of whom are immigrants, has not decreased.18,19 Some immigrant groups only qualify for limited emergency coverage without access to quality mental health care.20 Within such constraints, a proposed solution is building capacity by training individuals from the targeted communities to offer evidence-based programs to address mental health needs.21 This could contribute to closing the research-to-practice gap, particularly for racial, ethnic, and linguistically minoritized populations.21

Community health workers (CHWs) can offer needed services to address the lack of bilingual and bicultural clinicians and reduce mental health symptoms of diverse populations.22 For the current project, we define CHWs as public health workers who apply their knowledge of the language and culture of the participants they work with to deliver evidence-based interventions and address personnel shortages.23,24 A 2018 review of 39 RCTs (mostly international) that included CHWs delivering evidence-based mental health interventions in underserved communities, found that the majority led to symptom reduction.22 However, most were conducted outside the U.S., with small samples.22 Compared to the control condition, CHWs trained in the CBT-based Thinking Healthy Programme reduced six- and 12-month perinatal depression in new mothers in Pakistan25 and three-month depressive symptoms in India.26 A culturally adapted, lay health worker-delivered, CBT-based intervention with 50 older adults in rural China demonstrated a difference between intervention and usual care participants after eight weeks.27 Culturally adapted CBT treatments have also reduced anxiety, depression, and PTSD in patients in Pakistan.28 A community-academic research partnership with Latinx immigrants delivered a stress and coping intervention in Spanish that significantly improved stress management.29

Of the few CHW-led trials in the U.S., most target only one language or cultural group.22 To our knowledge, none of these trials examined whether results vary by participant language, race, and ethnicity, or whether the mental health intervention is superior to usual care that attempts to address social determinants of health issues. Our study improves upon past work by testing the effectiveness of a six-month psychosocial intervention, Strong Minds-Strong Communities (SM-SC). This intervention aims to improve self-reported depression and anxiety symptoms, level of functioning, and perceived quality of care from baseline at month six (intervention end) and month 12 (six months post-intervention). We offered the intervention in community-based organizations (CBOs) or community clinics serving mostly low-income racial, ethnic, and linguistic minoritized communities in two U.S. states.

A number of studies suggest that the SM-SC intervention (previously the Engagement and Counseling for Latinos intervention, ECLA) are effective. The ECLA intervention reduced depression and anxiety symptoms and improved functioning compared to usual care when delivered via clinical staff.30 Subsequent research also showed the intervention’s effectiveness in reducing mood symptoms when CHWs provided it to older adults in combination with physical exercises.23 The SM-SC CHW training program has also shown high acceptability and satisfaction (measured through a six-item self-report scale and participant attendance of >50% of sessions) and CHWs’ increased competency in mental health care provision.23,31 Building on this accumulating evidence, the current paper reports on testing its effectiveness and implementation in a community context to fully assess its generalizability.32

METHODS

Study Design

The SM-SC trial was an investigator-initiated multicenter, randomized, controlled trial funded by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). It was conducted at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston and the University of North Carolina (UNC) Greensboro. The Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the trial, with review ceded from UNC Greensboro. The synthesized trial protocol is available in the Supplementary Appendix. An independent data safety and monitoring board oversaw the trial. The authors assume accountability for the accuracy and completeness of the data and analyses, the trial’s fidelity, and this report’s accuracy. No commercial interests were involved.

Participants

All participants provided written or oral informed consent before enrollment. Bilingual research staff who assessed capacity to consent in the participant’s primary language (English, Spanish, Mandarin, or Cantonese) obtained consent using a validated screener. Consent was transitioned from written to oral after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, as permitted after IRB review. Staff recruited participants from 20 CBOs (non-profits that seek to provide support to members of their local community) and 17 clinics serving a high proportion of clients who identify as Latinx (primarily Spanish speaking), Asian (mainly Mandarin or Cantonese speaking), or Black (primarily English speaking). Half of the participants were recruited in North Carolina and half in Massachusetts. From September 4, 2019, to March 2, 2023, research staff assessed 2,584 potential participants for eligibility. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 or older, fluent in English, Spanish, Mandarin, or Cantonese, with moderate to severe depression or anxiety symptoms, ascertained using the Computerized Adaptive Test for Mental Health (CAT-MH), a suite of validated computer adaptive tests.33–35 A cut-off score of 50+ is indicative of moderate to severe depression on the CAT-MH depression when administered in English and 42+ when administered in Spanish or Chinese. A cut-off score of 51+ is indicative of moderate to severe anxiety on the CAT-MH anxiety when administered in English and 41+ when administered in Spanish or Chinese (Gibbons, R., University of Chicago, personal communication). Both CAT-MH scores are based on responses to algorithm-selected items that minimize participant burden using item response theory methodology and were calibrated with case-control data (anxiety cases for CAT-MH-Anxiety and depression cases for CAT-MH-Depression).33,35 The study excluded potential participants if they did not meet the minimum threshold in a capacity to consent questionnaire; reported mania, active suicidal ideation, or substance use concerns (clinical concerns the intervention did not address); or if they were receiving ongoing or recent psychological services (either in the past three months or scheduled for the upcoming month), to avoid confounding effects. The intervention does not address substance use because, without access to medications, it was beyond the scope of work for CHWs and thus, a safety issue. The study team presented to clinical and community-based organization leaders to discuss outreach and recruitment opportunities and to generate feedback on appropriate recruitment procedures. Recruitment occurred in person or remotely by phone through outreach to clinicians, participant referrals, or clinical lists (See Supplementary Appendix). Our target population was participants who racially or ethnically self-identified as Black, Latinx, or Asian with moderate to severe depression or anxiety symptoms, to assess whether the intervention worked for these groups. We included a sample of White participants if they were in the same recruitment sites. CHWs offered the intervention in four languages spoken by these groups (Spanish, English, Mandarin, or Cantonese), which allowed us to offer participation to many immigrants.

Randomization and Masking

Eligible participants first conducted a baseline interview and then were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either a ten-session psychoeducational intervention or usual care (control group) for up to six months. Randomization was stratified by site using a computer-generated (NQuery software) block randomization scheme of size two so that for every two participants, one was randomized to each group by site. A blinded data manager generated the allocation sequence using a pseudorandom numbers generator. Research staff enrolled patients after assessing eligibility. Investigators and participants were not masked to treatment allocation, but outcome assessors were. Follow-up assessments were conducted by phone or in person at three, six, and 12 months after baseline.

Procedures

SM-SC is an evidence-based, culturally tailored intervention incorporating approaches adapted from cognitive behavioral therapy (e.g., cognitive restructuring), mindfulness exercises, psychoeducation on healthy habits, motivational interviewing, and behavioral activation through pleasant activities and supportive relationships (see Supplementary Appendix).23 The intervention manual is organized in ten one-hour sessions and was provided individually in the participant’s language by one of 20 trained CHWs.

CHWs were recruited through job postings and advertisements and required to have completed high school (the majority had some college education and higher), experience working within the community, and shared language and background with one of the target groups. CHWs received 60–80 hours of structured training on psychoeducation; skills covered in the manual; techniques supporting delivery (e.g., motivational strategies, establishing a relationship, cultural responsiveness); and supervision of manual delivery through audio-recorded practice, role plays, and pilot cases, with training processes that were identical across the two sites. Supervisors (licensed psychologists or counselors) determined CHWs’ competency based on four days of instruction, two 10-session role plays, two 10-session CHW pilot cases, and two hours of weekly supervision. Supervisors listened to audio-recorded role plays and pilot cases to assess 75% CHW fidelity to intervention components. CHWs then received ongoing one-hour supervision to offer the skills-based program and monthly group training on specialized topics (See Supplementary Appendix).

The study team culturally adapted the intervention (both in language and content) for use among a broad group of minoritized populations (Latinx, Asian, and Black) with few exclusionary criteria to improve generalizability and ease of implementation. We achieved cultural adaptation through formal stepped adaptation processes guided by Castro and Barrera’s frameworks.36 This included cultural translation of the manual and measures, review and edits by mental health clinicians from the target ethnic and racial groups, and adaptations that addressed issues of mental health literacy, social customs, social norms, and the role of race, ethnicity, language, and sociocultural context, following work done by the author (See Supplementary Appendix).31 CHWs delivered weekly SM-SC sessions by phone, in-person, or video conference across six months, including skills practice, symptom monitoring, and the creation of a self-care plan. To adapt to the COVID-19 pandemic, the study team updated the protocol to shift from in-person to phone and Zoom-based sessions. Care managers linked participants to social determinants of health (SDoH) referrals. Care managers generally had similar, non-clinical backgrounds to CHWs, but were differentiated by the training they received to provide the activities corresponding to their role. Research staff assessed SDoH needs using a tool which screened for food insecurity, housing instability, problems paying utilities, lack of transportation, trouble paying for medications, unemployment, child or family care demands, and mental health care needs.37

The control condition (usual care) involved receipt of an NIH booklet about anxiety and depression in Spanish, English, or Mandarin/Cantonese. A care manager contacted participants by phone four times over six months to monitor depression and anxiety symptoms (using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)),38 and suicidal thoughts and behaviors, thus mimicking the administration schedule in the intervention group. We instituted an emergency protocol for addressing suicidal behaviors and worsening of symptoms (see Supplementary Materials). As in the intervention group, the care manager offered referrals for SDoH, characterized by offering an appointment, recommendation, or contact information to control participants. Staff conducted all elements of the study in the participant’s language. On average, each of the 20 CHWs saw 25·7 cases, with total case numbers ranging from two to 54.

Supervision, Fidelity, and Adherence to Intervention

CHWs recorded all intervention sessions, and supervisors evaluated fidelity for 15% of each CHW’s cases (randomly selected using a number generator) using a 9–12-item form assessing manual compliance and session quality. Average fidelity across all five supervisors was 85·8 (range, 77·0–89·0) (see Supplementary Appendix). Ongoing supervision and training prevented fidelity “drift” over time. Supervisors focused on the fidelity of skill delivery and provided support for high-risk or emergency situations. Across both sites, we identified seven enrolled participants who reported a suicide attempt, considered a serious adverse event, and one death not by suicide, none of which were deemed related to the protocol.

Outcomes

The primary effectiveness endpoints were change in depression and anxiety symptoms from baseline at month six (intervention end) and month 12 (six months post-intervention) using scores on the Hopkins Symptom Checklist-25 (HSCL-25);39 level of functioning using scores on the 12-item World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2·0 (WHODAS 2·0);40 and perceived quality of care using scores on the Global Evaluation of Care domain of the Perceptions of Care Outpatient Survey (PoC-OP).41 Scores on the HSCL-25 range from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating worse depression and anxiety symptoms.39 Scores on the WHODAS 2·0 range from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating worse functioning.40 Scores on the PoC-OP range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better perceived quality of care (see Supplementary Appendix).41 The key secondary effectiveness endpoints include change from baseline in CAT-MH depression score (range 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating worse depression symptoms)33 and CAT-MH anxiety score (range 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating worse anxiety symptoms)35 at months six and 12. The adaptive nature of the CAT-MH targets a participant’s specific level of severity at that point in time. Because the same questions are not repeatedly administered, CAT-MH is ideal for longitudinal assessments. Thus, a pattern of change in CAT-MH scores consistent with that of the primary outcomes would strengthen the significance of our findings. We also evaluated primary and key secondary effectiveness endpoints in prespecified subgroup analyses based on participants’ race and ethnicity, language, and site. Other exploratory endpoints included change from baseline in HSCL-25, WHODAS 2·0, PoC-OP, CAT-MH depression, and CAT-MH anxiety scores at month three, about midway through the intervention. Finally, we evaluated the primary and key secondary effectiveness endpoints in exploratory subgroup analyses based on the participant’s baseline severity of depression and anxiety. All measures showed adequate psychometric properties across languages (see Supplementary Appendix).

Statistical Analysis

A sample of 1044 participants provided adequate power to evaluate whether the effect of the intervention varied as a function of the participant’s race, ethnicity, language, or site (see Supplementary Appendix). Effect size estimates from our preliminary data suggested that the ten-session SM-SC psychoeducational intervention improves the primary outcomes of depression and anxiety symptoms and level of functioning with an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0·68 and 0·24, respectively. With a two-sided significance level of 0·05, a sample of 1044 participants would provide the study with 87% power to detect a between-group difference of size 0·20 or higher in the primary effectiveness outcomes.

We analyzed all primary and key secondary endpoints according to the intention-to-treat principle and included all randomized participants. The SM-SC intervention is designed to improve depression and anxiety symptoms, level of functioning, and perceived quality of care. To test global effectiveness, as well as specific improvements in each outcome, we adopted the step-down Benjamini-Hochberg approach to keep the false discovery rate to be less than 5%. This step-down procedure adjusted for the multiple comparisons across five outcome scores (HSCL-25, WHODAS 2·0, PoC-OP, CAT-MH depression, and CAT-MH anxiety) at two follow-up points (months six and 12). If the lowest of the ten P values was less than 0·005 (0·05 ÷ 10), it was considered significant; the second lowest P value was significant if less than 0·0056 (0·05 ÷ 9); the third lowest P value was significant if less than 0·006 (0·05 ÷ 8); and so on. Results are presented as point estimates and their standardized effect size (Cohen’s d). The latter is presented to provide a common metric for comparing the intervention effect across all primary and key secondary endpoints and to aid in interpreting the magnitude of the difference between intervention and control groups.

We separately analyzed primary and key secondary effectiveness endpoints using a general linear model to estimate the treatment effect. Except for PoC-OP, all analyses included baseline outcome scores as covariates. All analyses also included the site as a covariate. Because we recruited participants from 20 CBOs and 17 clinics across the two sites, we used generalized linear mixed models with a random intercept accounting for clustering within CBOs/clinics for each primary and key secondary effectiveness endpoint in sensitivity analyses. We performed prespecified subgroup analyses of the primary and key secondary endpoints for participant’s self-identified race and ethnicity (non-Latino White, non-Latino Black, non-Latino Asian, and Latino), language (English, Spanish, Mandarin, and Cantonese), and site (Massachusetts and North Carolina). We tested subgroup differences in three omnibus interaction tests examined for five outcome scores (HSCL-25, WHODAS 2·0, PoC-OP, CAT-MH anxiety, and CAT-MH depression) at month six and month 12, which resulted in 30 statistical tests. In prespecified subgroup analyses, no adjustment for the multiple comparisons was made. Thus, with a two-sided P value of 0·05, about two statistically significant interaction tests were expected based on chance alone (30 × 0·05).

Under the missing at random assumption, we handled missing values using 20 imputed data sets using model-based imputation for the primary and key secondary effectiveness endpoints at months six and 12. Similarly, we used model-based multiple imputation (20 imputed data sets) to handle missing data for the prespecified subgroup analyses of the primary and key secondary endpoints. All imputation models included the auxiliary baseline variables associated with missingness (see Supplementary Appendix).

We similarly analyzed change from baseline at the exploratory endpoint at month three and exploratory subgroup analyses based on participant’s baseline severity of depression and anxiety using a general linear model, which included baseline scores (except for PoC-OP) and site as covariates. We performed analyses with Stata software, version 18 (Stata Corp), and RStudio software, version 2023·12·0·369 (Posit Software, PBC).

Role of the Funding Source

The study’s funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

RESULTS

From September 25, 2019, to March 3, 2023, the research team enrolled 1044 participants; 337 (32·3%) because of moderate to severe depression symptoms only, 43 (4·1%) because of moderate to severe anxiety symptoms only, and 664 (63·6%) because of both moderate to severe depression and anxiety symptoms. We randomly assigned 524 (50·2%) to the intervention condition, and 520 (49·8%) to the control group. Figure 1 provides complete details about the trial flow. The distribution of baseline characteristics was well-balanced between the two groups (see Table 1). The mean age of participants was 42·6 years; 875 (83·8%) were female, 654 (62·6%) were Latino, 149 (14·3%) were non-Latino Black, 137 (13·1%) were non-Latino Asian, and 92 (8·8%) were non-Latino White participants. Most participants were foreign-born (N=704; 67·4%), had at least a high school diploma (N=640; 61·3%), and were insured (N=683; 65·4%). After adjusting for multiple comparisons, all p-values for the primary and key secondary endpoints remained significant (see Supplementary Appendix). At the six-month endpoint (end of intervention), intervention participants had a reduction in depression and anxiety symptoms (HSCL-25 scores) of 0·44 points, while control participants had a reduction of 0·24 points (difference, 0·20 points; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0·14 to 0·26; Cohen’s d, 0·39; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·27 to 0·52) (Figure 2A). Intervention participants had a greater improvement in the level of functioning (WHODAS 2·0 scores) compared to control participants (difference, 2·39 points; 95% CI, 1·38 to 3·40; Cohen’s d, 0·28; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·16 to 0·39) (Figure 2B) and a notable higher improvement in perceived quality of care (PoC-OP scores) (difference, 8·75 points; 95% CI, 5·87 to 11·63; Cohen’s d, 0·47; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·31 to 0·62) (Figure 2C). Six months post-intervention at the 12-month endpoint, intervention participants still reported greater improvements in HSCL-25 scores (difference, 0·14 points; 95% CI, 0·08 to 0·20; Cohen’s d, 0·28; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·16 to 0·40), WHODAS 2·0 scores (difference, 1·78 points; 95% CI, 0·71 to 2·85; Cohen’s d, 0·21; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·08 to 0·33), and PoC-OP scores (difference, 6·18 points; 95% CI, 3·00 to 9·35; Cohen’s d, 0·33; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·16 to 0·50), although intervention effects attenuated (standardized effect sizes were about 30% lower; Figure 3).

The standardized effect sizes for HSCL-25 scores were in the small to medium range. However, they represented a sizable difference in the proportion of participants reporting clinically relevant symptoms of depression and anxiety (scores above 1·75). At the six-month endpoint, 50·3% of control participants reported clinically relevant symptoms of depression and anxiety compared to 36·0% of intervention participants. The success rate difference (SRD) was 14·7%. At the 12-month endpoint, 47·8% of control participants reported clinically relevant symptoms of depression or anxiety compared to 38·4% of intervention participants. The SRD over the one-year follow-up was 8·8% (see Supplementary Appendix).

The pattern of change in key secondary endpoints was consistent with that of primary outcomes. At the six-month endpoint, intervention participants had a reduction in depression symptoms (CAT-MH depression scores) of 19·1 points (on a scale from 0 to 100) and control participants a decrease of 11·0 points, which represented a large effect size (difference, 8·07 points; 95% CI, 5·65 to 10·49; Cohen’s d, 0·65; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·45 to 0·84) (Figure 4A). Intervention participants also had a larger reduction in anxiety symptoms (CAT-MH anxiety scores) of 20·1 points compared to a decrease of 11·5 points among control participants (difference, 8·56 points; 95% CI, 5·84 to 11·27; Cohen’s d, 0·48; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·33 to 0·63) (Figure 4B). Intervention participants still reported greater improvements in CAT-MH depression and anxiety scores six months post-intervention at the 12-month endpoint (difference, 5·14 points; 95% CI, 2·95 to 7·33; Cohen’s d 95%, 0·41; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·24 to 0·59; and difference, 4·29 points; 95% confidence interval, 1·59 to 6·99; Cohen’s d, 0·24; Cohen’s d 95% CI, 0·09 to 0·39, respectively) (Figure 4), with attenuated standardized effect sizes. All results from sensitivity analyses accounting for clustering within CBOs/clinics were almost identical (see Supplementary Appendix).

The results of prespecified subgroup analyses at the primary six- and 12-month endpoints are shown in Figures 2 and 3. For the level of functioning (WHODAS 2·0 scores) at the 12-month endpoint, there was an indication of greater treatment effectiveness among non-Latino White participants and null effectiveness among non-Latino Asian participants, which combined Mandarin and Cantonese speakers (P for interaction = 0·02) (Figure 3). There was also an indication of greater treatment effectiveness in anxiety symptoms per the CAT-MH at the six-month endpoint among Mandarin-speaking participants and null effectiveness among Cantonese-speaking participants (P for interaction = 0·02) (see Supplementary Appendix). The point estimates for all the treatment effects were in the predicted direction, except for HSCL-25 scores in the Cantonese group. This discrepancy is not definitive because of the small sample size, but it is important to note. Results were generally consistent at the exploratory endpoint in month three in that participants in the intervention group had greater improvements in all primary (HSCL-25, WHODAS 2·0, and PoC-OP scores) and key secondary outcomes (CAT-MH depression and anxiety scores) even before the intervention period ended (see Supplementary Appendix). The results of exploratory subgroup analyses at the primary and key secondary endpoints also indicated that the intervention effect did not vary as a function of baseline severity of depression or anxiety symptoms (see Supplementary Appendix).

DISCUSSION

Our study in two US states with similar immigrant populations to US estimates (12·7% across Massachusetts and North Carolina versus 14·3% in the US)42,43 shows promise as a secondary prevention and disparities reduction strategy that may tackle mental health worker shortages and reduce the research-to-practice gap. Few researchers have collaborated with CHWs to test psychosocial interventions in non-English languages for large, diverse populations in low-resource CBOs in the U.S. to determine if the effectiveness differs by language, race, or ethnicity. In a systematic review of the literature, we found no randomized controlled trials of CHW-led mental health interventions conducted in more than two languages, or that utilized social determinants referrals as a control group. Findings are consistent with a previous systematic review conducted by Barnett et al. (2018), showing that CHW-led interventions in the US performed significantly better than a comparison condition on the mental health outcome measured, including depression and anxiety.22 Our findings are also consistent with Barnett et al. on sustained effects at follow-up assessment22 and with successful RCTs of CHW-led interventions for racial, ethnic, and linguistic minoritized groups in the US44 and internationally.45,46

Consistent with previous estimates for native-born and first- and second-generation immigrants in the US,47 most enrolled participants had both moderate to severe depression and anxiety symptoms, and a higher proportion of participants were enrolled because of depression symptoms only. Similar to other smaller US and international studies with CHW-led interventions, results showed program effectiveness for primary outcomes in the small to moderate effect size range and significant effects in the secondary outcome, sustained at 12-month follow-up.48 With extensive training and continued clinical supervision, CHWs maintained high fidelity rates to a manualized intervention, leading to significant participant symptom improvement. These effect sizes are comparable to a recent meta-analysis of culturally-adapted psychosocial interventions for Latinos in the US primarily delivered by mental health professionals.48 Effects were evident across most racial, ethnic, and language groups, underscoring the potential to task-shift the delivery of psychoeducational interventions to expand the workforce.

The few exceptions were the non-Latino Asian group when both Mandarin and Cantonese speakers were combined for the level of functioning (WHODAS 2·0 scores) at the 12-month endpoint and the Cantonese-speaking group for anxiety symptoms per the CAT-MH at the six-month endpoint. The results from our prespecified subgroup analyses (Figures 2 and 3, and Figures S1 and S2 in the Supplementary Appendix) suggested that the Cantonese participants drove any indication of a null intervention effect in the non-Latino Asian group. Given that Mandarin participants did well when separated from Cantonese participants, our findings emphasize the importance of not aggregating non-Latino Asian populations as a monolithic group. Because the intervention sample size for the Cantonese-speaking group was small, it is premature to conclude that it does not work. However, our Chinese research assistants noticed challenges with some of the terminology used in the assessment interviews, consistent with others’ clinical observations with Cantonese participants.49 Further, the difficulties navigating the differences between written and spoken Cantonese reported by Chinese CHWs might be related to 40·5% of Cantonese speakers completing less than high school compared to only 12·5% of Mandarin speakers. It is possible that education or health literacy could explain the differences between these groups. Interestingly, the Cantonese group had the strongest intervention effect for greater perceived quality of care, highlighting a possible disconnect between their experience of the intervention and its functional benefits. Findings point to the importance of additional research on this understudied group, including potential interactions between intervention effectiveness, language, and education level (which we were not powered to test). The result of greater treatment effectiveness among non-Latino White participants for the level of functioning (WHODAS 2·0 scores) at the 12-month endpoint might be similarly linked to having more resources, and less hardship and SDoH.50 There are many well-documented drivers of inequity, such as housing and food insecurity, that are exacerbated for minoritized populations.50 By 12 months there may have been challenges to implement some of the skills adopted because of persistent stressors.

The SM-SC intervention adds to the rapidly growing research base of CHW models of mental health service delivery that can effectively address global and domestic disparities in care.22 Our findings support the need to diversify the workforce to allow for race, ethnic, and language-concordant services that are culturally responsive. Lack of cultural and language-concordant services can lead to premature service termination of minoritized populations.21 Our study included elements that have been shown to bridge cultural mistrust (e.g., racial and language matching)51 and emphasized cultural responsiveness components similar to those mentioned by Bhui et al.52 Further, CHWs reported several intervention components as central to having a shared narrative of experience. These components included shared identity with their patients (e.g., language and racial and ethnic concordance), tailoring sessions to their patients using a collaborative approach, and receiving supervision by culturally and linguistically matched licensed clinicians.

The present study has several limitations, including a 12-month follow-up to assess the intervention’s sustainability. Future studies should evaluate whether the intervention might retain its effects on the outcomes over longer durations. We also had a small sample size in the Cantonese-speaking group, which did not allow us to further investigate the discrepancy in HSCL-25 scores. Future work is needed to examine the potential sources of this discrepancy. The research was only conducted in two states of the US, a high-income country. However, because many sites were CBOs with limited resources and personnel, the intervention showed promise for working in other settings with similar low resources (i.e., other states in the U.S.). Although CBOs participated in a partnership agreement and contributed to study implementation and recruitment, the study did not include Community Advisory Boards (CAB), partly because of the pandemic. Study resources were focused on adapting to a remote intervention, delivering equipment to homes, purchasing participant phones or internet plans, and addressing unexpected challenges, including the closure of community programs. The lack of a CAB delayed obtaining information about CBOs’ need for financial literacy regarding the cost of maintaining the intervention and the high cost of maintaining a licensed supervisor in CBO settings. Rather than a formal CAB, we met regularly with community sites for the project to discuss challenges and obtain input on strategies. Finally, this current study presents only the effectiveness results and not dissemination outcomes that are being separately assessed.

Our study had several important strengths, including randomized assignment with an active control condition (care manager calls to monitor symptoms and provide resources), standardized outcome measures, and careful intervention fidelity monitoring. Our sample provided more diversity than studies carried out in academic institutions, but it was 85% female and primarily immigrants, which limits its generalizability. While this large percentage of female participants is common among depression and anxiety studies,53 it underscores the need to improve our efforts toward greater inclusion of male and nonbinary participants from linguistic minority populations. We note that study enrollment began before the COVID-19 pandemic. Originally intended as a primarily in-person program, SM-SC abruptly pivoted to virtual platforms (phone and videoconference). Because few participants received solely in-person sessions, we cannot examine delivery mode as a potential moderator.

To ensure the continued viability of SM-SC and other future CHW-led interventions, the sustainability of the CHW role within the healthcare system should be addressed. Currently, there is no national U.S. training or certification program for CHWs since requirements vary by state,54 with different stakeholders (e.g., governments, healthcare institutions)55 and Medicaid reimbursement rules.56 While standardized certification requirements can increase CHWs’ career opportunities and credibility,56 mandatory training and certification requirements, along with stringent financial and linguistic requirements, could exclude members of underrepresented communities from becoming CHWs.56 This could inadvertently undermine core approaches that are integral to and uniquely tied to the CHW field.56

The implications of our findings for CHWs are three-fold. First, generous compensation and reimbursement policies and fair and balanced requirements can support the growth and integration of CHWs and CHW-led interventions. Second, CHWs require professional supervisors to receive support and help with burnout. Finally, they require settings in which they are respected as knowledgeable members of the organization. Our findings could have important implications for efforts to mobilize CHWs, as they are crucial for enabling task-shifting to deliver evidence-based mental health care, supervised by licensed clinicians in settings with severe workforce shortages.

Overall, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of a structured, CHW-led, culturally adapted psychoeducational program for adults with moderate or severe depression or anxiety delivered in the participant’s language (English, Spanish, or Mandarin). The use of CHWs permits a more rapid expansion of the mental health care workforce by offering an alternate pathway for community members with a shared language and lived experience to enter the mental health field and offer much-needed services. Providing high-quality, evidence-based care with community experts entails extensive training, ongoing support, and supervision, which requires investment in the mental health care system. Training in the intervention is the first step to building organizational capacity, and along with enhancing the infrastructure of organizations to implement programs on their own, can ultimately lead to wider dissemination.57

However, the scope of practice regulations in many states may limit the implementation of such programs and raise liability concerns, previously raised with nurse practitioners,58 and more recently with CHWs.59 Supervision for CHWs should support them to conduct their highest quality work while simultaneously ensuring that task-shifting does not overstep the line into direct clinical care. CHWs are natural helpers in their communities, and this investment in training and supervision provides evidence-based tools to improve their communities’ mental health. This can result in fewer care disparities and increased community and human capital.60 It is an opportunity to achieve the US Department of Health and Human Services 2022–2026 Strategic Plan of “Expanding equitable access to comprehensive, community-based, innovative, and culturally competent healthcare services while addressing social determinants of health.”61 Only then can we ensure mental healthcare for all.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Evidence before this study

Significant disparities in mental health care exist for racial, ethnic, and linguistic minoritized populations, who are less likely to start and remain in treatment and receive quality care. There is considerable empirical support for brief, manualized, and scalable skill-based interventions delivered by licensed clinicians or experts to reduce symptoms of depression and/or anxiety in adults. However, culturally and linguistically adapted evidence-based mental health care for diverse populations is still limited, and a shortage of trained providers can exacerbate mental health service disparities for diverse communities. Community health workers (CHWs) with expertise in engaging with diverse communities and who may themselves have community connections have been successful in increasing mental health access for minoritized populations. We systematically searched PsychInfo and Web of Science for evidence of brief, scalable, or CHW-led mental health interventions using the terms ((“psychosocial intervention”) OR (“mental health intervention”)) AND (“community health worker”) OR (“brief”) OR (“scalable”) without date or country restrictions. We found evidence for multiple brief CHW-led mental health interventions for racial and ethnic minoritized groups, older adults, and individuals with disabilities. One was a problem-solving mental health intervention for low-income adolescents in India. Another was a combined behavioral activation and social learning intervention for older Latinx adults with anxiety and depression. A final example was a parenting and nutrition intervention that also had secondary effects on maternal depressive symptoms in Tanzania. Most of these studies only utilized participants from one specific racial or ethnic minoritized group. We could not find any randomized controlled trials that were administered in more than two languages or that utilized social determinants of health referrals as a control condition. We also did not find any randomized controlled trials that assessed whether mental health outcomes could vary as a function of the participant’s language, race, or ethnicity.

Added value of this study

This study adds to growing evidence base as an intervention that is culturally responsive, linguistically appropriate, and delivered by CHWs with clinical supervision. We evaluated the intervention’s effectiveness in varied practices with different populations in diverse languages. Our study enabled the community engagement and organizational readiness necessary to build capacity to tackle mental health worker shortages. As such, the intervention provides an alternative for reducing mental health symptoms and improving functioning for minoritized populations. Our study builds off previous interventions designed to reduce mental health disparities of minoritized older adults. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of a new, standardized CHW-led mental health intervention, delivered in English, Spanish, Mandarin, and Cantonese for racial, ethnic, and linguistic minoritized populations. Participants in both treatment and control conditions received referrals to address social determinants of health needs, and thus the culturally adapted, linguistically appropriate intervention was compared to a meaningful usual care condition. We found that participants assigned to the intervention reported a significant reduction in depression and anxiety symptoms and greater improvements in the level of functioning and perceived quality of care when compared to those assigned to usual care with social determinants referrals.

Implications of all the available evidence

The Strong Minds-Strong Communities intervention demonstrates significant potential in mitigating racial and ethnic disparities in mental health care through the role of community experts to fill gaps in mental health services. While racial and ethnic disparities in mental health stem from multiple factors (both at the individual and structural level), a lack of language-matched mental health services can perpetuate disparities. Multiple studies have suggested that not being able to deliver services in the dominant language of the client can decrease the quality of mental health services and discourage racial and ethnic minoritized populations from initiating and engaging in treatment. Our findings support the inclusion of CHW-led, linguistically appropriate interventions in mental health care. Future research should examine how investment in this model can address a range of practice and healthcare system challenges.
