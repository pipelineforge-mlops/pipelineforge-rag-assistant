ABSTRACT

There are no reliable screening tests for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). We performed this study to address this issue. Using 206 serum samples from 95 healthy subjects and 111 patients with PDAC, we developed the novel ACAm‐S index, which consists of age, CA19‐9, and methylated somatostatin. The ACAm‐S index showed a sensitivity of 91.0% (101/111) for all stages of PDAC and a specificity of 94.7% (90/95). Notably, its sensitivity was high at 85.4% (35/41), even for Stage I PDAC. The ACAm‐S index may have potential as a blood‐based screening tool for early‐stage PDAC in the general population.

1 Introduction

As pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a refractory cancer with a poor prognosis, the best way to improve prognosis is through early detection. However, useful biomarkers have not yet been established. Although CA19‐9 has been used clinically, its sensitivity in Stage I PDAC is low at approximately 50% [1]. Additionally, 5%–10% of the general population lacks Lewis enzymes, which can cause false‐negative CA19‐9 results [2]. Therefore, there is a need to develop new biomarkers that complement CA19‐9. One candidate is a liquid biopsy of serum methylated somatostatin (m‐SST) that we reported previously [1]. In this study, we aimed to develop a novel index including CA19‐9 and m‐SST to further improve the diagnostic performance for PDAC.

2 Methods

We retrospectively enrolled 206 participants consisting of 95 healthy subjects (control group) and 111 patients with PDAC (PDAC group) between April 2017 and December 2022. The healthy subjects, who had no gastrointestinal tumors, pancreatic disease, chronic liver disease, or a history of cancer, were recruited from St. Hill Hospital and Ajisu Kyoritsu Hospital. Patients with PDAC were recruited from Yamaguchi University Hospital. The control group consisted of individuals who underwent routine health checkups and had no history or clinical evidence of pancreatic disease or malignancy. As part of routine screening, some participants underwent imaging examinations, such as abdominal ultrasonography or computed tomography, in addition to clinical evaluation and review of their medical history. PDAC was diagnosed based on pathological findings and/or imaging modalities, and tumor staging was determined according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM Classification, 8th edition [3].

Serum samples were stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. DNA was extracted from 1.0 mL of serum into 60 μL of elution buffer using a Maxwell DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Methylation analysis was performed using our proprietary 1‐step combined restriction digital PCR (CORD) assay [4]. Ten microliters of the DNA was mixed with 1 μL each of ExoI, HhaI, HpaII, 10× AmpliTaq Gold Buffer II, 25 mmol/L MgCl2 (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and BstUI (New England Biolabs, Hitchin, UK) in a total volume of 16 μL. The DNA mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h, then at 60°C for 16 h. Finally, the DNA mixture was heated to 98°C for 10 min. Subsequently, 8 μL of DNA that had been treated with the aforementioned enzymes (equivalent to DNA from 83 μL of serum), 0.25 μmol/L of each sense and antisense primer, 0.125 μmol/L of probe, 1× droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) Supermix for Probes (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and water to a total volume of 22 μL were mixed for the ddPCR mixture. The 5′‐3′ DNA sequences of the sense primer, antisense primer, and probe were AAAACAGAGGGAGACGGTTG, GAGTGGCTGGTCAAACTCTA, and Yakima Yellow‐TGCTGCCTGCTGATCCGCGCC‐Eclipse Dark Quencher (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan), respectively. Droplets were generated from the ddPCR mixture using an automated droplet generator (Bio‐Rad). PCR was subsequently performed in a T100 thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad), with initial denaturation at 94°C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, and 56°C for 60 s, and final extension at 98°C for 10 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a QX200 Droplet Reader with QuantaSoft Analysis Pro software (both from Bio‐Rad), and m‐SST copy numbers were calculated per 1 mL of serum. For CA19‐9, an ARCHITECT i2000 automatic immunoassay analyzer and an ARCHITECT CA19‐9XR commercial CA19‐9 immunoassay kit were used (both from Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA).

The chi‐squared test, Mann–Whitney U test, Dunn's test, receiver‐operating characteristics (ROC) analysis, and DeLong's test were performed. Logistic regression with stepwise variable selection was used to identify factors contributing to the diagnosis of PDAC. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications. To address the imbalance in baseline age between the PDAC group and control group, a propensity score‐matched analysis was performed. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and JMP Student Edition 19 (SAS, Cary, NC, USA). A p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

3 Results

3.1 Subject Characteristics

In the control group, 57 healthy subjects (60.0%) were male (median group age, 59 years), whereas in the PDAC group, 50 patients (45.0%) were male (median group age, 72 years). The numbers of patients at stages I, II, III, and IV in the PDAC group were 41, 17, 14, and 39, respectively (Table S1).

3.2 CA19‐9 and Methylated SST

The median values of CA19‐9, with Q1 (lower quartile) and Q3 (upper quartile) values, were 4.0 U/mL (2.0–9.2) in the control group and 463.0 U/mL (28.5–1987.8) in the PDAC group. Similarly, the median values of m‐SST were 324.8 (155.7–638.0) copies/mL in the control group and 2006.1 (1196.1–3315.3) copies/mL in the PDAC group. CA19‐9 levels increased at each stage as PDAC progressed, compared to the control group (each stage vs. the control group, p < 0.001; Figure 1A). Meanwhile, m‐SST levels remained significantly elevated to a similar extent across all stages (each stage vs. the control group, p < 0.001; Figure 1B). Univariate analysis revealed significant differences between the control group and PDAC group in age (p < 0.001), sex (p = 0.032), CA19‐9 (p < 0.001), and m‐SST (p < 0.001).

3.3 Integrated Index for Diagnosing PDAC

We performed a stepwise multivariate logistic regression analysis incorporating the variables that were significantly different in the univariate analysis (age, sex, CA19‐9, m‐SST). After adjustment for age, we found that m‐SST and CA19‐9 were mutually independent factors contributing to the diagnosis of PDAC, ranked in order of strength (z value) (Table 1). Based on these findings, the following formula, termed the ACAm‐S (Age, CA19‐9, and m‐SST) index, was developed to predict the probability of PDAC:

The median values of the ACAm‐S index were 0.024, 0.959, 0.980, 0.992, and 0.998 in the control group and Stages I, II, III, and IV PDAC groups, respectively (each stage vs. the control group, p < 0.001; Figure 1C). As determined by ROC analysis, the area under the curve (AUC) values of CA19‐9, m‐SST, and the ACAm‐S index were 0.90, 0.92, and 0.98, respectively (ACAm‐S index vs. CA19‐9, p < 0.001; ACAm‐S index vs. m‐SST, p < 0.01) (Figure 1D). CA19‐9 alone showed 72.1% (80/111) sensitivity for overall PDAC and 58.5% (24/41) for Stage I PDAC, with 93.7% (89/95) specificity at a cutoff value of 37 U/mL. The cutoff values for m‐SST and the ACAm‐S index were determined based on a specificity of approximately 95%. Under this condition, m‐SST alone showed 63.1% (70/111) sensitivity for overall PDAC and 51.2% (21/41) for Stage I PDAC, with 94.7% (90/95) specificity at a cutoff value of 1605.8 U/mL. In contrast, the ACAm‐S index showed higher accuracy, achieving 91.0% (101/111) sensitivity for overall PDAC and 85.4% (35/41) for Stage I PDAC, and 94.7% (90/95) specificity at a cutoff value of 0.63 (Figure 1E).

To ensure the robustness of the ACAm‐S index and address potential overfitting, internal validation using bootstrapping was performed. The optimism‐corrected AUC estimate of 0.978, with optimism of 0.002, indicated that overestimation due to overfitting was negligible (Table S2).

Furthermore, as the PDAC group was significantly older than the control group at baseline (72 vs. 59 years, p < 0.001; Table S1), a propensity score‐matched analysis was performed. Using nearest‐neighbor matching with a strict caliper of ±5 years, an age‐balanced sub‐cohort was constructed (n = 118; 59 pairs; mean age of 65.1 and 66.8 years for the PDAC group and control group, respectively). The age discrepancy was consequently eliminated (p = 0.280), and the AUC of the ACAm‐S index remained high at 0.964 (Figure S1).

4 Discussion

SST encodes the hormone somatostatin, which regulates growth hormone and inhibits all known hormones of the gastrointestinal tract [5]. SST is highly expressed in the normal pancreas but undergoes hypermethylation in pancreatic cancer tissue. A liquid biopsy targeting m‐SST could be a promising tool for diagnosing PDAC [1, 6]. In this study, the diagnostic performance of m‐SST showed 63.1% sensitivity for overall PDAC and 51.2% for Stage I PDAC, with 94.7% specificity. However, relying solely on m‐SST for the diagnosis of PDAC has its limitations. To further improve the diagnostic performance for PDAC, we developed a novel index that includes CA19‐9 and m‐SST.

The novel aspect of this study is the development of the ACAm‐S index, which demonstrates high diagnostic capability, even for Stage I PDAC. Henriksen et al. reported a similar diagnostic prediction model based on eight methylated genes in plasma cell‐free DNA, age > 65 years, and CA19‐9, with an AUC‐ROC of 0.82–0.89, to distinguish Stage I/II PDAC from chronic pancreatitis [7]. Majumder et al. reported a panel of 13 methylated genes in combination with CA19‐9 that exhibited 80% sensitivity for Stage I PDAC with 97.5% specificity [8]. However, given the limited number of Stage I PDAC cases in each report (11 and 5 cases, respectively), the diagnostic accuracy for Stage I PDAC remains uncertain. Compared to the models described in these reports, the ACAm‐S index has the following advantages. First, it is simple, requiring only three parameters: age, CA19‐9, and m‐SST. Second, cell‐free DNA from only 83 μL of serum is sufficient for a single test, and the methylation assay does not require bisulfite treatment of the DNA, reducing labor and costs (production cost: approximately USD 20 per test). Third, quantitative evaluation of the methylation level by ddPCR ensures accurate and reproducible results. Fourth, in Stage I PDAC patients, the ACAm‐S index achieved 85.4% (35/41) sensitivity and 94.7% (90/95) specificity, indicating its potential for detecting early PDAC.

The present study has several limitations. First, the control group was younger than the PDAC group. Because age is included as a component of the ACAm‐S index, this difference may have partially influenced the discriminatory performance of the model. To address this issue, a propensity score‐matched analysis was performed, and the AUC remained high at 0.964, even in the age‐balanced sub‐cohort. However, because the sample size was small (n = 118; 59 pairs), further examination with a larger sample size is required. Second, because this was a retrospective study and did not include patients with chronic pancreatitis or other benign pancreatic disorders, the diagnostic performance of the index may be overestimated compared with that observed in a more heterogeneous clinical population. Third, as the diagnostic index was developed and evaluated using the same dataset in this exploratory study and we did not perform a validation study using an independent external cohort, we admit that presently, the ACAm‐S index is an exploratory, highly promising diagnostic candidate rather than an immediate, ready‐to‐use screening application. Further validation using an independent cohort will be necessary to confirm the robustness of the model. Fourth, m‐SST is not a specific biomarker for PDAC. In fact, hypermethylation of SST is observed in gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and lung cancer [6]. The ACAm‐S index may be influenced by the presence of other types of cancer, which could reduce its accuracy in diagnosing PDAC in broader clinical populations. Further research on different types of cancer is needed to determine the diagnostic performance of the ACAm‐S index for each type. Finally, we did not evaluate previous illness or lifestyle factors, such as diabetes mellitus, nutritional habits, physical activity levels, smoking, or alcohol consumption, nor did we evaluate metabolic and inflammatory parameters, which may affect the development and progression of PDAC [9]. Further studies will be required to investigate the usefulness of these factors in combination with the ACAm‐S index for PDAC screening.

In conclusion, the ACAm‐S index showed excellent diagnostic performance for PDAC. Further prospective studies will be needed to validate its potential for the early detection of PDAC.
