ABSTRACT

Primary pulmonary MALT lymphoma is a rare, indolent disease with nonspecific imaging often mimicking lung adenocarcinoma. We retrospectively analysed five patients (2020–2025) who underwent video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for suspected adenocarcinoma but were histopathologically diagnosed with MALT lymphoma. Notably, two patients presented atypically with pure ground‐glass nodules (GGNs) and one exhibited a rare coexistence of bilateral consolidations and GGNs. At a median follow‐up of 22.8 months, all patients remained disease‐free or stable. In conclusion, diverse imaging manifestations, such as pure GGNs, complicate the diagnosis of pulmonary MALT lymphoma. Surgical resection is often a diagnostic necessity that also provides secondary therapeutic benefits for localised disease. Despite a generally favourable prognosis, long‐term follow‐up remains essential due to the potential risk of high‐grade transformation.

Our case series illustrates five atypical image manifestations of primary pulmonary MALT lymphoma. We highlight learning points, discuss treatments, prognosis and monitoring and suggest taking this disease into differential diagnosis in clinical practice.

1 Introduction

Mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma is an extranodal lymphoma comprising morphologically heterogeneous small B cells. At mucosal sites, lymphoma cells typically infiltrate the epithelium to form lymphoepithelial lesions. Over 50% of MALT lymphomas occur in the gastrointestinal tract (stomach accounting for up to 85%). In addition to the stomach, the lungs are among the most common organs affected. Lymphoma involving the lung, lobar or primary bronchus is defined as primary pulmonary lymphoma (PPL), with or without mediastinal involvement and no evidence of extrathoracic lymphoma at the time of diagnosis or for 3 months thereafter [1]. Common imaging findings include patchy consolidation, pulmonary nodules and mixed patterns, with nodules being the most prevalent [2]. Given the diagnostic ambiguity and high pre‐test probability of lung cancer, we report five patients suspected of lung adenocarcinoma who were ultimately diagnosed with pulmonary MALT lymphoma following surgery. Two patients presented atypically with pure ground‐glass nodules (GGNs) and a third exhibited the rare coexistence of bilateral consolidations and GGNs. Such radiologic patterns mimic early‐stage lung adenocarcinoma, emphasising the need for clinical awareness, particularly, in asymptomatic patients.

2 Case Series

All five patients were lifelong nonsmokers and asymptomatic.

Patient 1: A 61‐year‐old female with an opacity found 1 year prior. 2024 CT confirmed an 11 × 9‐mm pure GGO with pleural indentation in the right S8 and a 7 × 5 mm GGO in the right S7. Mediastinal lymphadenopathy (10 mm) was present. She underwent VATS S8 segmentectomy. Pathology confirmed MALT lymphoma, while lymph nodes showed inflammatory changes. 3 Discussion

Pulmonary MALT lymphoma is rare and has diverse clinical and imaging characteristics. Its peak incidence occurs in patients aged 60–70 years [3]. MALT lymphoma frequently arises in the gastrointestinal tract but can manifest in pulmonary tissue, accounting for < 5% of Hodgkin lymphoma cases and up to 33% of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma cases [4]. Clinically, most patients with pulmonary lymphoma are asymptomatic; however, approximately 30% present with coughing or dyspnoea. Less common symptoms include chest pain and haemoptysis. Its aetiology is associated with chronic inflammatory or autoimmune disorders and some specific infections (i.e., Helicobacter pylori in the stomach), Sjogren's syndrome of the salivary glands, Hashimoto disease, primary biliary cirrhosis and others [5, 6].

Pathologically, pulmonary MALT lymphomas are characterised by diffuse proliferation of small lymphocytes with slightly irregular nuclei, scant cytoplasm and faint nucleoli. The tumour cells predominantly comprise monocytoid and centroblast‐like cells, with fewer centrocyte‐like and immunoblastoid cells. Neoplastic cells infiltrate the bronchiolar and alveolar epithelium to form lymphoepithelial lesions [7, 8, 9]. Russell bodies inside plasma cells are frequently observed in these nodules.

Typically, pulmonary MALT lymphoma presents as solitary or multiple nodules, masses or areas of consolidation containing air bronchograms [3, 10, 11]. However, our series demonstrates a distinct and atypical imaging phenotype: pure GGNs and mixed patterns of consolidation with GGNs. Unlike typical mass‐like consolidations, the pure GGNs observed in Patients 1 and 2 lacked specific features to distinguish them from lung adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, the coexistence of bilateral consolidations and GGNs (Patient 3) represents a rare ‘mixed’ phenotype that deceptively mimics multicentric lung cancer or inflammatory disease. This radiological overlap with adenocarcinoma is the primary driver of misdiagnosis and necessitates a high index of suspicion in persistent GGNs (Figure 2).

The management of pulmonary MALT lymphoma typically favours conservative observation or systemic therapy once a diagnosis is established. However, in our series, surgical resection was performed as a diagnostic necessity driven by oncologic uncertainty, rather than a primary therapeutic intent. Preoperative differentiation from lung adenocarcinoma was impossible due to the nonspecific imaging (GGNs) and the limitations of percutaneous biopsy for small, deep‐seated lesions. As to PET‐CT, pulmonary MALT lymphoma demonstrates the same FDG avidity as primary lung adenocarcinoma does. For lesions such as pure GGOs and some low‐density partial consolidations on CT scan, which indicate small probability of metastases, it is not a convention to prescribe PET‐CT test. Therefore, malignancy cannot be excluded by a positive PET‐CT result, nor can PET‐CT definitively distinguish low‐grade lymphoma from inflammation or lepidic‐growth adenocarcinoma. Therefore, Video‐Assisted Thoracic Surgery (VATS) was ethically and clinically justified to obtain definitive histology. While the primary goal was diagnostic, surgical resection provided a secondary therapeutic benefit. For localised lesions (Patients 2 and 5), surgery effectively achieved local control. For patients with bilateral disease (Patients 1, 3 and 4), the resection of the dominant lesion confirmed the diagnosis, allowing for observation of the remaining stable nodules, thereby avoiding overtreatment. Also, patients with multiple lung lesions need to be comprehensively evaluated to avoid misdiagnosing coincident adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma (Figure 3) [12].

Despite radiological diversity (e.g., unilateral or bilateral, pure GGN or mixed), our cohort exhibited unified clinical and pathological features. Clinically, all five patients were asymptomatic with an indolent disease course characterised by slow growth over several years. The patients were suspected with lung adenocarcinoma and thus underwent surgery according to radiographic findings. Pathologically, all cases shared consistent histological features: a diffuse proliferation of small B‐cell lymphocytes (centrocyte‐like and monocytoid cells) infiltrating the alveolar epithelium to form lymphoepithelial lesions. Immunohistochemistry consistently confirmed a B‐cell phenotype (e.g., CD20(+), CD21(+) and CD79a (+), CD3(−), CD5(−) and CD10(−)) while excluding other small cell lymphomas (Figure 4).

Pulmonary MALT lymphoma follows an indolent course. Surgery alone or surgery in combination with chlorambucil‐containing chemotherapy for the disease can achieve a complete response [10, 11, 13]. At a median follow‐up of 22.8 months (range: 8–48 months), all patients in our series remain disease‐free or stable without progression. As indolent as it is, advancement to diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) occurs in approximately 18% of cases. When this transformation is confirmed, anthracycline‐containing chemotherapy provides survival outcomes comparable to those of low‐grade MALT lymphoma (Figure 5) [6].

Pulmonary MALT lymphoma is an uncommon malignancy with diverse imaging manifestations that can closely mimic lung adenocarcinoma, particularly when presenting as pure GGNs or mixed bilateral lesions. In selected cases where clinical and radiological findings are indeterminate, surgical resection serves as a critical diagnostic intervention to exclude lung cancer and establish a definitive pathology. While surgery offers therapeutic control for localised disease and guides the management of residual lesions, it should be viewed primarily as a necessary diagnostic step in ambiguous cases rather than a universal treatment standard. Clinicians should maintain awareness of this entity in asymptomatic patients with persistent pulmonary nodules to ensure appropriate long‐term surveillance.
