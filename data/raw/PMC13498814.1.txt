Sepsis remains a leading cause of mortality in intensive care units worldwide, a challenge exacerbated by pathophysiological and clinical heterogeneity that limits the effectiveness of uniform management strategies, motivating the development of phenotype-guided approaches to diagnosis and treatment. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted phenotyping can stratify patients with sepsis into distinct subpopulations with differential immune profiles and heterogeneous treatment responses. This narrative review synthesizes recent advances in AI-assisted sepsis phenotyping, focusing on three persistent research bottlenecks: terminological inconsistency, fragmented integration between prognostic stratification and treatment-response phenotyping, and ambiguous clinical translation pathways. A standardized nomenclature is proposed to improve cross-study comparability, accompanied by a delineation of mainstream AI methodological frameworks, critical care datasets, and multilevel validation systems tailored for intensive care unit scenarios. Eight complementary research dimensions are mapped, including transcriptomic endotyping, single-cell profiling, electronic health record-derived clinical phenotyping, dynamic trajectory modeling, organ dysfunction stratification, biomarker panels, multi-omic integration, and treatment-response phenotyping, with treatment-response phenotyping highlighted as the highest-priority translational frontier. Critical analysis of predominant translational barriers, such as limited model generalizability, insufficient interpretability, poor workflow compatibility, and regulatory uncertainty, is presented alongside stage-specific actionable roadmaps designed to promote real-world clinical deployment. By constructing a unified interdisciplinary framework, this review defines key future research priorities to accelerate the evidence-based transition from algorithmic prototypes to bedside precision sepsis management.

1. Introduction

Sepsis is defined by the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. 1 Despite advances in critical care support and therapeutics, sepsis affects millions of patients annually. 2 This definition represents the latest stage in a decades-long evolution: from the 1992 SIRS-based inflammatory criteria, through the 2001 PIRO staging system that introduced predisposition and organ dysfunction as stratification anchors, to the 2016 Sepsis-3 framework that anchored diagnosis to Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score organ dysfunction.1,3,4 Each iteration sought to improve prognostic precision, yet all remained fundamentally one-dimensional, applying uniform thresholds to a biologically heterogeneous population. Patients with sepsis present with diverse etiologies, complex phenotypes, comorbidities, and rapidly progressive organ dysfunction.5,6 This variability poses significant clinical challenges.

Conventional severity scoring systems assume population-level homogeneity.7–9 Among these tools, the SOFA score is explicitly anchored to the organ dysfunction criteria defined in the Sepsis-3 consensus framework, and therefore identifies sepsis cases only after overt, established organ failure has developed. By contrast, acute physiology-based scoring systems — including the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II/IV (APACHE II/IV) and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II/III — quantify extreme physiological derangements, ranging from vital sign abnormalities and electrolyte disturbances to oxygenation deficits, and these metrics are typically calculated using data collected within the first 24 hours of intensive care unit (ICU) admission.10,11 Unlike SOFA, these physiology-focused scores can flag high-risk patients before structural organ failure is fully established.7,8,12 Nevertheless, neither category of conventional severity scores captures the dynamic, time-varying immune trajectories that drive sepsis progression.13,14

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative tool to decode temporal heterogeneity and dynamic phenotypic evolution.15,16 These algorithms identify latent patient subgroups with distinct immune profiles and treatment responses. This identification occurs independently of baseline risk, enabling precision sepsis care.17–19 Conventional phenotyping strategies evolved from SIRS-based recognition through the PIRO staging system to the consensus-driven Sepsis-3 framework, with Seymour et al. 20 subsequently employing k-means clustering of 29 clinical variables to derive four clinical phenotypes (α, β, γ, δ). Although k-means clustering represents an early application of machine learning, these approaches remained limited by low-dimensional, static admission snapshots and linear assumptions. Contemporary AI—particularly deep learning and ensemble architectures—shifts this paradigm by processing ultra-high-dimensional, longitudinal data without a priori feature constraints, capturing non-linear interactions that conventional scoring and classical clustering cannot encode.17–19 Emerging data streams—including continuous wearable vital-sign monitoring, emergency department-to-ICU trajectory tracking, and post-discharge longitudinal follow-up—are beginning to extend phenotyping beyond the ICU bedside, though these modalities remain largely peripheral to current algorithmic frameworks.

Recent reviews by Tocu et al., 21 Gorecki et al., 22 and Papareddy et al. 23 summarize AI applications in sepsis detection, prognostication, and tailored therapy. However, these reviews do not systematically integrate prognostic stratification and treatment-response phenotyping across the full care continuum.21–23 They also do not address terminological and methodological standardization for interdisciplinary collaboration. Departing from prior reviews that compartmentalize prognostic stratification and treatment-response phenotyping, this article proposes an eight-dimensional, non-hierarchical taxonomy spanning transcriptomic, single-cell, clinical, temporal, organ dysfunction, biomarker, multi-omic, and treatment-response domains. To reconcile the tension between mechanistic depth and bedside accessibility, we introduce a three-axis comparative matrix (bedside accessibility × mechanistic interpretability × translational maturity) that aligns each dimension with its corresponding clinical milestone. 2. Core terminology standardization in sepsis phenotyping

Terminological inconsistency has long hindered sepsis phenotyping research. This inconsistency represents a fundamental barrier to cross-study comparability, research reproducibility, and clinical translation.21,24 Based on current international consensus, four core terminological frameworks are defined: subtypes (or subgroups), endotypes, phenotypes, and theranostic biomarkers.15,21,24 They form a mechanistic-to-actionable spectrum: subtypes are purely descriptive, endotypes are mechanism-anchored, phenotypes are bedside-accessible, and theranostics directly guide therapy. 2.1. Subtypes/subgroups

These are generic descriptive terms for statistically derived patient classifications. No functional, biological, or clinically observable differences are implied between groups. Subtypes/subgroups are limited to preliminary exploratory research. They must not directly inform clinical decision-making. 24

2.2. Endotypes

Endotypes represent patient subpopulations defined by distinct functional or pathophysiological mechanisms. These subpopulations typically show differential mortality risks and treatment responses. Endotypes form the core biological foundation for targeted sepsis therapies.15,32 For example, transcriptome-derived hyperinflammatory and immunosuppressive endotypes directly guide individualized immunomodulatory treatment selection. 32

2.3. Phenotypes

Phenotypes are clinically defined categories or symptom clusters. They arise from genotype-environment interactions. Phenotypes are stratified using routine clinical and biochemical parameters, medical history, disease manifestations, and/or therapeutic outcomes. Unlike endotypes, phenotypes are not tied to confirmed pathophysiological mechanisms. However, they offer far greater clinical accessibility for rapid bedside implementation.24,36

2.4. Theranostic biomarkers

Theranostic biomarkers are measurable indicators. They encompass predictive biomarkers, treatment effect markers, and theranostic markers that predict patient response to specific interventions. These biomarkers serve as the critical bridge linking phenotyping results to clinical decision-making.15,30 The interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)/interleukin-10 (IL-10) ratio, for example, is a validated candidate marker for guiding hydrocortisone therapy in sepsis. 30

2.5. AI-driven electronic phenotyping

AI-driven electronic phenotyping extends traditional phenotype boundaries. This extension occurs through automated, dynamic classification via electronic health record (EHR) integration. Model accuracy and reproducibility remain pivotal to clinical translation. 16 The field is transitioning from proof-of-concept validation to demonstration of clinical utility. Interpretable models, multicenter validation, and standardized infrastructure are core priorities for next-generation precision sepsis care. 16

3. Artificial intelligence applications and phenotyping positioning in sepsis

3.1. Core AI applications in sepsis management

AI applications relevant to sepsis phenotyping fall into two interrelated, clinically distinct domains aligned with the sepsis care continuum.15,37 The first, prognostic stratification, groups patients by mortality risk or adverse event probability using baseline comorbidities and organ dysfunction severity, serving as a foundational approach to guide resource allocation and clinical counseling. 38 While AI-driven early warning systems achieve high discrimination for sepsis recognition, they do not stratify patients by treatment response—the core translational priority of this review. 25 The second domain, treatment-response phenotyping, stratifies patients by expected response to specific interventions independent of baseline prognosis, a framework first established and validated in multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) cohorts by Seymour et al. 20 AI-integrated phenotyping identifies patient subgroups with differential organ dysfunction trajectories from EHR, improving diagnostic specificity for sepsis while enabling personalized therapeutic selection.23,39

3.2. Core dimensions of AI-Assisted sepsis phenotyping

3.2.1. Molecular and cellular granularity: Transcriptomic endotypes and single-cell profiling

Moving beyond rigid hierarchical taxonomies, contemporary approaches can be conceptualized across eight principal, non-hierarchical dimensions: transcriptomic endotypes, single-cell resolution profiling, EHR-derived electronic phenotyping, temporal trajectory modeling, organ dysfunction trajectories, biomarker panels, multi-omic integration, and treatment-response phenotyping.24,32 To begin with the most granular level, transcriptomic endotypes utilize whole-blood gene-expression profiling—often via microarray or RT-qPCR-compatible panels—to delineate molecular subpopulations characterized by divergent immune trajectories and differential treatment responses.33,40,41 Complementing this, single-cell resolution approaches employ single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and related cellular atlases to dissect pathogenetically distinct immune cell populations that drive heterogeneous clinical outcomes.42–45

3.2.2. Bedside-accessible clinical layers: Electronic phenotyping, temporal trajectories, and organ dysfunction trajectories

Transitioning to the clinical domain, electronic phenotyping leverages EHR data to identify clinical subtypes via unsupervised clustering of routine admission parameters—such as vital signs, laboratory values, and comorbidities—offering the highest degree of bedside accessibility currently available.20,31,36 However, static classification often obscures the evolutionary nature of the disease; therefore, temporal trajectories utilize longitudinal modeling of routinely monitored physiological variables, most notably temperature and vital-sign patterns, to capture dynamic disease progression.13,17,34 Closely related is the analysis of organ dysfunction trajectories, which involves the dynamic tracking of repeated organ-failure scores over the first 24–72 hours. This allows for the identification of rapid deteriorators or improvers, thereby enabling more precise treatment-response assessments.6,35,46

3.2.3. Integrative and action-oriented frameworks: Biomarker panels, multi-omics, and treatment-response phenotyping

In terms of diagnostic tools, biomarker panels integrate machine learning with multiplex molecular or biochemical assays to balance biological specificity with point-of-care compatibility.32,47 For a more comprehensive mechanistic grounding, multi-omic integration fuses transcriptomic, proteomic, and/or metabolomic layers—often via dimensionality reduction and deep clustering—to construct subtypes that single-modality approaches cannot resolve.18,24,48,49 Ultimately, these analytical efforts converge on treatment-response phenotyping. This dimension focuses on stratifying patients by their predicted differential response to specific interventions, independent of baseline prognostic risk, representing a central translational goal of precision sepsis care.

These eight dimensions are complementary entry points for unpacking sepsis heterogeneity, yet they remain predominantly host-centric, largely omitting pathogen identity, antimicrobial resistance, and tissue pathology data essential for disentangling host-driven from pathogen-driven biology. 33 Integrating real-time microbiology and digital pathology with host-derived classifiers represents a critical frontier that current systems have only begun to address.

These dimensions also vary sharply in technical maturity: electronic phenotyping and temporal trajectories have accumulated robust multicenter retrospective evidence, whereas treatment-response stratification has rarely achieved prospective therapeutic validation in RCT designs, 50 and multi-omic integration remains confined to small-scale proof-of-concept. This uneven progress creates a lopsided translational gradient that the field has long overlooked. Ultimately, clinical actionability depends on the intended use case, local infrastructure, and the trade-off between turnaround time and biological resolution.

3.3. Comparative clinical utility

To systematically compare the eight non-hierarchical phenotyping dimensions, we map them along three orthogonal axes: bedside accessibility, mechanistic interpretability, and translational maturity (Table 2). This framework reveals inherent trade-offs. Electronic, temporal, and organ dysfunction phenotyping offer high accessibility but limited mechanistic depth. 4. Technical framework of AI-Assisted sepsis phenotyping

The AI-assisted sepsis phenotyping framework integrates three core modules that collectively determine reliability and clinical translation value15,50: Algorithm systems spanning traditional methods to ensemble learning and deep learning architectures, each offering distinct advantages in feature extraction, nonlinear relationship modeling, and interpretability.38,52 Data integration leverages EHR, multi-omics datasets, and continuous physiological monitoring to comprehensively characterize sepsis heterogeneity.32,39 Validation protocols employ multilevel strategies including internal cross-validation, temporal validation, external independent validation, and prospective clinical assessment to ensure model robustness and generalizability.53,54 Additionally, controllable AI techniques such as cautious classification enhance clinical deployment safety by enabling model abstention during distribution shifts. 55

4.1.1. Supervised learning

Supervised learning establishes input-output mappings using labeled datasets. SVM classifiers with continuous physiological monitoring predict sepsis with moderate accuracy.54,56 XGBoost outperforms qSOFA/SIRS in emergency sepsis identification, though external validation reveals performance decline, highlighting generalizability concerns. 54 Advantages include automated classification of validated subtypes; limitations involve vulnerability to missing data, multicollinearity, overfitting, and dependence on high-quality labels.15,54 Given that supervised learning serves as the most fundamental algorithm for ICU sepsis modeling, its application in the context of sepsis ICU data demands particular attention. It heavily relies on high-quality annotated EHR data and is susceptible to common defects of real-world ICU datasets, including missing values and noise.53,57

4.1.2. Unsupervised learning

Unsupervised learning discovers latent structures in unlabeled data, dominating heterogeneity research.15,32 Clustering (k-means, PAM, LPA, hierarchical) and dimensionality reduction (PCA, t-SNE) identified NK cell cytotoxicity-related hub genes (6-HubGss), yielding the robust SepxFindeR model for sepsis-septic shock differentiation. 32 Plasma peptidome PCA with ML distinguished inflammatory states with high sensitivity and specificity. 48 Key strengths include novel subtype discovery; limitations involve lack of direct outcome association requiring supervised validation.15,32 However, a more fundamental structural limitation is that conventional unsupervised clustering operates on static admission snapshots and assigns each patient to a single, immutable subtype. This architectural assumption conflicts with sepsis pathophysiology, in which patients can oscillate between hyperinflammatory and immunosuppressive states within hours. Recent longitudinal phenotyping studies demonstrate that nearly half of patients with sepsis transition between phenotypes during the initial 6 hours of emergency care, with early antibiotic administration and fluid resuscitation actively modifying these trajectories. 58 Consequently, static clustering risks misclassifying patients who have already shifted to a biologically distinct state by the time therapeutic decisions are made, and it obscures the evolutionary dynamics that define the syndrome.

4.1.3. Large language models

Large language models (LLMs) process unstructured clinical data to enhance phenotyping interpretability. LLaMA-3 8B extracted symptoms from admission records with high accuracy and identified mortality-associated symptom clusters in large patient cohorts. 59 GPT-4-derived sepsis knowledge graphs achieved moderate performance for entity-relation extraction. 60 However, the current consensus supports LLMs as feature extractors, not autonomous decision makers, in sepsis phenotyping.20,61,62 When deployed in ICU settings, LLMs present specific failure modes beyond general “black-box” concerns. Drawing on current LLM safety literature, we hypothesize three risk patterns warranting vigilance in ICU phenotyping: (i) reasoning hallucination, in which the chain-of-thought rationale becomes misaligned with final predictions; (ii) adversarially induced output distortion, whereby manipulated inputs may elicit fabricated patient parameters or inconsistent trajectories; and (iii) output inconsistency, under which repeated queries or minor prompt variations yield divergent phenotyping outputs. These patterns are informed by complementary evidence: adversarial hallucination attacks can propagate fabricated clinical details 63 ; prompt-injection attacks demonstrate vulnerability to manipulated inputs 64 ; and general hallucination risks together with cross-query inconsistency in clinical note analysis remain substantial. 65

Accordingly, rigorous validation cannot rely solely on conventional natural language processing (NLP) metrics including ROUGE and BERTScore. A safety-critical evaluation framework must instead integrate four standardized metric domains: (a) task performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for phenotyping classification against structured EHR ground truth; (b) factual consistency metrics: proposition-level precision, recall, and F1-score calculated against source EHR data, alongside hallucination rates quantified via the contradiction-to-context ratio; (c) robustness metrics: consistency analysis across repeated identical queries, semantic entropy for uncertainty quantification, and systematic prompt-sensitivity testing; and (d) clinical utility metrics: impact on clinician workflow, time-to-decision, and non-inferiority to established statistical baselines in prospective head-to-head comparative evaluations. LLM-derived features may only be integrated into hybrid system architectures that delegate final classification decisions to interpretable, clinically validated algorithms after all four metric domains meet pre-specified performance thresholds. 66 This apparent paradox — superior natural language processing capability coexisting with unreliable clinical reasoning — defines the current translational bottleneck for LLM deployment in sepsis phenotyping.

In practice, LLMs process unstructured clinical notes to augment structured EHR features for downstream random forest or XGBoost classifiers, rather than performing autonomous sepsis subtyping.62,66 This hybrid architecture leverages LLMs for natural language understanding while delegating classification decisions to interpretable, validated algorithms with established performance boundaries.20,66 Although LLMs enable multimodal early warning and automated clinical documentation, representing a promising frontier for clinical translation, their deployment requires rigorous hybrid validation with traditional algorithms and clinical expert oversight to mitigate patient safety risks.61,67 When considering their integration into sepsis ICU data analysis, as an emerging unstructured data processing algorithm, LLMs have unique potential for mining ICU clinical text information. However, clinical constraints such as medical jargon ambiguity and real-time computing latency limit their bedside application.20,61,62

4.1.4. Semi-supervised and self-supervised learning

Given the paucity of definitive sepsis labels in ICU data and the high cost of expert annotation, semi-supervised and self-supervised learning address a critical data bottleneck in phenotyping.68,69 Autoencoder models predicted septic shock in acute pancreatitis with high discriminative performance (AUC 0.90 validation, 0.88 test), demonstrating that self-supervised feature learning from unlabeled physiological data can capture latent representations predictive of sepsis outcomes without requiring complete phenotypic labels. 70 When applied to sepsis ICU data analysis, semi-supervised and self-supervised learning perfectly adapt to the sparse labeling dilemma of ICU sepsis datasets. These lightweight algorithms effectively reduce annotation costs, while inherent sampling bias and cross-center distribution drift remain unresolved.55,68

Collectively, contemporary AI-assisted sepsis phenotyping research is undergoing a distinct developmental transition from preliminary proof-of-concept validation toward practical clinical deployment. Each algorithm category presents inherent trade-offs: supervised learning delivers stable classification but relies heavily on high-quality labeled datasets; unsupervised methods facilitate novel subgroup discovery yet lack standardized clinical validation; semi-supervised and self-supervised learning effectively alleviate data scarcity issues while suffering from unavoidable distribution drift; and LLMs excel at unstructured text mining but remain constrained by computational latency and hallucination risks. To bridge the gap between algorithmic prototypes and real-world ICU application, ongoing research prioritizes three core optimization directions: the construction of interpretable model architectures, standardized multicenter external validation, and the improvement of clinical deployment infrastructure.15,71

4.2. Critical care public data sources

Data quality fundamentally determines the reliability and reproducibility of AI-driven sepsis phenotyping.15,30 A critical threat is diagnostic label uncertainty: Sepsis-3 operationalization in EHRs—via billing codes, antibiotic exposure, or composite indicators—introduces misclassification that prospective adjudication rarely corrects. 16 Because deep learning amplifies rather than averages label noise through hierarchical feature transformations, latent subtypes may appear statistically robust yet biologically spurious.

Current research employs three primary data sources selected according to study objectives and validation requirements. (1) Multicenter RCT data from prospective trials provide high internal validity and causal inference through randomization. With low missing rates and comprehensive treatment adherence records, these datasets represent the gold standard for validating subtype-specific treatment responses. Machine learning analysis of CORTICUS trial data identified IFNγ/IL10 ratio as a potential stratification biomarker for hydrocortisone therapy, validated across three independent external cohorts. 30 (2) Observational cohort data derived from EHRs offer flexibility in sepsis definition adaptation, high-frequency longitudinal measurements, and substantially larger sample sizes than RCT, providing robust AI training resources.15,53 However, these datasets require rigorous correction for missing values, temporal practice variations, and unmeasured confounders. (3) Public critical care and multi-omics databases provide accessible training resources. Beyond established repositories (MIMIC series, PIC, NHANES, GEO),72–76 emerging high-quality databases include INSPIRE, SICdb, and ZFPH.77–79 These real-world datasets require standardized quality control before model development. Critically, most public databases lack granular treatment intervention logs, limiting their utility for treatment-response phenotyping—a gap that must be addressed through prospective data collection.5,51

Equally consequential is the profound geographical and institutional concentration of these repositories. The MIMIC series derives predominantly from a single tertiary academic center in Boston, Massachusetts; eICU-CRD, while multicenter, is confined to 208 U.S. hospitals with limited representation of community or safety-net institutions; and AmsterdamUMCdb reflects a single Dutch academic center. Consequently, models trained almost exclusively on these datasets inherit embedded biases: they underrepresent populations with distinct genetic architectures, comorbidity burdens, and healthcare access constraints prevalent in low- and middle-income countries or resource-limited settings. 80 This geographical skew fundamentally compromises global generalizability, as demonstrated by the fact that externally validated models often exhibit performance degradation even when transferred across institutions within the same high-income country. 55 Core characteristics of international public ICU databases are summarized in Table 4.72–74,77,79,81–85Table 4. 4.3. Model validation framework

Model validation determines clinical translatability of AI phenotyping. A meta-analysis of 36 studies comprising 98 sepsis prediction models revealed that translation into practice remains limited by inconsistent evaluation and reporting, and by inadequate external validation. 25 A 5-center Italian study (n=5,344) revealed sepsis detection models with excellent internal performance (AUROC 0.91–0.98) showed significant external performance variation (AUROC 0.75–0.95), highlighting distribution shift challenges even across institutions within the same country. 55 Prospective validation of sepsis prediction models remains scarce; a recent methodological systematic review found that only 2.2% of studies employed prospective data, with prospective external validation being particularly rare, highlighting challenges in evaluating models in contemporaneous real-world cohorts following implementation. 86 These findings underscore that the vast majority of published AI phenotyping studies lack rigorous external validation, contributing to poor reproducibility and clinical stagnation.

Per international AI guidelines, sepsis phenotyping models require three-tiered validation with specific operational standards69,87: (1) Internal validation through resampling stability tests and 5-fold cross-validation, supplemented by temporal validation to avoid future data leakage and assess robustness against temporal distribution shifts25,88; (2) External validation in ≥2 independent geographic regions using different EHR systems and distinct patient populations to ensure generalizability beyond single-institution data distributions69,89; (3) Clinical utility validation through prospective cohort studies, pragmatic cluster-randomized controlled trials (pcRCTs), or silent prospective testing phases, with treatment response difference or process-of-care metrics as the primary endpoint.69,90–92

Reporting Standards and Quality Assessment. Adherence to established reporting guidelines is essential for reproducibility and regulatory review, yet current sepsis phenotyping studies demonstrate variable compliance. Existing frameworks such as the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials–Artificial Intelligence (SPIRIT-AI) extension provide minimum requirements for reporting algorithms in healthcare, emphasizing external and prospective validation as prerequisites for reproducibility. 93 The APPRAISE-AI tool, developed for quantitative evaluation of AI studies for clinical decision support, evaluates methodological and reporting quality across six domains (clinical relevance, data quality, methodological conduct, robustness of results, reporting quality, and reproducibility) and has demonstrated strong correlation with adherence to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement. 94 Application of this tool to 28 sepsis machine learning studies revealed that the five lowest-scoring items included source of data, sample size calculation, bias assessment, error analysis, and transparency, indicating systematic deficiencies in validation reporting. 94 The development of AI-specific reporting standards for interventional studies (CONSORT-AI) and diagnostic/prognostic prediction models (TRIPOD-AI) represents an active area of methodological advancement; however, their adoption in critical care phenotyping research remains limited.94,95 Future sepsis AI studies should incorporate these reporting frameworks from protocol inception to ensure transparent validation and facilitate cross-study comparison.

5. Multidimensional research advances in AI-Assisted sepsis phenotyping

AI has advanced sepsis phenotyping by integrating clinical, genomic, physiological, and immunological data. However, these eight research directions differ markedly in their evidentiary maturity, clinical accessibility, and proximity to therapeutic application. The following sections critically evaluate each dimension through comparative evidence synthesis, identifying shared bottlenecks and divergent trajectories toward precision sepsis care. This chapter further supplements and expands the algorithmic adaptation characteristics of core AI models mentioned in Chapter 4.1, focusing on the practical application performance of different algorithms in multi-dimensional sepsis phenotyping scenarios.

5.1. Molecular endotypes: Transcriptomic and single-cell insights

Building on the molecular granularity outlined in Section 3.2, early transcriptomic work by Maslove et al. applied PAM clustering to neutrophil profiles, identifying inflammatory and TLR signaling subtypes with divergent hydrocortisone responses in the CORTICUS trial. 40 A subsequent meta-analysis by Schaack et al. showed that ML models (random forest, SVM, deep neural networks) achieved classification accuracy >0.96 on microarray data, with deep models retaining the highest resilience after sequential gene ablation. 41 While these results confirm that model architecture shapes biological signal extraction, they also expose a critical tension: Du et al.’s RT-qPCR-compatible model offers a pragmatic balance between molecular depth and bedside access, 32 but synchronizing fixed-time transcriptomic readouts with dynamic immune trajectories remains unresolved. More broadly, transcriptomic endotypes have not demonstrated treatment-specific utility beyond corticosteroid stratification, leaving their clinical value largely theoretical.

Unsupervised clustering has identified transcriptomic subgroups with divergent hydrocortisone responses, 40 yet their clinical utility remains contingent on prospective outcome validation and dynamic systems-level characterization. 18 The core limitation of transcriptomic endotypes is temporal: standard microarray profiling requires 24–48 hours of processing, creating a mismatch between static molecular snapshots and the rapidly evolving acute sepsis state.40,41 This delay conflicts with the 1–3 hour intervention window mandated by the Surviving Sepsis Campaign, 9 meaning results arrive well after the critical resuscitation period. Even optimized RT-qPCR panels still require hours of laboratory work, offering only marginal improvement within this narrow therapeutic window. 32 Consequently, transcriptomic endotypes are currently best suited as retrospective research tools or post-hoc trial stratification biomarkers, rather than real-time inputs for acute bedside phenotyping.

Moving beyond bulk averages, scRNA-seq reveals pathogenic immune subpopulations driving clinical heterogeneity. Lin et al. derived a DNA-damage-response (DDR)-based risk score from peripheral blood scRNA-seq, stratifying patients into high- and low-risk groups with divergent survival. 42 However, the 1–2-day specialized workflow confines it to research centers, clashing with sepsis’s hour-scale therapeutic urgency. Droplet-based 5′ sequencing may accelerate library prep but lacks prospective validation in sepsis. Ghazal et al. further cautioned that static single-timepoint sequencing cannot capture the dynamic bioenergetic and homeostatic drivers of immune trajectories, limiting its diagnostic precision. 43

Given small cohorts and high costs, most scRNA-seq models employ semi-supervised learning.44,45 Yet beyond expense, the need for fresh, high-viability specimens and complex bioinformatics pipelines renders current methods incompatible with acute decision windows. The field must therefore prove that scRNA-seq insights (e.g., HLA-DRlow monocyte programs) offer actionable value beyond what faster, cheaper RT-qPCR or targeted proteomics can deliver within clinically relevant timeframes.

5.2. Clinical and dynamic trajectories: From admission snapshots to evolving physiology

Clinical big-data phenotyping uses routinely available EHR parameters to identify subtypes with divergent outcomes and treatment responses. Unlike clinician-assigned labels, it reduces inter-rater variability but depends on IT infrastructure. 16 The landmark SENECA project applied k-means to 29 variables from 16,552 patients, defining four phenotypes (α–δ) later validated prospectively. 20 Yang et al. 31 confirmed this in 55,169 patients, with α-subtype mortality lowest (10%) and δ highest (41%). However, these studies relied on static admission data, missing evolutionary divergence during ICU stay. Zhang et al. addressed this via latent profile analysis of fluid-resuscitation responses, identifying four phenotypes with distinct fluid tolerance 96 —showing that dynamic treatment-response profiles hold greater therapeutic relevance than baseline stratification alone. A key methodological vulnerability is the susceptibility of k-means and latent profile analysis to imbalanced, noisy, and missing EHR data, which can bias clusters and obscure rare but critical subgroups. Although electronic phenotyping has the largest retrospective evidence base among the eight dimensions,20,31,97 its reliance on admission snapshots versus dynamic responses represents a fundamental design tension that limits therapeutic granularity. This is not merely a technical gap but a conceptual one: clustering static admission data cannot capture sepsis’s evolutionary nature, and the four SENECA phenotypes have never been prospectively linked to differential therapy responses.

Trajectory modeling captures disease evolution that static admission-based subtyping misses. Bhavani et al. used group-based trajectory modeling in 31,466 patients to define four temperature-trajectory subphenotypes (slow-resolution hyperthermia, rapid-resolution hyperthermia, normothermia, hypothermia) with mortality ranging from 3.6% to 14.2%. 34 A 2024 follow-up showed these trajectories mapped to distinct immune profiles: hypothermic patients had the highest mortality but suppressed cytokine levels, whereas hyperthermic subtypes showed robust inflammatory activation. 17 Critically, the rapid-resolution hyperthermic subtype had the lowest mortality despite the highest cytokine levels, challenging the dogma that hyperinflammation universally predicts worse outcomes and underscoring that inflammatory dynamics, not absolute magnitude, determine prognosis. Temperature-trajectory subphenotyping has achieved multicenter validation and immune characterization, placing it at the dynamic phenotyping forefront.17,34 However, it requires specialized modeling techniques distinct from static clustering, and inferences are sensitive to temporal binning strategies—improper binning can obscure critical state transitions.

Beyond temperature, dynamic organ-dysfunction trajectories assess progression and treatment response. Xu et al. used dynamic time warping to cluster 72-hour SOFA trajectories, identifying rapid-deteriorator and rapid-improver phenotypes; rapid deteriorators had the highest ventilation and mortality rates (28.3%). 35 However, SOFA’s ordinal scale lacks granularity, especially for cardiovascular drug nuances. Rajendran et al. addressed this with a two-stage ML approach that first stratified by trajectory, then predicted class, revealing significant corticosteroid-response differences across groups in a target-trial emulation—a key step toward linking dynamic phenotypes to therapy. 51 However, constrained by limited longitudinal sample sizes, organ-trajectory modeling frequently employs semi-supervised training paradigms to enhance generalization, although this introduces risks of distribution shift when models are transferred across institutions with divergent organ-support practices.

5.3. Biological specificity and integration: Biomarkers, multi-omics, and the path to treatment response

Biomarker-guided phenotyping offers high clinical accessibility because most assays can be performed on existing laboratory infrastructure. Masson et al. reported that presepsin yielded an AUC of 0.803 for sepsis diagnosis, and a machine-learning prognostic model incorporating aspartate aminotransferase, activated partial thromboplastin time, urea, procalcitonin, and presepsin achieved an AUC of 0.706 for 30-day mortality. 98 However, single biomarkers have demonstrated limited therapeutic-guidance value. Llitjos et al. emphasized that biomarker-guided interventions such as sTREM-1–guided nangibotide treatment or adrenomedullin-guided enibarcimab treatment appear promising but require further prospective evaluation; current barriers include the absence of strict recommendations for sepsis biomarker development, lack of consensus regarding diagnostic performance thresholds, and methodological validation challenges. 29 Ristori et al. integrated procalcitonin, mid-regional pro-adrenomedullin, and lactate with clinical scores, deriving machine-learning prognostic models that outperformed conventional methods for predicting in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality, with model interpretability validated using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis. 47 These findings suggest that although single biomarkers achieve modest discrimination (AUC 0.70–0.80), machine-learning–integrated panels consistently outperform individual markers, supporting multi-analyte strategies for phenotyping. Dimension-specific limitations include the lack of standardized performance benchmarks for sepsis biomarker panels and the absence of validated point-of-care multiplex platforms that can deliver results within the narrow therapeutic windows required for sepsis bundle compliance.

5.4. Multi-omic integration: Toward mechanistically grounded subtypes

Multi-omic phenotyping integrates transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data to characterize sepsis heterogeneity across biological scales. At the proteomic level, Bracht et al. identified three clinical phenotypes using unsupervised machine learning that corresponded closely with plasma proteomic profiles, demonstrating graded depletion of complement and coagulation factors with increasing phenotypic severity. 36 Ledesma et al. 48 used plasma peptidomics combined with machine learning to discriminate pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory/immunosuppressive states with 95.7% sensitivity and 90.9% specificity. Sardesai et al. 99 employed a biomarker panel with machine learning to achieve 96.64% accuracy in immune-response stratification. These studies demonstrate that biological specificity does not necessarily require full multi-omic profiling: Bracht’s seven-parameter clinical model achieved comparable phenotypic discrimination to Ledesma’s specialized peptidomic assay.36,48 Pinto et al. 100 established a multi-omics pipeline integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data for septic shock with myocardial injury, representing the most comprehensive multi-omic subtyping effort to date. Collectively, these studies establish that multi-omic integration—spanning transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—provides superior mechanistic resolution over single-layer analyses for dissecting organ-specific injury in sepsis.

Dimension-specific technical barriers are substantial. Current implementations face prohibitive costs, complex bioinformatics pipelines, and turnaround times of 7–14 days, rendering them incompatible with acute sepsis decision windows.18,29 High-dimensional multi-omics data contain massive redundant noise; consequently, principal component analysis and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) are universally used for preprocessing. Nevertheless, these unsupervised dimensionality-reduction tools may distort inter-sample distance and produce false subtypes in the presence of missing values or batch effects, which is consistent with the algorithmic limitations discussed in Section 4.1. Bracht et al.’s demonstration that clinical phenotypes can proxy proteomic profiles suggests that dimension selection involves a deliberate trade-off between mechanistic depth and bedside accessibility. 36

5.5. Treatment-response phenotyping: The critical path to precision trials

Treatment-response phenotyping stratifies patients by predicted differential response to specific interventions—such as corticosteroids, antibiotics, or fluid resuscitation—independent of baseline prognostic risk. König et al. 30 used machine learning to screen 137 clinical and immunological variables from the CORTICUS trial, identifying the interferon-γ (IFN-γ)/interleukin-10 (IL-10) ratio as a hydrocortisone-response stratification biomarker: patients with low ratios exhibited significant survival benefits from steroid therapy, validated across three independent cohorts. Rajendran et al. 51 extended this paradigm through multicenter target-trial emulation, stratifying patients by predicted organ-dysfunction trajectory and demonstrating significant corticosteroid-response heterogeneity across trajectory groups. However, these findings stand in contrast to the broader landscape: while IFN-γ/IL-10–guided stratification showed consistent external validation, most other treatment-response phenotyping attempts have failed to replicate across cohorts. This discrepancy arises because treatment-response phenotyping requires randomized-controlled-trial (RCT)–level causal inference; observational data suffer from immortal-time bias and confounding-by-indication, explaining why so few machine-learning biomarkers have achieved multi-cohort validation.30,51 Wendland et al.'s OptAB framework demonstrated that reinforcement learning can optimize antibiotic selection while accounting for nephrotoxicity and hepatotoxicity, achieving faster computational performance than clinician-selected regimens—though this remains an in-silico demonstration lacking prospective clinical validation. 101

Dimension-specific limitations center on data architecture rather than algorithmic performance. The fundamental bottleneck is the scarcity of randomized-trial datasets containing both structured treatment arms and comprehensive biomarker profiling. Large language models are increasingly applied to extract intervention information from unstructured trial texts to expand treatment-response sample libraries, yet hallucination risks and reasoning uncertainty restrict their large-scale application in clinical-trial curation, consistent with the limitations summarized in Section 4.1. The transition from diagnostic and prognostic stratification to therapeutic decision support therefore depends less on algorithmic refinement than on the availability of purpose-designed prospective trials with embedded biomarker collection and adaptive randomization strategies.

6. Current limitations and translational roadmap of AI-Assisted sepsis phenotyping

The field of AI-driven sepsis phenotyping remains largely at the proof-of-concept stage, with few algorithms having achieved guideline-endorsed, widespread routine clinical implementation (Figure 2). A meta-analysis of 98 prediction models identified inconsistent evaluation standards and inadequate external validation as the principal translational bottlenecks, 25 a finding reinforced by a systematic review of 262 pediatric and neonatal ICU studies showing 77% carried high risk of bias and none had been integrated into daily clinical care. 69 These shortcomings are not isolated technical flaws; they stem from four interconnected, clinically consequential barriers that separate algorithmic performance from bedside utility, compounded by broader systemic gaps in regulation, health economics, and data infrastructure.Figure 2. 6.1. The limits of explainability: Transparency does not equal clinical trust

Most explainable AI (XAI) tools developed for sepsis phenotyping are post-hoc add-ons, methodologically disconnected from the causal reasoning clinicians use at the bedside. Deep learning and ensemble models inherently operate as opaque black boxes, 27 and while SHAP and attention-based techniques can quantify retrospective biomarker contributions, 28 they are not embedded into model design to support real-time clinical decision-making. Critically, explainability alone does not drive safe, sustained adoption: a high-fidelity simulation study found that 92% of clinicians rejected clearly unsafe AI recommendations, yet acceptance of dosing modifications depended on bedside nurse concurrence, not the quality of the algorithm’s stated rationale. 102 Layered interpretability frameworks—concise risk flags for time-sensitive acute decisions paired with on-demand granular detail—represent a logical design direction, but their clinical utility remains entirely untested in prospective clinician-in-the-loop trials, rather than being validated solely by retrospective accuracy benchmarks. 103

6.2. Limited generalizability: A structural constraint of current data sources

Models trained on the geographically concentrated repositories described in Section 4.2 consistently degrade when deployed across independent institutions. Performance drops are steepest in community hospitals or resource-limited settings, where laboratory assays, case mix, and care workflows diverge most sharply from high-resource academic centers. 55 This gap is unlikely to be closed by larger sample sizes alone, because the dominant public databases share a systematic selection bias toward high-income, urban, academic populations. 80 Federated learning is widely cited as a mitigation strategy, but existing demonstrations have focused on mortality prediction rather than subtype-specific treatment response, and federated aggregation cannot correct for selection bias in the underlying source populations. 104 Meaningful progress will require prospective data collection that actively incorporates underrepresented populations and care settings.

6.3. From statistical validation to real-world clinical implementation

Most AI phenotyping tools never leave the validation laboratory; they are never embedded into emergency department or ICU clinical workflows. 105 The single greatest barrier to adoption is not model accuracy, but alert fatigue and cognitive overload from poorly integrated alerts. A systematic review of 30 real-world implementation studies identified 14 barriers and 22 decision points, with mistimed, uncontextualized alerts as the leading cause of AI tool abandonment in clinical practice. 105 Real-time EHR data streams also introduce unique noise profiles—intermittent vital sign transmission gaps, laboratory turnaround delays, and order-entry synchronization errors—that differ fundamentally from the clean, batched data extracts used for model training. 53

Silent prospective deployment—in which the algorithm runs invisibly on real-time data to quantify baseline alert rates and data artifacts before any clinician-facing interface is activated—represents the most reliable approach currently available to bridge this gap before clinical rollout. 106 Subsequent cluster-randomized trials, such as a pragmatic trial of a real-time machine learning-assisted sepsis alert in the emergency department, demonstrate the value of randomizing at the unit or clinician level to evaluate real-world effectiveness, including timeliness of interventions under conditions where clinicians retain discretion to act on or disregard AI suggestions. 107 Such designs help capture aspects of clinical adherence and potential alert fatigue that are not evident in retrospective performance metrics alone.

6.4. The treatment-response gap: The missing link to clinical value

A key limitation of current AI phenotyping research is that nearly all data-driven subphenotypes have only been validated for prognostic stratification, not for guiding targeted therapy. The landmark SENECA phenotypes, for example, reliably stratify mortality risk but have not been prospectively validated for differential treatment responses in randomized designs. 20 Similarly, most transcriptomic endotypes are associated with prognostic differences, but none have been prospectively confirmed to predict treatment-specific clinical benefit.40,41

With the exception of the IFN-γ/IL-10 hydrocortisone stratification biomarker validated across the CORTICUS trial and three independent cohorts, 30 very few sepsis phenotypes meet the dual standard of reproducibility across independent cohorts plus demonstrated therapeutic benefit in randomized controlled trials or target-trial emulation designs. 50 Until phenotyping algorithms can reliably guide treatment decisions rather than just predict patient outcomes, their incremental clinical value will remain marginal.

6.5. Systemic barriers: Regulation, economics, and data-sharing constraints

Beyond technical and clinical gaps, broader systemic barriers slow translational progress. AI tools that guide therapeutic stratification qualify as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), yet regulatory approval pathways remain inconsistent across jurisdictions, and liability frameworks for algorithmic recommendations are largely undefined. Safety-constrained reinforcement learning for sepsis resuscitation offers a rare model for generating preclinical safety evidence: by explicitly restricting hazardous action spaces during training, these models can reduce unsafe decisions relative to human clinicians in predefined scenarios, 108 but the vast majority of phenotyping studies lack comparable safety data. Proactive adoption of the IMDRF SaMD risk classification framework can streamline regulatory review, 71 paired with a priori safety-constrained training to generate the empirical evidence required by regulators. 95

Economically, despite promising AUROC values, virtually no AI phenotyping study has incorporated formal cost-effectiveness analyses using metrics such as quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) or incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), leaving hospital administrators without evidence-based procurement criteria. 109 Future trials should embed prospective health economic evaluations aligned with ISPOR guidelines, comparing phenotype-guided care to standard practice across biomarker testing costs, ICU length of stay, antibiotic stewardship outcomes, and adverse event management. Compounding this, cross-institutional phenotyping requires collaborative data sharing, yet patient privacy regulations and incompatible EHR systems create substantial technical and legal barriers. 110 Broader adoption of HL7 FHIR standards will be critical for building scalable data ecosystems, though mapping complex ICU variables such as weight-based vasopressor dosing remains highly resource-intensive. 111

Collectively, these interconnected barriers mean meaningful translational progress will require more than incremental refinements to model architecture. Prioritizing treatment-response validation, prospective real-world testing, and representative multicenter data collection will deliver far greater clinical impact than isolated improvements to algorithmic accuracy.

7.Conclusion

In summary, AI-assisted sepsis phenotyping has evolved into a multidimensional landscape spanning transcriptomic, cellular, clinical, and dynamic trajectory models. Despite these advances, the field remains largely at the proof-of-concept stage, lacking robust prospective validation and integration into routine care. A persistent trade-off between mechanistic granularity and bedside accessibility further challenges clinical translation. Bridging this gap requires standardized external validation, workflow integration, and regulatory clarification.
