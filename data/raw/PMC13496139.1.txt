Purpose

Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs) are rapidly progressive and associated with high mortality. We aimed to develop and externally validate a parsimonious Cox-based model for predicting 28-day all-cause mortality using routinely available admission variables.

Patients and Methods

This two-center retrospective cohort study included 496 adults with NSTIs: 265 in the development cohort and 231 in an independent external validation cohort. Candidate predictors were selected using 10-fold cross-validated least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression. Model performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-index, time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) at days 7, 14, and 28, calibration, Brier scores, decision curve analysis, and 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Static and web-based nomograms were developed.

Results

Platelet count, all-cause circulatory shock, activated partial thromboplastin time, and serum albumin were retained in the final model. The C-index was 0.871 (95% CI, 0.822–0.920) in the development cohort and 0.864 (95% CI, 0.821–0.908) in the external validation cohort. The time-dependent AUCs were 0.914, 0.888, and 0.845 at days 7, 14, and 28 in the development cohort and 0.897, 0.922, and 0.906, respectively, in the external cohort. In external validation, the calibration slope was 0.711; mortality was overpredicted at days 7 and 14, whereas mean calibration was close to ideal at day 28. Exploratory comparisons showed higher observed discrimination than retrospectively reconstructed LRINEC, APACHE II, and SOFA scores, although APACHE II and SOFA were not assessed within their conventional ICU-based windows.

Conclusion

A four-variable Cox model based on routinely available initial clinical data showed good discrimination in both cohorts. Mean calibration was close at the 28-day horizon, but the external calibration slope and early-horizon overprediction indicate that further validation and potential recalibration are needed before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs) are life-threatening acute infectious emergencies.1–3 Characterized by rapid, destructive progression along fascial planes and extensive liquefaction of the subcutaneous tissues, these infections bypass routine antibacterial regimens and demand immediate surgical debridement. NSTIs encompass a heterogeneous anatomical spectrum, including necrotizing fasciitis of the extremities, Fournier’s gangrene of the perineal and perianal regions, and advanced necrotizing cellulitis or myositis. Despite advancements in critical care and multidisciplinary emergency frameworks, mortality routinely exceeds 20% to 30% globally, and survivors frequently suffer from permanent physical disfigurement, limb amputation, and long-term systemic morbidity.4,5 Diagnostic and triaging delays remain major barriers to survival, driven primarily by early clinical presentations that mimic non-necrotizing soft tissue infections such as simple cellulitis or localized abscesses. The acute therapeutic urgency is underscored by clear clinical evidence showing that each cumulative hour of delay between emergency admission and primary surgical intervention exponentially escalates the risk of in-hospital death.6,7

The critical challenge of early risk stratification across the broad presentation of NSTIs has fueled a sustained interest in composite scoring systems. However, the widely utilized Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score was originally designed as a diagnostic screening instrument to distinguish necrotizing from non-necrotizing processes, strictly limiting its clinical utility for survival prognostication.8 Conversely, general physiological indices such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores were derived from highly heterogeneous intensive care populations.9,10 These generic tools integrate non-specific organ dysfunction parameters that frequently fail to capture the unique, accelerated immunocoagulopathy and localized tissue destruction pathways characteristic of NSTIs. Consequently, accumulated empirical data demonstrate that neither score provides sufficient discriminative precision for this specific patient population, reinforcing the clear requirement for predictive models developed specifically for the NSTI spectrum.

Currently available prognostic models for NSTIs retain important methodological limitations. Many rely on small, single-center samples,11–14 use logistic regression models that do not incorporate time-to-event information, and have limited independent external validation, which restricts assessment of transportability across institutional settings. Cox proportional hazards regression represents the standard statistical methodology for survival analysis,15 offering unique advantages over logistic regression by utilizing the entire follow-up interval, effectively handling administratively censored observations, and providing specific survival probability estimates across any designated time horizon. When paired with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regularization,16 this survival framework minimizes model complexity by penalizing non-essential parameters, thereby suppressing the over-fitting and collinearity issues inherent in traditional stepwise selection procedures.

Several NSTI-specific prognostic approaches have nevertheless been reported. The MNF score provides a practical mortality risk score based on routinely available clinical variables,14 whereas Johnson et al showed that admission immunocoagulopathy measures were associated with in-hospital mortality in necrotizing fasciitis.17 These studies support disease-specific prognostication and the biological relevance of coagulation-related markers; however, they did not address the same objective as the present study: development of a parsimonious time-to-event model for a prespecified 28-day endpoint, bootstrap internal validation, independent external validation without coefficient re-estimation, and direct comparison with LRINEC, APACHE II, and SOFA.

Twenty-eight-day all-cause mortality was selected a priori as a fixed four-week endpoint that captures early deaths associated with the acute infection and initial treatment, permits uniform follow-up and censoring across the two cohorts, and is less influenced than in-hospital mortality by differences in length of stay and discharge practices. A 28-day rather than a 30-day horizon was used because it represents a fixed four-week interval and was consistently ascertainable in both centers.

Against this background, we conducted the present two-center study with the following specific objectives: (i) to isolate key baseline admission variables independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with NSTIs via regularised LASSO-Cox regression; (ii) to construct and internally validate a parsimonious multivariable prognostic survival model; (iii) to evaluate model transportability in a geographically distinct external validation cohort without coefficient re-estimation; and (iv) to benchmark the derived model’s time-dependent accuracy directly against LRINEC, APACHE II, and SOFA scores. Visual and interactive web-accessible nomograms were subsequently developed to support bedside use.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Clinical Setting

We undertook a retrospective cohort study with a two-center design. The development cohort comprised consecutive NSTI admissions to a tertiary surgical referral center (Center 1) over a prespecified enrolment window. An independent external validation cohort was assembled from a second institution (Center 2) over a comparable calendar period. Both centers are tertiary hospitals with infectious disease, surgical, and intensive-care services. Routinely collected data were retrieved from electronic medical records, laboratory systems, surgical records, microbiology reports, imaging reports, and discharge or mortality records. The study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement18 and, where applicable, the TRIPOD+AI extension.19

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Baise People’s Hospital (Approval No. LW2026032401). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Baise People’s Hospital. All patient data were de-identified before analysis, and patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Eligibility Criteria

Adults aged ≥18 years who were hospitalised with a confirmed diagnosis of NSTI between January 2017 and March 2026 at Center 1 and between June 2017 and March 2026 at Center 2 were considered eligible. Confirmation required at least one of the following: (a) operative findings consistent with fascial and soft-tissue necrosis; (b) histopathological evidence of fascial or subcutaneous thrombosis and necrosis; (c) positive microbiological cultures from deep fascial specimens; or (d) cross-sectional imaging findings consistent with necrotizing infection.20 A total of 83 patients were excluded (50 from Center 1 and 33 from Center 2): 12 were aged <18 years, 8 did not meet the diagnostic criteria for NSTI, 37 had missing mortality outcome, follow-up time, or essential admission information, and 26 had undergone previous major debridement or prolonged treatment at another hospital before transfer (Figure 1). Primary Outcome

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality within 28 calendar days of hospital admission (day 0). All included patients had an ascertained 28-day vital status. Survival time was measured from hospital admission to death; patients alive at day 28 were administratively censored at 28 days (event status = 0). Patients who died within 28 days had survival time recorded as the number of days from admission to death (event status = 1).

Candidate Predictor Variables

All candidate predictors were measured at hospital admission or within the first 24 hours of hospitalization and were available before ascertainment of the study outcome. The original dataset contained 33 candidate variables. Before outcome modelling, the predictor set was reviewed for deterministic or substantial information redundancy. Total protein was excluded because albumin and globulin were retained separately; direct and indirect bilirubin were excluded because total bilirubin was retained as the representative bilirubin measure; and prothrombin time was excluded because INR was retained as its standardized measure. These decisions were made without reference to univariable associations or the results of the original model.

All remaining 29 non-redundant candidate predictors were entered simultaneously into a LASSO-penalized Cox proportional hazards model without univariable significance-based pre-screening. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to determine the penalty parameter. The one-standard-error penalty value (λ.1se) was selected to obtain a parsimonious model, and predictors with non-zero coefficients at this value were retained for subsequent model fitting. Univariable Cox regression analyses were conducted only for descriptive reporting and were not used for predictor selection.

All 29 non-redundant candidate predictors were entered directly into the LASSO-Cox regression model, irrespective of their univariable associations with 28-day mortality. Composite severity scores, including APACHE II, LRINEC, and SOFA, were recorded solely for comparative performance assessment and were not included in the candidate predictor set. APACHE II and SOFA scores were retrospectively reconstructed using the worst available physiological and laboratory values recorded during the first 24 hours after hospital admission. Standardized values from the first 24 hours after ICU admission were not available for all patients. Therefore, this assessment window differed from the conventional ICU-based application of these scores and may have missed subsequent physiological deterioration, potentially underestimating their severity and prognostic performance. Comparisons with APACHE II and SOFA were consequently regarded as exploratory.

All-cause circulatory shock was defined as a physician-documented diagnosis of acute circulatory shock, irrespective of presumed etiology, during the interval from hospital admission to the earlier of 24 hours or the first surgical debridement. It was not restricted to septic shock. Each potential case was manually verified using admission and progress notes, blood-pressure records, fluid-resuscitation records, and vasopressor administration records. Sepsis was defined separately as a physician-documented diagnosis of sepsis during the same ascertainment period and was verified using the admission and progress notes, intensive-care records, relevant laboratory and organ-function assessments, and discharge diagnoses.

Missing Data Handling

Before model development, missingness was evaluated for all candidate predictors. Variables with more than 15% missing data were excluded unless considered clinically indispensable and accompanied by sensitivity analyses. Variables with less than 15% missingness were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 5) under a missing-at-random assumption; the imputation model included the event indicator and all available candidate predictors.21 The four final predictors (platelet count, all-cause circulatory shock, APTT, and albumin) were completely observed in both cohorts. Therefore, the final multivariable Cox model and its coefficients were estimated using the original non-imputed data. Patients with missing outcome data or insufficient information to confirm the NSTI diagnosis were excluded.

Study Size and Model Complexity

Because this retrospective study used fixed available cohorts, no a priori sample-size calculation determined recruitment. The adequacy of the development sample was assessed retrospectively using the pmsampsize package for time-to-event prediction models and the framework proposed by Riley et al.22 Assuming a Cox–Snell R2 of 0.275, 29 candidate predictor parameters, a target shrinkage factor of 0.90, an event rate of 0.00704 per person-day, a 28-day prediction horizon, and a mean follow-up of 24.668 days, the estimated minimum development sample was 796 participants, including approximately 138 events. The available cohort was smaller than this requirement; therefore, overfitting and predictor-selection instability cannot be excluded despite penalization and bootstrap internal validation.

Assessment of Functional Forms

The functional forms of the continuous predictors retained in the final model, platelet count (PLT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and albumin (ALB), were evaluated using restricted cubic splines with three knots in separate adjusted Cox proportional hazards models. In each analysis, the predictor of interest was represented by a restricted cubic spline, whereas shock status and the other two continuous predictors were included in linear form. Knots were placed at 110.16, 272.00, and 442.60 × 109/L for PLT; 24.82, 30.10, and 36.96 s for APTT; and 20.44, 31.10, and 40.76 g/L for ALB. A three-knot specification was selected to limit model complexity given the number of outcome events. Wald chi-square tests evaluated the overall association and nonlinear component. A P value for nonlinearity below 0.05 was considered evidence against a linear specification.

Statistical Analysis

Univariable Cox regression analyses were performed to describe the unadjusted associations between individual candidate predictors and 28-day mortality. These analyses were not used for predictor pre-screening. All prespecified candidate predictors were entered directly into the 10-fold cross-validated LASSO-Cox regression model, irrespective of their univariable P values.16 The penalty parameter λ was determined by two complementary criteria, the minimum cross-validated partial deviance (λ.min) and the one-standard-error rule (λ.1se), to balance predictive accuracy and parsimony. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was fitted using the LASSO-selected predictors; continuous variables were modelled linearly and binary predictors as indicator variables. Proportional hazards (PH) assumption adherence was verified for each covariate using Schoenfeld residual tests. The linear predictor (risk score) was computed as the sum of each variable’s value multiplied by its estimated β coefficient.

A static bedside nomogram and a web-accessible dynamic nomogram were developed. Discrimination was quantified using Harrell’s C-index15 with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. Time-dependent AUCs at days 7, 14, and 28 were calculated using inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting. Because the comparator scores were retrospectively reconstructed outside their conventional ICU-based assessment framework, comparative analyses were interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of definitive superiority. Calibration was assessed graphically and numerically. Calibration plots compared predicted and observed survival probabilities at days 7, 14, and 28. For numerical external calibration, the original development-model linear predictor was calculated in the external cohort using fixed coefficients without coefficient re-estimation. The overall calibration slope was estimated from a Cox model with the original linear predictor as the sole covariate; a value of 1 indicated ideal calibration. Calibration-in-the-large was estimated separately at each horizon using complementary log-log calibration models with the original predicted complementary log-log risk included as an offset; a value of 0 indicated ideal mean calibration. Kaplan–Meier observed mortality risks, mean predicted mortality risks, and observed-to-expected ratios were calculated at each time point. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis.23 Internal validation used 1,000 bootstrap resamples to obtain optimism-corrected estimates. Patients were dichotomised into low- and high-risk strata at the median linear predictor in the development cohort. The model was applied to Center 2 using coefficients derived exclusively from Center 1, without re-estimation. Analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2, with two-sided α = 0.05.

Functional Forms of Continuous Predictors

Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated significant overall associations of PLT, APTT, and ALB with 28-day mortality. However, no statistically significant departures from linearity were detected for PLT (Poverall = 0.020, Pnonlinearity = 0.181), APTT (Poverall = 0.001, Pnonlinearity = 0.716), or ALB (Poverall = 0.034, Pnonlinearity = 0.128). Therefore, all three continuous predictors were retained as linear terms in the final Cox model (Supplementary Figure S1).

Multivariable Cox Regression Model

The final multivariable Cox model was fitted in 265 patients with 46 deaths. Sample-size adequacy was evaluated using all candidate predictor parameters rather than only the four retained variables. Schoenfeld residual testing showed no evidence of violation of the proportional hazards assumption (global P = 0.28). All four retained variables were independently associated with 28-day mortality (Table 2). Internal Validation

Bootstrap internal validation showed modest optimism. The apparent C-index was 0.871 (Somers’ Dxy = 0.742), and the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.863 (adjusted Dxy = 0.726). The Dxy optimism was 0.016, equivalent to a C-index optimism of 0.008. The bootstrap calibration slope was 0.877, indicating modest coefficient-level overfitting. Time-dependent Brier-score analysis in the development cohort showed lower prediction error than the null model at each horizon: 0.057 versus 0.088 at day 7 (ΔBrier = −0.030, P = 0.002), 0.090 versus 0.123 at day 14 (ΔBrier = −0.033, P = 0.010), and 0.122 versus 0.163 at day 28 (ΔBrier = −0.041, P = 0.027). Calibration curves showed generally close agreement in the development cohort (Figure 5). Decision curve analysis indicated net benefit over the reference strategies across relevant portions of the evaluated threshold ranges (Figure 6). External Validation

Time-dependent Brier-score analysis in the corrected external validation cohort showed no significant difference between the proposed model and the null model at day 7 (0.062 vs 0.057; ΔBrier = 0.006, 95% CI, −0.020 to 0.031; P = 0.677) or day 14 (0.074 vs 0.090; ΔBrier = −0.016, 95% CI, −0.048 to 0.017; P = 0.342). At day 28, however, the proposed model had a significantly lower Brier score than the null model (0.077 vs 0.133; ΔBrier = −0.056, 95% CI, −0.081 to −0.030; P < 0.001). Numerical calibration showed an overall slope of 0.711 (95% CI, 0.564–0.858; P < 0.001 versus 1), indicating predictions that were too extreme. Mortality was overpredicted at days 7 and 14 (O/E ratios, 0.495 and 0.624), whereas mean calibration was close to ideal at day 28: observed mortality was 21.6%, mean predicted mortality was 19.4%, the O/E ratio was 1.113, and CITL was −0.011 (95% CI, −0.347 to 0.325) (Supplementary Table S4). The external calibration findings therefore differed by prediction horizon: early mortality risk was overestimated, while mean 28-day risk was closely aligned. The suboptimal slope indicates that individual predictions remained overly dispersed even when the average 28-day risk was well calibrated (Figure 5). Decision curve analysis indicated net benefit across relevant portions of the evaluated threshold ranges (Figure 6). Application of the frozen development model to the independent external cohort (n = 231; 50 deaths), without coefficient re-estimation or recalibration, yielded a C-index of 0.864 (95% CI, 0.821–0.908). The time-dependent AUCs were 0.897 at day 7, 0.922 at day 14, and 0.906 at day 28, compared with 0.914, 0.888, and 0.845, respectively, in the development cohort (Figure 7). The temporal trajectories of discrimination differed between cohorts. In the development cohort, the time-dependent AUC decreased from 0.914 at day 7 to 0.888 at day 14 and 0.845 at day 28. In the external cohort, it increased from 0.897 at day 7 to 0.922 at day 14 before decreasing slightly to 0.906 at day 28. Because time-dependent AUC uses changing case and control sets at each horizon, differences in event timing and case mix may explain the opposing temporal patterns despite use of identical fixed coefficients.

Comparison with Other Scoring Systems

After verification of the comparator calculations, the time-dependent AUCs of the proposed model were higher than those of the evaluated LRINEC, APACHE II, and SOFA scores in the development cohort (Figure 8 and Supplementary Table S5). The proposed model’s time-dependent AUCs were 0.914, 0.888, and 0.845 at days 7, 14, and 28, respectively, compared with APACHE II (0.771, 0.695, and 0.676), LRINEC (0.739, 0.730, and 0.723), and SOFA (0.688, 0.696, and 0.668). Because APACHE II and SOFA were retrospectively reconstructed using hospital-admission data rather than standardized ICU-based assessment windows, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously and do not establish universal superiority over conventionally calculated scores. Discussion

This two-center study developed and externally validated a Cox-based prognostic model for 28-day mortality in patients with NSTIs. The model incorporated four variables available during the initial assessment—PLT, all-cause circulatory shock, APTT, and serum albumin—and achieved C-indices of 0.871 and 0.864 in the development and external cohorts, respectively. In exploratory comparisons within the development cohort, it showed higher observed discrimination than the evaluated LRINEC, APACHE II, and SOFA scores. The principal contribution is the development of a parsimonious NSTI-specific survival model evaluated in an independent cohort without coefficient re-estimation, rather than a claim of universal superiority over existing scores.

The cohorts differed in several demographic, laboratory, and clinical characteristics, although all-cause circulatory shock and sepsis prevalence were similar between centers. Despite this heterogeneity, discrimination remained good in the external cohort, providing encouraging but preliminary evidence of transportability between two tertiary hospitals in China. Numerical calibration was more nuanced: the external slope was 0.711, mortality was overpredicted at days 7 and 14, and mean calibration was close at day 28 (O/E = 1.113; CITL = −0.011). Prediction-error improvement was most evident at the 28-day horizon. Although the proposed model had a numerically lower Brier score than the null model at day 14, the difference was not statistically significant, and there was no evidence of improvement at day 7. This pattern may partly reflect the relatively small number of deaths accumulated at the earlier time points. These findings support the prognostic value of the model but do not establish broad international generalizability.

Baseline all-cause circulatory shock showed the largest association with mortality in the final model (HR = 6.534). Because the variable was not restricted to septic shock, it should be interpreted as a marker of severe circulatory compromise rather than as evidence for a single mechanism. In patients with NSTIs, acute circulatory failure may reflect septic/distributive, hypovolemic, cardiogenic, or mixed causes; regardless of etiology, its presence identifies a clinically unstable group requiring urgent assessment and management.

Prolonged APTT was independently associated with mortality (HR = 1.025 per second) and may reflect coagulation-system disturbance in severe infection.24,25 Tissue-factor activation, endothelial injury, and consumptive coagulopathy are biologically plausible contributors, but the observational design does not establish a causal pathway. The adjusted association indicates that APTT provided prognostic information beyond the other variables retained in the model.

Lower platelet count was also associated with higher mortality risk. Although the per-unit HR was close to 1, the unrounded coefficient corresponds to an approximately 17% higher hazard for each 50 × 109/L lower platelet count. Higher albumin was associated with lower risk (HR = 0.950 per g/L), equivalent to an approximately 5% lower hazard per 1 g/L increase. These routinely available markers may reflect coagulation consumption, inflammatory burden, and capillary leakage, but their predictive associations should not be interpreted as causal effects.17,25–28

The proposed model had higher observed time-dependent AUCs than LRINEC, APACHE II, and SOFA in the development cohort. However, LRINEC was designed primarily to support diagnosis rather than survival prediction,8,29 and APACHE II and SOFA were reconstructed using a nonstandard hospital-admission assessment window rather than their conventional ICU-based application.9,10 The comparator analyses are therefore exploratory and cohort-specific and do not establish universal superiority of the proposed model.

Methodological strengths include entry of the full non-redundant predictor set into LASSO without univariable significance-based pre-screening, bootstrap internal validation, and external validation of a frozen model without coefficient re-estimation. Nevertheless, the development cohort was smaller than the formal sample-size requirement, the bootstrap calibration slope indicated modest overfitting, and external calibration was imperfect. These features support cautious interpretation of the strong discrimination estimates and the need for prospective model updating and validation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, measurement error, and residual confounding; causal inference should not be made. Sepsis and all-cause circulatory shock were identified from physician documentation and manually verified using available source records. Although identical abstraction and verification procedures were applied at both centers, residual variation in original clinical documentation cannot be excluded. Second, recruitment was determined by the fixed number of eligible patients rather than an a priori prediction-model sample-size calculation. A retrospective assessment based on 29 candidate predictor parameters estimated a requirement of approximately 796 participants and 138 deaths, compared with 265 participants and 46 deaths in the development cohort. LASSO, bootstrap validation, and external validation cannot fully eliminate the resulting risks of predictor-selection instability, coefficient overestimation, and optimistic performance; the limited number of events also reduced precision of spline estimates at distributional extremes. Third, APACHE II and SOFA were reconstructed from the first 24 hours after hospital admission rather than their conventional ICU-based assessment windows, potentially underestimating comparator performance and favoring the proposed model. Fourth, the 2017–2026 enrolment period may have introduced temporal heterogeneity as surgical, antimicrobial, referral, and critical-care practices evolved. Fifth, both cohorts were from tertiary hospitals in China, so the external validation provides preliminary evidence of transportability rather than broad generalizability. Finally, anatomical subclassification was not recorded uniformly, limiting site-specific analyses. Prospective multicenter and multinational validation and formal clinical-impact evaluation are required before routine implementation.

Conclusion

In this two-center retrospective study, a four-variable Cox model based on platelet count, all-cause circulatory shock, APTT, and albumin showed good discrimination for 28-day mortality in both cohorts. Mean external calibration was close at 28 days, although the calibration slope was below 1 and mortality was overpredicted at earlier horizons. The nomogram and web calculator may support individualized risk estimation but should not replace comprehensive clinical assessment. Prospective multicenter validation, model-updating studies, and clinical-impact evaluation are required before routine use.
