Abstract

Kidney diseases represent a major global health burden, with current clinical treatments impeded by short circulatory half-lives, inadequate renal targeting, and the risk of systemic off-target toxicity. Conventional synthetic nanocarriers face critical bottlenecks, including rapid immune clearance and insufficient targeting specificity within the complex renal pathophysiological microenvironment. While biomimetic cell membrane nanoplatforms (BCM-NPs) offer a promising solution, to the best of our knowledge, no previous review has systematically integrated the pathophysiological basis of renal targeting with a membrane-stratified synthesis of BCM-NP design for kidney-targeted therapy and diagnosis. This review first delineates the anatomical basis of the nephron and key pathological targeting sites for renal drug delivery, then systematically summarizes recent advances in BCM-NPs for renal-targeted theranostics. Finally, we critically discuss the major challenges that continue to limit clinical translation, including large-scale manufacturing, membrane source heterogeneity, quality control, biosafety, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and regulatory considerations. This review aims to provide a structured framework for the rational design and future development of BCM-NP-based precision renal theranostics.

Introduction

Kidney disease, which currently ranks as the 12th leading cause of death globally and is projected to become the fifth by 2040, affects approximately 850 million individuals,1 exerts a severe financial toll on public health systems, with annual US Medicare expenditures alone exceeding $130 billion.2 As the core organ responsible for maintaining systemic homeostasis, the kidney possesses a highly specialized functional and anatomical architecture that underpins the heterogeneous spectrum of renal diseases.3,4 Critically, site-specific pathological injury triggers distinct disease cascades, which amplifies the complexity of clinical management and underscores a critical unmet need for targeted therapeutics.5,6 Current clinical management relies on three non-targeted core strategies: pharmacological intervention, renal replacement therapy (RRT),7 and supportive care, all of which fail to match the complexity of renal diseases. Standard drugs, including RAAS inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, and corticosteroids, lack specificity for renal lesions and typically distribute systemically.8,9 While RRT sustains life in end-stage renal disease (ESRD), it cannot reverse pathological damage, and non-pharmacological interventions serve primarily to delay disease progression.8,10 The primary limitation of current treatments is the lack of targeted delivery. Conventional drugs suffer from low renal bioavailability and short half-lives, making it difficult to precisely target specific sites such as the glomerular mesangium or renal tubules.11 Consequently, systemic distribution often leads to off-target adverse effects, which may paradoxically exacerbate disease progression. This mismatch between disease complexity and non-specific treatment creates a critical clinical gap: acute kidney injury (AKI) remains a common fatal inpatient complication due to the lack of tubular-targeted therapeutics,12,13 while diabetic kidney disease (DKD) lacks podocyte-specific or mesangium-targeted interventions, leading to persistent proteinuria and progressive renal decline.14,15 These challenges collectively highlight that precision targeted delivery systems may help address these limitations.16,17

Recent breakthroughs in nanomedicine offer promising solutions for the targeted diagnosis and treatment of kidney diseases.18–20 By tailoring the physicochemical properties of nanocarriers to match the renal anatomical and pathophysiological characteristics, including the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB), efficient renal targeting can be achieved, with significantly enhanced drug accumulation and bioavailability in renal lesions, reduced dosage and systemic side effects.21,22 Despite this potential, conventional synthesized nanoparticles face critical bottlenecks: rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) leads to an extremely short circulatory half-life, and their targeting efficacy is severely constrained by renal pathological structural changes and hemodynamic shear stress in vivo.23,24

To address the above limitations of synthetic nanocarriers, biomimetic cell membrane nanoplatforms (BCM-NPs) have emerged as a promising solution.25,26 This approach leverages natural cell membranes to mimic their native features for nanoparticle modification, enabling the resultant biomimetic nanoparticles to inherit the structural and functional properties of the source cells.27,28 Compared with conventional nanomedicines, these cell membrane-biomimetic carriers effectively overcome key biological delivery barriers, such as immune clearance and non-specific distribution, and show great potential for treating refractory renal diseases such as DKD and renal fibrosis. Based on this, this review first elaborates the anatomical basis of the nephron and key pathological targeting sites for renal drug delivery, then summarizes the latest advances in BCM-NPs for renal drug delivery, clarifying their design principles, targeting mechanisms, therapeutic potential, and clinical translation challenges to guide the development of precision renal theranostics.

Physiological Basis of Renal Targeting: Nephron Anatomy and Pathological Target Sites

Understanding nephron anatomy, physiological barriers, key pathological sites and their interactions in kidney diseases is essential for optimizing delivery carrier design, including tuning particle size, refining surface modifications, and boosting renal targeted delivery efficiency. This knowledge addresses the key limitations of traditional nanocarriers, and is a prerequisite for realizing precise targeted delivery of cell membrane-biomimetic carriers for kidney disease therapy.29,30

Anatomical Structure of the Nephron

As the kidney’s fundamental functional unit, the nephron comprises the glomerulus and renal tubule. The glomerulus comprises capillary loops and Bowman’s capsule, where filtration integrity is maintained by a specialized three-layer barrier of glomerular endothelial cells (GECs), podocytes, and the intervening glomerular basement membrane (GBM), which is structurally and functionally supported by centrally located mesangial cells.31 GECs are flattened and adhere to the inner side of the basement membrane; their cytoplasm contains non-diaphragmed fenestrations (70–100 nm) that allow the passage of small molecules (<400 kDa) while impeding large proteins via surface negative charges. Additionally, these cells secrete vasoactive substances to regulate filtration efficiency.32 Mesangial cells, located between capillary loops, regulate vascular caliber through the contraction of α-smooth muscle actin and participate in GBM repair and phagocytosis.33 The GBM, with a pore size of 2–8 nm, acts as a molecular sieve to block substances >70 kDa; however, the deposition of circulating immune complexes can lead to membrane thickening, thereby impairing filtration. Podocytes, highly differentiated terminal cells located on the outermost layer of the filtration barrier, project into the capsular space. They extend primary processes that branch into secondary foot processes (pedicels), which interdigitate to form filtration slits (4–11 nm). The nephrin-podocin-CD2AP complex constitutes the slit diaphragm (SD), serving as the final barrier for plasma protein filtration by blocking substances >60 kDa and negatively charged small proteins via molecular recognition.34

The renal tubule constitutes the core component of the nephron, extending from the urinary pole of Bowman’s capsule to the collecting duct. Anatomically, it is segmented into the proximal tubule, the thin limb of the loop of Henle, and the distal tubule, ultimately draining urine into the renal pelvis via the collecting duct system. Situated within the cortical labyrinth and medullary rays, the proximal tubule comprises the pars convoluta and pars recta, accounting for 60–70% of the total tubular length. Its epithelium consists of a single layer of cuboidal cells characterized by a dense apical brush border (microvilli), abundant cytoplasmic mitochondria, and extensive lateral interdigitations, features that facilitate efficient reabsorption and substance exchange.35 The thin limb of the loop of Henle connects the straight portions of the proximal and distal tubules. Located in the renal medulla, it is lined by simple squamous epithelium with thin, highly permeable membranes, playing a critical role in urine concentration and dilution. The distal tubule, found in the medullary rays and cortical labyrinth, is subdivided into the pars recta, macula densa, and pars convoluta. Functionally, the pars recta participates in ion transport; the macula densa senses sodium concentration to regulate renin secretion; and the pars convoluta is responsible for the fine-tuning of ion balance and acid-base regulation. Although distinct from the nephron proper, the collecting duct is functionally integral to tubular physiology. Divided into cortical, medullary, and papillary segments, it contains principal cells and intercalated cells, which orchestrate water/sodium reabsorption, potassium secretion, and acid-base homeostasis, respectively. Collectively, the renal tubules form a looped architecture capable of establishing water and salt transport gradients. Furthermore, the high density of proximal tubules within the renal cortex renders them a primary target for nanocarrier accumulation.36

Pathological Target Sites for Precision Renal Drug Delivery

Site-specific pathological injury in distinct renal compartments is the core driver of kidney disease progression, and these injured sites with unique molecular signatures also act as key anchors for the rational design of precision renal drug delivery systems. Glomerulus-Related Pathological Target Sites

Renal Endothelial Cells (RECs)

The glomerular capillary endothelium is highly fenestrated and coated with a rich glycocalyx, contributing to the permselectivity of the GFB and supporting podocyte architecture. Homeostasis of RECs is pivotal for maintaining glomerular structural and functional integrity, preserving an anti-inflammatory and anti-thrombotic milieu, and preventing fibrosis.37 GECs are particularly vulnerable in typical and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, lupus nephritis, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) vasculitis, antibody-mediated rejection, and conditions of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) depletion.38 Furthermore, dysfunction of RECs is a hallmark of common CKD, including diabetic nephropathy and arteriolar nephrosclerosis. Mechanisms such as impaired endothelial repair, endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), and capillary rarefaction drive the fibrotic progression in CKD. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving or restoring glycocalyx integrity, reversing pro-coagulant and pro-inflammatory phenotypes in injured endothelium, and attenuating fibrosis hold promise for improving clinical outcomes in both rare and common renal pathologies.

Mesangial Cells (MCs)

MCs constitute 30–40% of the total glomerular cell population, playing a crucial role in regulating glomerular hemodynamics and preserving the structural integrity of the microvascular bed.39 Mesangial responses are integral to the pathogenesis of immune-mediated glomerular diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE),40 IgA nephropathy, and metabolic disorders such as diabetes. Hyperplasia and activation of MCs are hallmarks of various human glomerulopathies, triggering inflammation and exacerbating the progression of chronic conditions like glomerulonephritis (GN), glomerulosclerosis, and ESRD. Consequently, targeted strategies utilizing nano-delivery platforms typically aim to modulate MC activation and proliferation.

Podocytes

In addition to MCs, podocytes are critical for the maintenance of the GFB.41 These cells firmly adhere to the GBM via α3β1 integrin, a key anchoring protein.42 Cytoskeletal proteins are essential for bridging integrins to SD proteins and maintaining normal podocyte morphology. Representing 21–34% of glomerular cells, podocytes are vulnerable to perturbations in their associated proteins, which can precipitate podocytopathies. These conditions encompass minimal change disease, membranous nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, collapsing glomerulopathy, and DN.23 Podocyte injury is the primary mechanism underlying glomerular proteinuria and drives the progression of glomerular diseases.43 While proteinuria is conventionally managed with immunosuppressants (eg, cyclosporine A, tacrolimus, and corticosteroids like prednisolone), adverse effects often necessitate discontinuation, leading to high rates of disease relapse.44 Thus, targeted drug delivery to podocytes represents a promising therapeutic avenue for podocytopathies.

Tubule-Related Pathological Target Sites

The renal tubule is lined by a monolayer of epithelial cells extending across the proximal tubule, distal tubule, and collecting duct. The proximal tubule constitutes the predominant segment both anatomically and functionally, characterized by high metabolic activity and abundant mitochondria.45 Consequently, TECs are highly vulnerable to diverse insults, including ischemia,46 nephrotoxins,47 sepsis, and immune-mediated processes.48 These insults can precipitate apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, superoxide overproduction, and lipid peroxidation, which is evidenced by elevated malondialdehyde levels, ultimately leading to AKI.49 In the context of IRI, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) released from TECs exacerbates damage by activating TLR4 on non-renal cells; conversely, TLR4 activation on TECs may confer protection via transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, cytokines such as interleukin-24 (IL-24) and interleukin-34 (IL-34), secreted by injured TECs or inflammatory cells, can induce TEC apoptosis and drive the transition from AKI to CKD.50,51 Given these factors, the majority of nano-delivery systems target the proximal tubule, as increasing drug concentrations within these cells can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy against renal fibrosis.

Crosstalk Between Renal Pathological Structures

Renal dysfunction is frequently driven by a vicious cycle of structural injury involving both glomeruli and tubules, a core mechanism underlying CKD progression. This process is characterized by bidirectional injury transmission and complex pathological interactions. On one hand, disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier exposes TECs to serum proteins (eg, complement and cytokines), precipitating tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Chronic exposure leads to tubular atrophy and the formation of “atubular glomeruli”, resulting in irreversible nephron loss. Conversely, tubular injury adversely affects glomeruli via dual mechanisms: altering glomerular filtration rate through impaired reabsorption, and releasing injury mediators such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) that directly damage podocytes and parietal epithelial cells.52 This bidirectional damage is amplified by immune-inflammatory responses. Macrophage infiltration concurrently exacerbates glomerular mesangial hyperplasia and tubulointerstitial fibrosis,53 while activation of the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis intensifies inflammation in both compartments. Furthermore, injured tubular cells continuously trigger inflammation via TLR4 recognition of calcium phosphate crystals, perpetuating a “vicious cycle”.35 TGF-β-mediated fibrotic pathways,54 disruption of structural-functional coupling, and maladaptive tubular repair following AKI further entrench this pathological loop, culminating in sustained nephron loss.55 Consequently, impeding CKD progression requires transcending single-target approaches by synchronously intervening in both glomerular and tubular injury cascades to intercept this pathological crosstalk.

Applications of BCM-NPs in Kidney Diseases

Biomimetic delivery leverages biological components to mimic autologous cells and tissues, recapitulating the native microenvironment for therapeutic effects. Its successful implementation requires a thorough understanding of cell biology, metabolic regulation, and signaling pathways. By integrating the synergistic advantages of natural and synthetic materials, these systems show immense potential for disease management, particularly in drug delivery, targeted therapy, and molecular imaging.21 Conventional “naked” nanoparticles are limited by rapid clearance via the reticuloendothelial and mononuclear phagocyte systems, a challenge effectively addressed by cell membrane coating strategies that enhance targeted delivery while mitigating adverse effects.56 These membrane coatings confer nanoparticles with prolonged circulation, immune evasion, and inflammation-specific targeting. Membranes from diverse natural sources impart unique functionalities, enabling nanoparticles to mimic autologous cells.27 The core functional properties of widely used cell membrane types for kidney-targeted delivery are detailed in this review (Figure 3 and Table 1). With the rapid development of biomimetic nanotechnology, strategies to augment native membrane properties via engineering, chemical modification, and bio-functionalization are gaining traction. Overall, BCM-NPs break through the core bottlenecks of traditional renal drug delivery systems and hold great promise for achieving precise, efficient, and low-toxicity treatment of kidney diseases, with their specific applications discussed in the following sections. Red Blood Cell Membrane

Red blood cells (RBCs) constitute the most ubiquitous cellular component of the circulatory system, possessing a physiological lifespan of approximately 120 days. Their unique membrane architecture, characterized by specific transmembrane proteins embedded within a lipid bilayer, enables unhindered transit through the vasculature and organs, effectively circumventing immune clearance. This property establishes RBCs as an archetypal model for long-circulating drug delivery vehicles.94 Central to this immune evasion is the expression of CD47, a transmembrane glycoprotein that interacts with signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on macrophages. This interaction transmits a “don’t eat me” signal, thereby inhibiting phagocytosis by the MPS.95 While native RBCs excel in prolonging circulation, they lack inherent specificity for inflammatory or neoplastic lesions. The enucleated nature of mature RBCs facilitates the extraction of high-purity membranes with minimal contamination. The prolonged circulation of RBCs stems from a complex synergy of structural stability, mechanical deformability, and metabolic regulation; these traits provide a robust template for biomimetic engineering. Conversely, the clearance of senescent RBCs by splenic antigen-presenting cells offers a distinct pathway for targeted delivery, potentially exploited to modulate immune tolerance or activation.96,97 Consequently, RBC membranes are extensively utilized to coat nanomaterials, conferring enhanced biocompatibility and immune evasion.98

Biomimetic RBC membrane-encapsulated therapeutics hold substantial promise for managing kidney-related metabolic disorders, particularly in CKD where the prevalence of gout and hyperuricemia reaches 60%.99,100 Current enzyme replacement therapies for hyperuricemia are often compromised by rapid clearance and potential immunogenicity. To address this, biomimetic bioreactors such as RBC@SeMSN@Uri have been developed, encapsulating uricase and selenium-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SeMSNs).57 This design not only prolongs enzyme circulation but also facilitates a cascade reaction: degrading uric acid (UA) to allantoin while converting the cytotoxic byproduct hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to water, thereby mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation in gouty arthritis models. Similarly, Ming et al engineered a tandem nanoreactor (Pd-Ru/Uricase@RBC) integrating Uri with catalytic Pd-Ru nanosheets.101 This system creates a self-sustaining “UA-O2 loop”, effectively reducing systemic UA levels while demonstrating superior stability and biosafety.

Leveraging the size-dependent accumulation and immune-evasive properties of RBC membranes, researchers have developed carriers to deliver steroids and immunosuppressants with reduced systemic toxicity. For GN, RBC-camouflaged nanoparticles (RBC-PA-NPs) were designed to passively accumulate in the mesangium via glomerular fenestrations (100–400 nm).58 These carriers significantly enhanced the renal delivery of prednisolone, minimizing systemic adverse effects. In the context of SLE, RBC-coated PLGA nanoparticles loaded with cyclosporine A (CsA-RNPs) demonstrated a 2.63-fold increase in half-life and a 4.87-fold increase in AUC compared to free drug, optimizing therapeutic efficacy.59 Furthermore, for herbicide-induced AKI, epigallocatechin gallate-loaded RBC-mimetic nanoparticles (EGCG-RBCm/NPs) effectively targeted renal tubules, attenuating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling and activation of the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and significantly improving survival rates in diquat-poisoned models.60

To augment specificity, RBC membranes can be engineered with targeting ligands. Tang et al conjugated a kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) targeting peptide (LTH) to RBC extracellular vesicles to deliver siRNA targeting NF-κB p65 and Snai1.61 This approach synchronously silenced inflammatory and fibrotic pathway in injured TECs, effectively blocking the AKI-to-CKD transition. Additionally, He et al developed a ROS-responsive carrier (RBCM@CeO2/TAK-242) for crystal-induced nephropathy.62 By incorporating a thioketal linker and encapsulating cerium oxide nanozymes and a TLR4 inhibitor, this system achieved inflammation-responsive drug release, scavenging ROS and shifting macrophage polarization from an inflammatory M1 to a reparative M2 phenotype.

The physiological clearance mechanism of senescent RBCs has been repurposed for immunomodulatory therapy. In lupus nephritis, hybrid nanovesicles (MPA-R-Lipo) fused from RBC membranes and mycophenolic acid-loaded liposomes were engineered to target costimulatory markers CD40 and CD80 on DCs, inhibiting autoimmune activation and preserving renal function.63 These findings underscore the versatility of RBC-biomimetic strategies in navigating the complex pathological landscape of kidney diseases (Figure 4). To address diverse biomedical requirements, Zhao et al highlighted that hybrid cell membrane-coated biomimetic nanoparticles (HM@BNPs), exemplified by the fusion of RBC membranes with cancer cell membranes, can confer multifaceted functionalities upon nanocarriers, including immune evasion and tumour-specific targeting.102 This strategy holds promise for extrapolation to the precision management of renal pathologies. Furthermore, biomimetic RBC membrane technology has been instrumental in engineering kidney-on-a-chip platforms.103 By recapitulating the physiological and pathological microenvironments of the kidney, these models serve as robust tools for elucidating disease mechanisms and screening potential therapeutics. Notwithstanding the demonstrated advantages of RBC membrane carriers in prolonged circulation and immune escape, limitations regarding drug loading capacity and site-specific targeting necessitate further optimization. Innovative configurations, such as composite systems incorporating magnetic nanoparticles or near-infrared (NIR) dyes, offer novel avenues for enhanced renal targeting.104 Current endeavors are predominantly centered on leveraging RBC-biomimetic nanocarriers to augment drug delivery efficiency and ameliorate renal injury such as CaOx crystal-induced nephropathy via anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms. Future perspectives include the advancement of hybrid membrane engineering, biomechanical mimicry (eg, elastic nanoparticles), and the development of multimodal synergistic therapeutics.105

Macrophage Membrane

Macrophages serve as essential sentinels of the immune system, orchestrating pathogen clearance, tissue repair, and host defense. These cells exhibit intrinsic self-recognition capabilities, primarily mediated by the CD47-SIRPα axis, which prevents autologous phagocytosis. Furthermore, macrophages are equipped with a diverse array of surface receptors, such as TLR4 and scavenger receptors, which enable the active recognition of and response to PAMPs, viral antigens, and inflammatory cytokines.106 Unlike many short-lived immune cells, macrophages possess a prolonged lifespan, often persisting for months. A defining characteristic of macrophages is their phenotypic plasticity: the classically activated M1 phenotype drives inflammation and pathogen elimination, whereas the alternatively activated M2 phenotype facilitates tissue repair, angiogenesis, and the resolution of inflammation. Therapeutic strategies aimed at reprogramming macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 state to a reparative M2 state hold significant potential for restoring immune homeostasis.107 Consequently, macrophage membrane-coated nanocarriers inherit these unique biological properties, offering active homing to inflamed tissues, immune microenvironment modulation, high biocompatibility, and extended circulation half-life.

Macrophage membranes are endowed with a diverse array of surface proteins and receptors that enable active binding to pathological sites. Coating nanozymes or therapeutic agents with these membranes confers active inflammation-targeting capabilities, effectively circumventing intrinsic limitations such as rapid clearance, poor renal specificity, and limited membrane permeability. This strategy is particularly advantageous in managing the cytokine storm and mitochondrial oxidative stress associated with AKI. For instance, engineered macrophage membrane-coated ROS-responsive platinum nanozyme clusters (MM-PtNCs) target renal lesions via surface cytokine receptors and sequester pro-inflammatory cytokines, while the core PtNCs exhibit catalase-like activity to scavenge excess ROS.64 This dual-functionality system effectively mitigates inflammation and oxidative stress, blocking the transition from AKI to CKD. Similarly, biomimetic manganese-based nanozymes (MB@LM) were developed to exploit the inheritance of macrophage integrins, facilitating preferential accumulation in damaged renal tissues overexpressing intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1).70 The manganese core mimics antioxidant enzymes, reducing oxidative stress and inhibiting apoptosis. To address the pharmacokinetic limitations of irisin, Zhang et al encapsulated it within macrophage membrane-coated metal-organic frameworks (MCM@MOF).65 The retention of membrane proteins extended circulation and enhanced targeting to injured kidneys, leveraging irisin’s potential in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis. In another approach, the MPDAD nanosystem, comprising a mesoporous polydopamine (PDA) core loaded with dexamethasone and coated with M2 macrophage membranes, utilized CD206-mediated targeting of the inflammatory endothelium.68 This system achieved renal enrichment, where PDA scavenged ROS to prevent tubular epithelial apoptosis, while simultaneously inhibiting neutrophil infiltration and promoting Treg upregulation and Th17 downregulation. Addressing cisplatin-induced AKI, recent studies utilized M2 membrane-coated nanoparticles (M2FPPF@Cur) delivering ferrostatin-1 and curcumin.67 This platform achieved a tripartite effect: CD206-mediated targeting, ROS-triggered drug release via a 4-PBA/Fuc linker, and the orchestration of anti-ferroptosis mechanisms alongside M1-to-M2 macrophage repolarization. In the context of GN, a pH-sensitive biomimetic nanoparticle (MM/HA-DXM) was designed to synergize hyaluronic acid (HA)-induced macrophage reprogramming with dexamethasone-mediated mesangial cell suppression.66 The endogenous membrane coating facilitated reticuloendothelial system evasion and active homing to inflamed glomeruli (4.33-fold higher accumulation). Subsequent acid-triggered release of payloads inhibited MC proliferation and shifted macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, resulting in a 2.33-fold reduction in proteinuria in Adriamycin-induced GN models. Furthermore, an active “toxin-hunting” paradigm was introduced for diquat poisoning, distinct from passive hemodialysis. This system utilized charge-neutralizing carboxymethyl-α-cyclodextrins (threaded onto PEG5K to form polyrotaxanes) encapsulated within macrophage membranes.69 By targeting the kidney via inflammatory chemotaxis and releasing 4-OI to activate the Nrf2 pathway, this biomimetic system sequestered the toxin, neutralized ROS, and significantly improved survival rates.

Advanced functionalization of macrophage membranes can significantly augment the renal specificity and therapeutic index of nanocarriers while mitigating systemic toxicity. In the context of CaOx nephropathy, engineered biomimetic hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide nanoparticles (KM@M@M NPs) were loaded with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950.72 By coating these particles with M2-polarized macrophage membranes modified with a KIM-1 targeting peptide, they achieved precise delivery to injured renal tubules. This targeted approach effectively abrogated CaOx-induced pyroptosis and renal injury via inhibition of the NLRP3/GSDMD signaling axis, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for kidney stone disease. In SLE and lupus nephritis, dihydroartemisinin (DHA) has emerged as a potent immunomodulator. To enhance its efficacy, an innovative delivery system (CCR2-MM@PEG-PCL/DHA) was developed using macrophage membranes genetically engineered to overexpress C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2).73 Acting as a “decoy”, these nanoparticles sequester the inflammatory chemokine CCL2 within the microenvironment, thereby inhibiting macrophage-mediated inflammation. Furthermore, this system remodels the renal immune milieu by attenuating monocyte infiltration and reprogramming the M1/M2 macrophage balance, significantly ameliorating renal injury in SLE models. The pathogenesis of SLE is also driven by aberrant Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) signaling, which impairs macrophage efferocytosis (the clearance of apoptotic debris), leading to the accumulation of autoantigens and disrupting cellular homeostasis, particularly affecting platelets. Recognizing the limitations of targeting the macrophage-platelet feedback loop in isolation, a dual biomimetic nanosystem was proposed,71 an engineered macrophage membrane-coated nanosponge loaded with a TLR7 inhibitor to target inflammatory sites and sequester pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby restoring defective efferocytosis; and a resting platelet membrane-coated platform loaded with an inhibitor to suppress platelet activation. The synergistic application of these platforms reduced inflammatory infiltration, upregulated Tregs, and dampened immune overactivation. This strategy effectively restored key clinical indicators, alleviating complications such as lupus nephritis and thrombocytopenia. Collectively, these findings elucidate the critical link between TLR7 signaling and hemophagocytic/thrombocytopenic pathology, providing novel insights for the comprehensive management of SLE (Figure 5). Compared with conventional formulations, macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles retain inflammation-responsive receptors and adhesion molecules, enabling immune camouflage and targeting of inflamed tissues.106 Some systems can also absorb pro-inflammatory cytokines or regulate local immune responses. This is especially relevant to kidney disease, where macrophages contribute to AKI, maladaptive repair, AKI-to-CKD progression, and fibrosis. However, their effects vary with cell source, activation state, renal microenvironment, and disease stage.107 Future studies should therefore match membrane phenotype to the indication and assess membrane-protein function, batch consistency, pharmacokinetics, and long-term immune safety.

Platelet Membrane

Platelets are anucleate, megakaryocyte-derived blood cells whose primary physiological role is hemostasis, achieved through spontaneous accumulation at sites of vascular endothelial disruption. Beyond maintaining vascular integrity, platelets serve as key effector cells in inflammatory responses. This functionality is mediated by specific membrane receptors: the integrin family (eg, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa [GPIIb/IIIa]) facilitates adhesion and signaling via heterodimeric conformational changes; CD47 acts as an immunomodulatory “don’t eat me” signal; and P-selectin, stored in α-granules, translocates to the surface upon activation to mediate adhesion via C-type lectin domains.108 Leveraging these properties, platelet membrane-coated biomimetic nanoparticles mimic native platelets, exhibiting natural inflammatory homing, superior immunocompatibility, reduced immunogenicity, and prolonged circulation.

The inherent inflammatory tropism of platelet membranes, driven by receptors like P-selectin, offers a novel paradigm for targeted renal drug delivery, particularly for reducing the systemic toxicity of glucocorticoids. For instance, Zhang et al engineered a lipid nanoemulsion-dexamethasone-platelet hybrid system (LN-DEX@PLT) that employs a “platelet hitchhiking” strategy.74 This system achieved a 2.2-fold increase in glomerular accumulation compared to free drug. Crucially, it attenuated glucocorticoid-induced metabolic toxicity, evidenced by lower fasting blood glucose, suppression of hepatic gluconeogenic enzymes (PEPCK and G-6-Pase), and weight control. Furthermore, LN-DEX@PLT significantly ameliorated renal fibrosis by inhibiting IL-6/TNF-α and p53-p21Cip1-mediated senescence, reducing proteinuria by 81% (Figure 6). Addressing the challenge of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) and its progression to ESRD, a novel core-shell nanoparticle, Pm-GCH, was developed.76 It comprises a glycyrrhizic acid-calcium-hydrocortisone MOF core cloaked in a platelet membrane shell. This design facilitates immune evasion and inflammatory targeting. Upon reaching the acidic microenvironment of inflamed kidneys, the MOF releases its payload: glycyrrhizic acid prevents steroid inactivation and inhibits phospholipase A2 (PLA2) and complement C2 pathways, while hydrocortisone exerts synergistic anti-inflammatory effects. In vivo studies demonstrated that Pm-GCH effectively reversed steroid resistance, reduced proteinuria, and preserved bone microstructure. Additionally, to combat renal fibrosis driven by TGF-β, Fei et al utilized platelet membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles (PMVs@siRNA NPs) to deliver TGF-β1-siRNA.75 Leveraging P-selectin-mediated targeting of damaged microvasculature in unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) and IRI models, this system effectively silenced TGF-β1 expression via the Smad3 pathway, significantly attenuating fibrosis. These findings underscore the potential of platelet-biomimetic systems as translatable, precise interventions for diverse renal pathologies. Multimodal imaging serves not only as a disease assessment tool but also as a “visualization tool” for targeted nanodrug delivery and efficacy verification, enhancing diagnostic sensitivity to prevent or delay disease progression. The construction of “theranostic” biomimetic nanoplatforms relies on biomimetic properties for precise lesion localization and multimodal imaging for real-time tracking of drug delivery, early detection, and dynamic assessment of disease and therapeutic efficacy, avoiding blind administration and ineffective treatment. Regimens can be adjusted on-demand to reduce off-target toxicity and provide personalized precision intervention, significantly enhancing therapeutic safety and efficacy. As mentioned, AKI has diverse etiologies and complex mechanisms; the lack of effective early diagnostic methods often delays treatment. Some researchers have developed biomimetic platelet membrane-coated nanoplatforms focusing on visualizable, non-invasive means to monitor disease progression in real-time, integrating early diagnosis and treatment of AKI. For example, platelet membrane-coated hybrid microbubbles (Pla-MBs) were constructed to target and adhere to injured renal vascular endothelium via platelet integrin receptors (eg, αIIbβ3, also known as GPIIb/IIIa complex), enabling early diagnosis of sepsis-associated AKI (SAKI) via molecular ultrasound imaging. This strategy detected microvascular lesions before abnormalities in biochemical markers like creatinine and BUN appeared, with imaging signal intensity positively correlated with renal platelet adhesion protein expression.77 Similarly, a research team developed a nanosystem named Platelet membrane-ICG-SS31-PLGA (PISP) for early AKI diagnosis.79 Designed to selectively target the kidney utilizing the natural accumulation of platelets at vascular injury sites, it incorporated indocyanine green (ICG) to visualize in vivo distribution for NIR diagnosis of AKI. Additionally, elamipretide (SS31) served as a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant to prevent mitochondrial injury and reduce oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death. Similarly, to overcome the non-targeted distribution and low bioavailability of xenon inhalation therapy, Yang et al developed platelet membrane biomimetic microbubble carriers (Xe-Pla-MBs).78 Intravenously injected, they targeted injured renal endothelium and released xenon upon ultrasound triggering, reducing ischemia-reperfusion-induced renal fibrosis and improving renal function. Xenon reduced protein expression of senescence markers p53 and p16 and downregulated β-galactosidase in TECs. The integrated molecular ultrasound imaging function provided an early theranostic solution for sepsis-induced AKI.

Platelet membranes are distinguished from other membrane sources by their ability to recognize activated endothelium and injured renal microvasculature, which makes them particularly suitable for vascular-targeted delivery and imaging-guided theranostics.109 Nevertheless, this targeting function depends on activation-sensitive adhesion receptors, whose abundance and conformation may be altered during platelet isolation, membrane preparation, or storage.110 As current renal studies mainly involve intravenous administration and short-term evaluation in rodents, future work should verify retention of receptor function while systematically excluding unintended platelet activation, procoagulant activity, and cumulative vascular risks after repeated dosing.

Mesenchymal Stem Cell Membrane

MSCs, adult stem cells with multidirectional differentiation potential, are widely distributed in bone marrow, umbilical cord, and adipose tissue. They express markers such as CD73, CD90, and CD105, mediating anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and immunomodulatory effects via cytokine secretion, exosomes, and direct cell contact.80,111,112 Using biomimetic MSC membranes to coat nanocarriers retains natural targeting and immune evasion properties, addressing issues of low bioavailability, poor renal targeting, and instability of renal therapeutic drugs. Addressing challenges in gene therapy regarding delivery, safety, immunogenicity, and targeting, a study encapsulated Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Phosphatase 5 (MKP5) in Polyethyleneimine (PEI)-modified PLGA nanoparticles, subsequently coated with MSC membranes to produce MKP5@NP-M, introducing a new therapeutic target for DKD fibrosis. The regulatory mechanism of MKP5 is crucial for identifying new diagnostic and therapeutic targets for CKD. Utilizing the natural homing ability of MSC membrane structures to specifically recognize and bind cells in lesioned areas proposes a promising strategy for targeted MKP5 delivery in DKD.80

MSCs possess significant anti-fibrotic capabilities, a core mechanism garnering attention for treating fibrosis in organs like the liver, lung, kidney, and heart. Their anti-fibrotic effects are mainly realized by regulating myofibroblast activation and remodeling the tissue microenvironment via direct paracrine action. However, MSC efficacy in renal fibrosis is limited by short post-infusion duration and large size preventing passage through the pulmonary capillary system. Li et al developed nanoscale membrane particles (MPs) from MSCs and explored their anti-fibrotic activity and immunomodulatory effects in mouse and human renal fibrosis models.81 Results showed that IRI induced TGF-β and Collagen Type I Alpha 1 (COL1A1) expression in mouse kidneys. MP treatment significantly reduced TGF-β mRNA on day 3 and downregulated COL1A1 mRNA and protein on day 7. In kidney organoids, elevated COL1A1 and TGF-β indicated successful fibrosis induction by hypoxia and IL-1β. MP significantly reduced these markers. Additionally, immunohistochemistry showed reduced α-smooth muscle actin (myofibroblast marker). These results suggest MP’s potential for developing renal fibrosis inhibitory therapies. Furthermore, to address in vitro off-target effects and limited bioavailability of MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs), a team employed a donor cell-assisted membrane modification strategy to develop Arginine-Glycine-Aspartate (RGD) peptide-modified MSC-EVs (RGD-MSC-EV). The targeting ability and therapeutic efficacy of RGD-MSC-EV were comprehensively evaluated in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-mediated angiogenesis mechanisms were extensively studied to elucidate how RGD-MSC-EV alleviates renal fibrosis. In experiments, RGD-MSC-EV exhibited superior targeted delivery efficiency, with optimal biodistribution and retention within target tissues. RGD-MSC-EV maintained peritubular capillary endothelial cell viability by transporting microRNA-126-5p (miR-126–5p) and regulating ALKB Homolog 5 (ALKBH5)-mediated m6A modification of SIRT1 (Sirtuin 1), a key regulator of angiogenesis. By activating endothelial cells and promoting microcirculation, this approach restored oxygen metabolic homeostasis, ultimately delaying CKD-associated fibrogenesis.82

MSC-derived cell membranes, membrane particles, and naturally secreted extracellular vesicles share certain parental-cell functions but should not be regarded as equivalent therapeutic products. Their biogenesis, molecular cargo, isolation procedures, dose metrics, and mechanisms of action differ substantially, making direct comparison of the reported anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and anti-fibrotic effects difficult. The next priority is therefore not simply to enhance renal accumulation, but to establish product-specific identity criteria and kidney-relevant potency assays that link each preparation to a defined mechanism of action.113

Other Biological Membranes and Engineered Vesicle Systems

Cell membranes are versatile natural biomaterials. Different types of BCM-NPs can be developed for various therapeutic purposes. Extracting membranes from target tissues utilizes homotypic targeting or allows further artificial modification to enable precise recognition of specific renal cells or interstitial spaces, reducing off-target effects.

Renal Tubular Epithelial Cell Membrane

Renal tubular epithelial cell membranes are particularly suitable for kidney-targeted nanodelivery because they retain surface molecules that support homologous tubular recognition and can be further engineered to enhance injury-site targeting. For example, the STING antagonist C176 was encapsulated in TEC membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles (SNP@TEC).83 SNP@TEC utilized membrane αV integrins for homologous tubular targeting and retained PD-L1 immune checkpoint function. On one hand, it blocked the STING-TBK1-IFN-α axis, promoting M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization; on the other, it competitively inhibited T-cell activation via PD-L1/CD80, driving Th2/Treg differentiation. This simultaneously regulated innate and adaptive immunity in AKI inflammation (Figure 7). Recent research modified TECs with kidney-targeting peptide (KTP) to coat zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 nanoparticles for FGF21 delivery (KMZ@FGF21) in AKI treatment.84 KMZ@FGF21 was effectively internalized by renal cells, exhibiting antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory effects. Results showed specific accumulation in injured kidneys, exerting robust renoprotection. In IRI, mtDNA released from damaged mitochondria exacerbates inflammation and apoptosis by activating the cGAS-STING pathway. A study constructed apoptotic tubular epithelial cell membrane (TM)-coated baicalein-modified copper selenide nanoparticles (CSPBa@TM NPs). Membrane phosphatidylserine (PS) specifically bound to KIM-1 receptors in injured areas for targeted delivery. Simultaneously, it promoted mitophagy and inhibited the cGAS-STING pathway to treat IRI, offering innovative targeted therapy.85 The ICG/CORM-401 nanosystem (IRICP),86 coated with injured renal tubule membranes encapsulating ICG/CORM-401, significantly improved homologous homing efficiency compared to non-targeted carriers. CORM-401 effectively alleviated AKI-induced oxidative stress by scavenging ROS and generating carbon monoxide, thereby inhibiting AKI progression and promoting recovery. ICG enabled non-invasive, radiation-free dynamic tracking of renal drug distribution via dual-mode NIR fluorescence/photoacoustic imaging, providing a high spatiotemporal resolution tool for real-time renal function monitoring. Neutrophil Membrane

Biomimetic neutrophil membranes retain natural inflammatory chemotactic receptors like CXCR1, CXCR2, CD11b, and CD18, endowing nanocarriers with active targeting to renal inflammatory lesions and immunomodulatory capabilities. Novel glucose ligand-modified neutrophil-like cell membrane-coated melanin nanoparticles (Melanin@Glc-NCM) achieved excellent kidney-targeted delivery under NIR irradiation, with good stability over 72 h.87 Furthermore, photothermal therapy results in pregnant rats with diabetic nephropathy showed that Melanin@Glc-NCM targeted glomerular MCs via overexpressed GLUT1. Upon NIR irradiation, it significantly reduced excessive MC proliferation, promoting apoptosis. This significantly improved impaired renal function, offering potential for photothermal therapy in gestational diabetic nephropathy. Oleanolic acid possesses significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties; however, poor water solubility and low bioavailability hinder its clinical application. Fusing oleanolic acid-loaded liposomes with neutrophil membranes generated hybrid liposomes (N-OAL).88 Utilizing neutrophil membrane-mediated biomimetic chemotaxis to specifically target injured TECs enhanced N-OAL accumulation and retention at AKI-associated inflammatory sites. Optimized N-OAL exhibited spherical morphology. It showed good sustained release, stability, and biocompatibility. In vitro, N-OAL effectively attenuated H2O2-induced intracellular ROS production and inflammation, exhibiting superior antioxidant and anti-apoptotic properties. In vivo results confirmed significant protection against AKI-induced oxidative renal tissue injury. Overall, endogenous CO vasodilates, suppresses inflammation, and acts as an antioxidant, but exogenous gas supplementation is difficult to dose and prone to toxicity. A study loaded manganese carbonyl (MnCO) onto hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (hMSN) and coated them with neutrophil membranes modified with PS insertion (MnCO@hMSN@NM-PS),89 thereby constructing a H2O2-responsive nanodrug for CO therapy in AKI. Through biomimetic chemotaxis to inflammation sites and active binding of PS to KIM-1 overexpressed on injured TECs in AKI tubules, the drug showed good protection against in vitro tubular oxidative injury and glycerol-induced in vivo AKI.

Beyond direct neutrophil-membrane coating, hybridization and ligand engineering of extracellular vesicles provide complementary strategies for improving circulation, injury-site recognition, and reparative efficacy. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived small extracellular vesicles (hucMSC-sEVs) face challenges in tissue repair, including short circulation, insufficient targeting, and low efficacy. Engineered hybrid vesicles fused from human neutrophil membranes and hucMSC-sEVs were named neutrophil membrane-engineered hucMSC-sEVs (NEX).90 NEX significantly enhanced hucMSC-sEV targeting to injured renal tissue, improved impaired renal function by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, promoted renal cell proliferation, and inhibited renal apoptosis in vivo. Additionally, NEX enhanced hucMSC-sEV uptake by NRK52E cells but inhibited uptake by RAW264.7 cells. NEX administration reduced oxidative stress and promoted proliferation of cisplatin-treated NRK52E cells in vitro (Figure 8). A study identified P-selectin as a biomarker for ischemic AKI in endothelial cells and developed P-selectin Binding Peptide (PBP)-engineered extracellular vesicles (PBP-EV) with imaging and therapeutic functions.114 Results showed PBP-EVs exhibited selective targeting to injured kidneys while quantifying renal P-selectin expression via molecular imaging, providing spatiotemporal information for early AKI diagnosis. Meanwhile, PBP-EVs demonstrated superior renoprotection in promoting repair and inhibiting fibrosis by attenuating inflammatory infiltration, improving reparative angiogenesis, and ameliorating maladaptive renal parenchymal repair. Bacterial Outer Membrane Vesicles

Bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are nanoscale vesicles (approx. 20–250 nm) derived from gram-negative bacteria, containing most outer membrane components, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), phospholipids, and outer membrane proteins. PAMPs on their surface interact specifically with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on immune cells to initiate dendritic cell maturation and promote antigen presentation. This can be exploited to enhance biomimetic drug immunogenicity and cancer cell killing. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy is an emerging strategy for RCC. However, clinical efficacy is limited by inherent poor immunogenicity and insufficient cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) infiltration. Wu et al designed biomimetic copper nanozymes (Cu2O-OMV) by integrating OMVs with cuprous oxide nanoparticles for enhanced RCC immunotherapy. The carrier synergistically enhanced ICB efficacy via dual mechanisms: Cu⁺-mediated Fenton-like reactions generated ROS (·OH) for direct tumor killing and induced cuproptosis (aggregation of mitochondrial lipoylated proteins and ferredoxin 1 depletion). LPS carried by OMVs triggered tumour cell pyroptosis via the non-canonical inflammasome pathway, releasing inflammatory cytokines. Together, they amplified immunogenic cell death, promoted DC maturation and CTL infiltration, and significantly inhibited tumour metastasis when combined with αPD-L1 antibody.91

Cancer Cell Membrane

Cancer cells possess infinite replication potential and are easily genetically modified to provide abundant standardized membranes for manufacturing cancer membrane-coated nanoparticles. Tumour cells exhibit potent immunosuppression and stealth capabilities by upregulating immunosuppressive ligands and “don’t eat me” receptors.115 However, metastasis of viable tumour cells poses potential threats. Tumour cells are inactivated during membrane preparation, with nuclear and intracellular proteins removed to ensure safe and effective utilization of their immunosuppressive properties.116 Accordingly, cancer cell membrane-coated biomimetic nanocarriers can be developed for treating renal autoimmune diseases and RCC, utilizing the latter’s homotypic tumour targeting. Research identified PD-1+ and PD-1+ TIGIT+ CD4+ T cells as aberrantly activated CD4+ T cells in SLE. A team prepared IFN-γ-treated MHC Class I-deficient cancer membrane-coated nanoparticles loaded with dexamethasone. The coating was derived from MHC Class I-deficient 4T1 cells expressing high levels of PD-L1 and CD155. MHC I deficiency rendered MNPs less immunogenic to avoid allogeneic rejection or unnecessary immune stimulation. Dual “don’t eat me” receptors CD47 and CD24 on IM-MNPs/DXM minimized immune clearance. IM-MNPs/DXM preferentially targeted inflamed organs via adhesion protein CD44 binding to HA, which is highly expressed at inflammatory sites. Ligands PD-L1 and CD155 specifically bound pathogenic CD4+ T cells to regulate the effector T cell/Treg balance. IM-MNPs/DXM sustainedly released DXM in the inflammatory microenvironment, synergistically promoting Treg mediation to safely utilize tumour cell immunosuppression for SLE therapy.92 A downregulated piRNA in RCC, piR-hsa-28489 (designated piR-RCC), was identified to impede RCC progression in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistically, piR-RCC directly interacted with Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX-1), hindering p-AKT-mediated YBX-1 phosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation. Furthermore, YBX-1 acts as a repressor coordinating ETS Homologous Factor (EHF) transcription. Thus, piR-RCC enhanced EHF expression, inhibiting RCC proliferation and metastasis (Figure 9). Based on this, a biomimetic nanoparticle platform was constructed for RCC-specific piR-RCC delivery.93 Nanoparticles made with cancer cell membrane coatings encapsulated piR-RCC plasmids and delivered them to orthotopic kidney implants in mice, impeding RCC progression. This study elucidated the piR-RCC/YBX-1/EHF axis in RCC, providing a promising therapeutic avenue with homotypic tumour targeting capabilities. The membrane sources and engineered vesicle systems grouped in this subsection do not represent a single therapeutic class and should be evaluated according to distinct design rationales. Renal tubular epithelial cell membranes exploit injury-associated or homologous recognition, neutrophil membranes reproduce inflammatory trafficking, hybrid and ligand-engineered vesicles combine complementary homing and reparative functions, bacterial OMVs intentionally amplify innate immune activation, whereas cancer cell membranes rely on homotypic recognition and immunoregulatory ligands. Accordingly, future development should use indication-matched comparisons and membrane-specific safety criteria, including validation of disease-dependent target expression for renal-cell membranes, off-target inflammatory trafficking for neutrophil membranes, fusion consistency and component-ratio control for hybrid systems, endotoxin attenuation for OMVs, and rigorous control of residual tumour-derived material for cancer-cell membranes.117,118

Translational Challenges and Critical Appraisal of Current Evidence

Although biomimetic cell membrane-coated nanoplatforms have demonstrated encouraging preclinical potential for renal-targeted drug delivery and theranostics, the current evidence base remains dominated by in vitro experiments and small-animal disease models.11,119–122 Across the studies summarized in this review, substantial heterogeneity exists in animal species, sample size, disease induction method, dosing route, dose level, treatment frequency, observation window, and renal outcome measures. Therefore, improvements in renal accumulation, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, inflammatory cytokines, fibrotic markers, or tumour suppression should be interpreted as proof-of-concept findings rather than direct evidence of clinical efficacy.

Standardized reporting remains insufficient in several key aspects of preclinical nanomedicine evaluation, including pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, renal clearance, immune activation, dose-response relationships, and long-term retention or toxicity.119,122 Future renal BCM-NP studies should therefore report not only therapeutic outcomes but also key experimental variables that determine translatability, including species, sex, sample size, administration route, dose, treatment schedule, follow-up duration, and predefined renal functional and histological endpoints.

Engineering, Regulatory, and Human-Relevant Evaluation Strategies

Although protocols vary with membrane source and nanoparticle core, BCM-NP fabrication generally involves membrane extraction and purification, vesicle formation, core preparation, membrane–core fusion, and post-coating verification.120 Membrane isolation should remove intracellular components while preserving membrane integrity and functional surface proteins. Anucleate cells, such as mature erythrocytes and platelets, can usually be processed by hypotonic lysis or repeated freeze–thaw treatment followed by differential centrifugation, whereas nucleated cells require mechanical disruption and density-gradient centrifugation to remove nuclei, organelles, and cytoplasmic contaminants.123 Purified membrane vesicles are commonly fused with preformed cores through extrusion, sonication, or microfluidic electroporation. Extrusion is reproducible but labor-intensive and difficult to scale; sonication is simple but may cause particle heterogeneity and thermal protein damage; and microfluidic electroporation improves coating efficiency and particle uniformity but requires specialized equipment and standardization.124 Coating quality should then be assessed by core–shell imaging, particle-size and surface-charge measurements, membrane-protein profiling, and membrane-specific marker detection. Coating integrity and preservation of membrane functions should also be verified because they directly affect targeting, biological interactions, manufacturing reproducibility, and clinical translation.125

Building on the membrane preparation and post-coating verification framework described above, regulatory and manufacturing issues should be considered from early preclinical development rather than after therapeutic efficacy has already been established. Peer-reviewed reviews of nanotherapeutics emphasize that clinically compliant production requires approval of production methods, assurance of final product quality, and implementation of GMP-compatible manufacturing principles.126 Nanomedicine translation also requires early identification of critical quality attributes, suitable analytical methods, key process parameters, adequate preclinical models, batch-to-batch consistency, and scalable manufacturing strategies.127 Recent formulation and manufacturing reviews further support the use of quality-by-design and quality risk management principles to improve the reproducibility and clinical translatability of nanomedicine products.128 These considerations support a quality-by-design strategy with standardized CMC/QC parameters and early regulatory consultation during renal BCM-NP development.

Beyond manufacturing and regulatory readiness, human-relevant evaluation systems may further improve translational predictability. AI- and machine learning-assisted formulation design may help prioritize candidate formulations, but should remain decision-support tools requiring high-quality datasets and experimental validation.129,130 Kidney-on-a-chip and renal organoid systems may complement animal models by assessing tubular uptake, endothelial adhesion, nephrotoxicity, immune-cell interactions, and barrier transport under controlled microphysiological conditions.131,132 To improve transparency and comparability, a concise reporting and validation checklist for future renal BCM-NP studies is summarized in Table 2. Conclusions

In conclusion, biomimetic cell membrane-coated nanoplatforms provide a flexible strategy for renal-targeted drug delivery and theranostics by combining the biological recognition and targeting properties of cell membranes with the tunable loading and functional capacity of nanoparticle cores. These systems may enhance drug accumulation at renal lesions, reduce systemic exposure, and modulate key pathological processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, fibrosis, and tumor progression. However, the field remains predominantly preclinical, and reported therapeutic effects should be interpreted in light of model selection, dosing strategy, follow-up duration, endpoint definition, and reproducibility.

Future development should shift from simply expanding membrane sources or multifunctional designs toward reproducible, biodegradable, scalable, and regulatorily tractable platforms. Standardized formulation characterization, safety evaluation, clinically relevant models, human-relevant validation systems, and early regulatory consultation will be essential for improving the translational reliability of renal BCM-NPs.
