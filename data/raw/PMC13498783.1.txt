Background

Medication errors contribute significantly to patient harm and healthcare costs worldwide. However, there is limited evidence on their prevalence and characteristics in Cameroon.

Objective

To estimate the prevalence, types, and associated factors of medication errors in a tertiary hospital and identify targets for safety interventions in Cameroon.

Design

This study adopted a hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional design to examine medication errors in a tertiary hospital in the Northwest Region of Cameroon.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study reviewed the medical records of 1,360 randomly selected patients from multiple wards and the outpatient department over six months (January–July 2023). Medication errors was defined using the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error and Prevention framework, encompassing prescribing, dispensing, administration, and monitoring. Descriptive statistics characterises the prevalence, types, and associated factors of errors.

Results

The prevalence of hospital-based medication errors was (44.5% = 606/1360). Prescription errors accounted for (21.7% = 295/1360), administration errors 15.5% = 211/1360), and transcription errors (2.2% = 30/1360). Among administration errors, overdose predominated (12.8% = 174/1360), while underdose and wrong drug administration each represented (1.8% = 25/1360). Critical gaps emerged in managing patients with multiple comorbidities (59.4% = 808/1360) and unaddressed potential drug–drug interactions (40% = 544/1360). System-level factors included staff negligence (14.2% = 193/1360) and open-ended prescriptions (11.4% = 155/1360). Notably, perceived work overload and peak hour pressures were not identified as associated factors (0.0%). Ward-specific error rates are presented in the main text results section with breakdown by Men’s Ward, Women’s Ward, Surgical Ward, Ulcer Ward, and OPD/Pharmacy.

Conclusion

Medication errors are highly prevalent and are driven primarily by system-level deficiencies and clinical practice gaps rather than workload pressures. Urgent interventions are needed, including comprehensive medication management services, improved documentation, enhanced staff training, and structured medication safety policies to reduce medication errors and improving patient safety.

Introduction

Medication errors are a major global public health concern, contributing significantly to patient morbidity, mortality, and increased healthcare costs. 1 The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that medication errors cause at least one death daily and injure approximately 1.3 million individuals annually in the United States alone 2,3​. Annually, 7000 mortalities have been reported due to medication errors in India. 4 A study conducted in Australia shows that 250,000 admissions in the Australian hospital were due to medication errors and cost 1.4 billion Australian dollars to the health systems. 5 It can also be associated with death, increasing the length of hospitalisation in patients, 6 reducing the confidence they have in health systems and reducing productivity, 7 increasing poor quality of life, and an increase in health care-associated costs. 8 A study conducted in the United Kingdom in 2018 reveals that about 2 million people suffer the effects of medication errors, with close to 100,000 dying every year. 1 Globally, medication errors result in an estimated economic burden of $42 billion per year3,6 ​​. In hospital settings, the burden is substantial, with medication administration errors reported to occur in up to 25.2% of total opportunities for errors among hospitalised adults​. 9 In outpatient and ambulatory settings, medication error rates range from 23% to 92% of prescribed medications, with prescribing errors and dosing mistakes being the most prevalent 7,10​. Despite global efforts to address medication safety, the persistence of high error rates underscores the urgent need for continuous, context-specific investigations. 3

In Cameroon, there is a paucity of published data regarding the burden, nature, and associated factors of medication errors in healthcare facilities.11,12 In addressing the public health problem of medication errors in Cameroon, the focus has been to estimate the prevalence of medication errors, the nature of the errors, and the impact of the errors. 4 A critical review of published studies between 1995 to 2020 revealed that the prevalence was 0 – 89.7%. This conclusion was reached within the context of poor-quality medicines in Cameroon. 13 In 2020, a study on medical errors in Cameroon with the desire to push for litigation by sustaining evidence of health provider liability, and this has resulted in many litigations and defense or documentation challenges for fear of litigation. 4 In 2023, another study on medication and transcription errors in a faith-based hospital in Cameroon was conducted, around nursing practice using an observational approach to associate medication and transcription errors with different processes and levels of nursing care. 11 The approach of litigation or addressing the issue around a particular group of health practitioners might be a limited approach to the problem and may introduce data bias that inflates or deflates the prevalence. From this understanding, this study adopted a hospital-based approach using a retrospective cross-sectional design to study the issue of medication errors in a tertiary hospital in Cameroon. Given the high global prevalence and the severe consequences associated with medication errors, there is an imperative to generate localized evidence that can inform targeted interventions.14,15 Understanding the patterns and root causes of medication errors in Cameroon is essential to design and implement effective patient safety strategies and to strengthen medication use systems in line with the WHO Global Patient Safety Challenge: Medication Without Harm. 16 This study aims to estimate the hospital-based prevalence, types, and associated factors of medication errors in a tertiary faith-based hospital in Cameroon. It seeks to provide evidence-based recommendations to reduce medication errors, improve patient safety, and support health system strengthening.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional design to examine medication errors in a tertiary hospital in the Northwest Region of Cameroon. Patient medical records from January 1 to July 31, 2023, were reviewed across multiple inpatient wards and the outpatient department/pharmacy. The selection of patient records used simple random sampling. The subsequent systematic review of each selected record using a structured extraction form constitutes the data extraction process, not the sampling method. The scientific structure of this study is assessed and conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Checklist 17 as presented in Appendix 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patient records within the study period: All patient medical records from January 1 to July 31, 2023 were eligible for review.

Adult patients (≥18 years): The study population was restricted to adult patients, as reflected in the demographic profile (age range captured from 20 years and above, mean age 44.2 years, SD = 18.4).

Inpatient ward admissions: Records from the Men’s Ward, Women’s Ward, Surgical Ward, and Ulcer/Wound Care Ward were included.

Outpatient department (OPD)/Pharmacy visits: Outpatient prescriptions reviewed at the OPD/Pharmacy were included, provided they fell within the study period.

Unique patient encounters: One chart per unique patient encounter was included — either one inpatient admission or one outpatient visit. No patient had multiple charts included.

Receivable records: All eligible patient charts were assigned sequential numbers and selected via a computer-generated random number list (SPSS version 25.0), ensuring all charts within the defined wards and period had an equal probability of selection.

Exclusion criteria

Paediatric patients: Charts from the Paediatric ward were excluded. The study population was restricted to adults, and the Paediatric ward was not part of the sampling frame. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Obstetrics and Gynaecology (OB/GYN) patients: Records from the Obstetrics and Gynaecology ward were excluded. This ward was not included in the study setting, also acknowledged as a limitation.

Duplicate patient encounters: If a patient had more than one chart in the system during the study period, only one unique encounter per patient was included to avoid double-counting.

Records outside the study period: Patient charts with admission or visit dates outside January 1 – July 31, 2023 were not eligible.

Incomplete or missing records: Although not explicitly stated as a formal exclusion criterion, the structured data extraction process and the inter-rater review protocol imply that records that could not be assessed using the data collection checklist (e.g., entirely illegible or missing charts) would not have contributed usable data.

Study population, sample size, and sampling

Using Cochran’s sample size formula (95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, estimated proportion p = 0.20), a minimum of 1,183 patient records was required. Accounting for a 20% increase based on the estimated prevalence of medication transcription errors (28.6%) reported by Ntani et al. (2024), 11 the final target was 1,360 patient records. Simple random sampling was employed: all eligible patient charts within the study period were assigned sequential numbers, and records were selected using a computer-generated random number list (SPSS version 25.0). The study population included adult patients from the men’s ward, women’s ward, ulcer/wound care ward, surgical ward, and outpatient department/pharmacy. Paediatric and obstetrics/gynaecology wards were not included, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Definition of medication errors and denominators

Medication errors were defined using the NCC MERP framework (National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention, 2001), encompassing prescribing, order communication, product labelling, packaging and nomenclature, compounding, dispensing, administration, education, monitoring, and use. 2 For this study, one chart corresponds to one unique patient encounter (one inpatient admission or one outpatient visit). No patient had multiple charts included. The denominator for overall medication error prevalence is 1,360 patient charts. A chart was counted as having a medication error if at least one error of any type was identified during review. Type-specific error rates (e.g., prescription, administration, transcription) are reported as percentages of a total of 1060 for inpatient reviews and 1,360 of the overall totals of charts reviewed, or a percentage of 300 OPD reviewed charts and 1,360 of the overall totals of charts reviewed. The denominator for outpatient OPD errors is 300 OPD patient charts reviewed. The denominator for the associated factors analysis is 1,360 charts. Outpatient prescriptions as well as the inpatient wards’ prescriptions of the tertiary hospital, operated a hybrid documentation model during the study period. Physicians entered orders electronically; however, nursing staff were required to transcribe medication orders from printed or handwritten physician order sheets onto paper-based medication administration records (MARs) and nursing medication cards. Transcription errors therefore arose at this manual transfer step from the physician’s written/printed order to the nursing medication card or administration record and not from the EMR itself. This hybrid workflow is common in resource-limited tertiary hospitals in sub-Saharan Africa and explains the coexistence of electronic prescribing and transcription errors in this study.

Data collection tool and orderliness assessment

A structured data extraction checklist was developed and validated by the research team based on standard clinical audit frameworks used in resource-limited settings. The checklist captured: (i) patient demographics; (ii) medication orders (drug name, dose, route, frequency, duration); (iii) type and frequency of medication errors at each stage (prescribing, transcription, dispensing, administration); and (iv) chart orderliness indicators, including proper filing, legibility of entries, correct patient identification, completeness of laboratory documentation, and alignment of the action plan with the diagnosis. These orderliness criteria were pre-specified in the checklist before data collection began. All checklist items were assessed from the chart record by the same two reviewers who assessed medication errors. The data collection tool is provided in full as the Appendix.

Data collection and reviewer process

A total of 1,360 patient records were reviewed systematically using the structured checklist. The two reviewers the lead pharmacist-researcher (PharmD, with clinical research training) and a trained pharmacist technician assessed all charts independently. Discrepancies were identified and resolved through consensus discussion. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated on a random 10% subsample of charts (n = 136); Cohen’s kappa was κ = 0.82, indicating strong agreement.

The associated factors reported in Table 8 were identified exclusively through structured retrospective chart review by the research team, not from spontaneous adverse event reports submitted by staff. For each identified medication error, reviewers applied pre-defined checklist criteria to classify plausible associated factors. For example, “staff negligence” was operationalized as the absence of a required verification signature, undocumented medication administration, or unexplained deviation from a prescribed order. “Inadequate knowledge” was inferred from evidence of irrational prescribing (e.g., contraindicated drug combinations) or failure to assess drug–drug interactions. The near-zero rate of formally documented errors on medication sheets (0.0%) confirmed that healthcare workers themselves did not report errors; all error identification was performed by the research team through independent audit.

Although outpatient prescriptions were managed through an electronic medical record (EMR) system, inpatient wards operated a hybrid documentation model: physicians entered orders electronically, but nursing staff transcribed orders from printed/handwritten physician order sheets onto paper medication administration records (MARs) and nursing medication cards. Transcription errors arose at this manual transfer step. (See Appendix 1).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages, standardised to one decimal place) were used to summarise the prevalence, types, and associated factors of medication errors. Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS version 25.0. As this study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design, no inferential statistical tests (e.g., chi-square, regression) were performed. The findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, identifying priority areas for future analytical and interventional studies.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Summary of patient characteristics

The table presents the demographic and clinical profile of a study sample comprising 1,360 patients (derived from combining female and male counts: 740 + 620).

Sex distribution

The sample is slightly female-predominant, with women accounting for 54.4% (n = 740) and men making up 45.6% (n = 620). This near-even split suggests the findings may be broadly generalizable across both sexes.

Age profile

The mean age was 44.2 years (SD = 18.4), indicating a middle-aged adult population with considerable variability. The largest age group was 30–40 years (34.4%), followed by 50–60 years (17.4%) and 40–50 years (16.5%). Younger adults (20–30 years) represented 13.2% of the sample, while those aged 60 and above collectively comprised 18.5% (8.3% + 10.2%). The relatively wide standard deviation reflects the broad age range captured across the cohort.

Comorbidity burden

A notably high proportion of patients 59.4% (n = 808) presented with two or more comorbidities. This majority signals a clinically complex population in which multimorbidity is the norm rather than the exception, with important implications for treatment planning and medication management.

Polypharmacy

The number of drugs prescribed per patient ranged from 1 to 13, with a median of 4. The wide range indicates significant heterogeneity in treatment complexity. Combined with the high comorbidity burden, this raises important considerations around drug interactions, adherence, and medication safety, particularly for older patients or those with multiple chronic conditions.

Overall impression

The cohort represents a diverse, predominantly middle-aged adult population with a substantial burden of multimorbidity and moderate-to-high polypharmacy. Discussion

This study revealed a medication error prevalence of 46.5% across inpatient and outpatient settings, highlighting a significant patient safety concern. This rate is relatively high compared to global estimates; a systematic review and meta-analysis of hospitalised adults reported a pooled medication administration error rate of 19.6%–25.2%. 7 The lower rates in those studies may partly reflect more robust medication safety systems in high-income settings.

In outpatient and ambulatory settings, error rates have been reported to range from 23% to 92%, 17 consistent with our observed outpatient prevalence of 43.3%. Assiri et al. (2018) found prescribing errors ranging from 2% to 94% and identified drug–drug interactions, polypharmacy, and multimorbidity as common risk factors 18 closely mirroring our finding that multiple comorbidities (59.4%) and unaddressed drug–drug interactions (40.0%) were the most prevalent associated factors.

A striking finding was the near-complete absence of formal medication error documentation (0.0% recorded per policy), contrasting sharply with settings where electronic prescribing systems and structured reporting frameworks support systematic documentation.7,17 Unlike studies from high-income settings that identified workload as a major contributor, our study found no chart-level evidence of workload pressure (0.0%), suggesting that medication handling may have become routinised rather than actively reasoned a systemic risk factor distinct from individual staffing pressures.7,17

Future research should prioritise linking medication errors to clinical outcomes to quantify their direct impact on patient morbidity and mortality, thereby informing high-priority targets for medication safety interventions. Multi-site studies incorporating obstetrics/gynaecology and paediatric departments would provide richer comparative data.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, its retrospective design relied on existing patient records, which may have contained incomplete or inaccurate documentation, likely leading to underestimation of the true prevalence of medication errors. Second, the study was conducted in a single tertiary faith-based hospital; findings cannot be generalised to other healthcare settings in Cameroon or other low-resource environments without further investigation. Third, direct observation was not employed, and errors not documented in charts may have been missed. Fourth, the study did not assess the clinical severity or patient outcomes of identified errors, limiting insight into their direct clinical impact. Fifth, the study did not include obstetrics/gynaecology or paediatric wards, limiting the generalisability of ward-specific findings. Sixth, as a descriptive study, no inferential statistical testing was conducted; contributing factor attribution was based on chart-level evidence and may be subject to reviewer interpretation. Finally, sociodemographic variables such as educational background, rural/urban residence, and socioeconomic status were not routinely recorded in patient charts and therefore could not be analysed. Future prospective studies should prospectively collect these variables. Broader generalisation awaits multisite replication across Cameroonian healthcare settings.

Conclusion

Medication errors are highly prevalent in this tertiary hospital in Cameroon (46.5%), driven primarily by system-level deficiencies particularly inadequate management of patients with multiple comorbidities, unaddressed drug–drug interactions, poor documentation practices, and open-ended prescribing. Strengthening medication management through pharmacist-led interventions, enforcing standardised documentation and reporting practices, integrating simple technological solutions, and fostering a multidisciplinary approach are critical strategies to reduce medication errors and enhance patient safety. Investing in these interventions is essential to align with global patient safety initiatives and to build resilient health systems capable of delivering safer, higher-quality care in resource-constrained environments.
