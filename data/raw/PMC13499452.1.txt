Summary

Bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS) are a diverse group of disorders that commonly present with pancytopenia and require a structured approach to evaluation. Initial assessment focuses on excluding reversible causes, followed by consideration of inherited and acquired conditions. Although the diagnostic approach is broadly similar across age groups, the underlying BMF etiology frequently differs: younger patients are more likely to have immune-mediated or inherited disease, whereas older adults more often have immune or clonal hematopoietic disorders. This review uses two age-defined cases to illustrate key diagnostic and management principles. Integration of clinical features, laboratory findings, and molecular testing is essential for accurate diagnosis. Timely referral to a hematologist and geneticist with expertise in bone marrow failure conditions can facilitate evaluation and multidisciplinary management. Careful classification, appropriate use of genetic testing, and ongoing monitoring are critical to guide treatment decisions, refine prognosis, and assess disease progression over time.

Search strategy and selection criteria.

A structured references search was performed using PubMed. Both MESH terms and free-text keywords were used to search for pertinent references including: “bone marrow failure syndromes”, “pancytopenia”, “aplastic anemia”, “inherited bone marrow failure syndromes”, “acquired and immune mediated bone marrow failure”, “myelodysplastic syndromes”, “clonal evolution and progression to malignancy”, “age and epidemiology, “therapeutic strategies”, to identify relevant studies on inherited and acquired bone marrow failure syndromes, clonal hematopoiesis, and disease evolution across age groups. The references listed include narrative and educational reviews (e.g., NEJM, Blood, and ASH Education Program articles), clinical practice guidelines and consensus statements, along with original research including retrospective and prospective cohort studies and real-world population analyses. Only papers published in English were reviewed. Preference was given to recent publications, (e.g., within the last 7 years), but older seminal references were included as needed. We manually screened references of selected articles, reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines to identify additional studies. The final reference list was generated on the basis of relevance to the scope of this review.

Introduction

Bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS) are a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by impaired hematopoiesis and reduced production of one or more blood cell lineages. As a consequence, the most common presenting finding is some degree of bicytopenia or pancytopenia. These cytopenias represent a clinical syndrome rather than a specific diagnosis, with a broad differential diagnosis including reversible causes that must first be excluded, such as nutritional deficiencies, drugs or toxins, infections, and comorbid systemic disease. BMFS should be considered when there is bone marrow hypocellularity with evidence of hypo-proliferation (classically reflected by reticulocytopenia), once reversible causes have been excluded. While the work-up is largely similar across age groups, the differential diagnosis and relative likelihood of bone marrow failure etiologies and pancytopenia differ by age (Fig. 1), which may also influence the timing and emphasis of specific investigations (e.g., earlier assessment of telomere length in younger patients). In children and young adults, immune-mediated aplastic anemia predominate and inherited bone marrow failure syndromes are more common, whereas in older adults clonal disorders such as myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) predominate. Early recognition, and accurate classification of BMFS are essential to ensure appropriate therapy and to prevent disease-specific complications. BMFS are broadly classified into inherited (constitutional) and acquired forms. Constitutional BMFS are commonly termed the inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFS) and arise due to germline genetic defects that result in BMF and other clinical manifestations. While classically these have been considered diseases of childhood, they are increasingly recognized even later in life.1 Acquired BMFS include immune aplastic anemia (iAA), paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH), and MDS. The pathophysiology of acquired BMFS can be immune-mediated (as with iAA) or clonal (as with MDS), though there is some overlap. The distinction between inherited versus acquired and immune versus clonal provides a clinically meaningful framework, as the differences in disease biology have an impact on prognosis and treatment strategies.

BMFS are rare, with the incidence of acquired aplastic anemia approximately 2–3 cases per million in North America and Europe, with two- to three-fold higher rates reported in Asia.2,3 PNH affects a similar proportion of people, 1–2 per million yearly.4 In contrast, IBMFS have heterogenous rates across individual conditions, ranging from approximately 1 in 100,000–160,000 births for Fanconi anemia (FA) to around 1 in one million for dyskeratosis congenita (DKC) (Table 1. Age distribution differs markedly across BMFS. Aplastic anemia has a bimodal pattern, with peaks in young adults (15–25 years) and older adults (>60 years).3 IBMFS predominantly present in childhood, with a peak incidence between 2 and 5 years of age. They account for approximately 25–30% of bone marrow failure in pediatric populations, compared with 5–15% of cases in younger adults. Although diagnosis is made usually early in life, up to 10–20% of cases may be recognized later in adulthood, often with attenuated or atypical features.5,6,9,23 This broader age distribution reflects increasing use of genetic testing and improved recognition of milder phenotypes. On the flip side, clonal disorders such as MDS are rarely seen in children or younger adults, predominantly affecting older individuals.21

This review uses two representative hypothetical cases to highlight diagnostic and therapeutic principles in BMFS, emphasizing the role of age in generating a differential diagnosis, and the need for an integrated approach.

Case #1: an 18-year-old with pancytopenia

Clinical presentation and initial differential diagnosis

An 18-year-old previously healthy woman presented with progressive bruising, gingival bleeding, menorrhagia, and fatigue. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed severe pancytopenia, with platelets of 4000/μL, hemoglobin of 9.8 g/dL, and leukopenia (2.43 × 109/L). The absolute reticulocyte count (ARC) was 0.02 × 109/L, consistent with a hypo-proliferative BMF. Organ function was normal. Her family history was notable for autoimmune conditions in her mother and maternal aunt, but no cytopenias or characteristic constitutional abnormalities.

The initial approach to a young patient with pancytopenia begins with assessing clinical stability, including the need for transfusions, antibiotics, or hospitalization (see diagnostic algorithm Fig. 1). Markers of hemolysis such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin and bilirubin should be checked to rule out hemolysis. The evaluation then focuses on reversible causes, with laboratory testing for nutritional deficiencies (vitamin B12, methylmalonic acid levels, folate, iron and copper), erythropoietin level and infections (e.g., HIV, hepatitis b and c), which can lead to transient cytopenias responsive to treatment. Toxin and drug exposure, particularly benzene, is a rare, recognized cause but is often difficult to confirm. Patients with two or more cytopenias or unexplained, severe single-lineage cytopenia should undergo a bone marrow aspirate and biopsy to assess cellularity, cell morphology, and to evaluate for underlying clonal or germline abnormalities.

In this patient, A markedly reduced reticulocyte count supported a hypo-proliferative process. The primary differential diagnosis included reversible causes (nutritional deficiency, viral infection and toxin exposure), IBMFS, iAA, and iAA associated with paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH). Initial laboratory testing excluded nutritional deficiencies and infectious etiologies, narrowing the differential towards intrinsic marrow pathology. A bone marrow biopsy demonstrated profound hypocellularity (<5%) without dysplasia or increased blasts. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of the BM identified a mutation in the BCOR gene with a variant allele frequency (VAF), which reflects the proportion of cells carrying each mutation, of 18.5%.

Her acute course and absence of constitutional symptoms was consistent with iAA, although an underlying IBMFS needed to be excluded using specialized and genetic testing (see below). The hypocellular marrow and presence of a small PNH clone by flow cytometry supported an immune-mediated process (iAA or iAA/PNH overlap) rather than IBMFS, as PNH clones can co-occur with iAA approximately 40–60% of the time, but are virtually absent in IBMFS.24 Mutations in BCOR are also commonly seen in iAA.25,26

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of AA, which remains a diagnosis of exclusion and requires careful evaluation for inherited syndromes and reversible acquired causes. The identification of mutations on the NGS panel underscores the need to integrate laboratory and molecular data when establishing the diagnosis.

Congenital and acquired bone marrow failure in young patients

Inherited/congenital bone marrow failure syndromes

The central challenge in young patients with BMFS lies in distinguishing acquired immune-mediated disease from inherited or genetically predisposed conditions, particularly as this has direct therapeutic implications (discussed under the treatment section). IBMFS arise from germline defects in genes affecting DNA repair, telomere biology, or hematopoietic stem cell maintenance. They are rare mendelian disorders with more than a hundred disease genes identified that exhibit a wide phenotypic spectrum, ranging from subtle to profound clinical manifestations, and presenting at any time from early childhood to adulthood.5 Once a diagnosis of IBMFS is made, given their rarity and complexity, referral to a specialty center is advised. Details of the commonest IBMFS, including Fanconi Anemia, telomere biology disorders (TBD), Diamond-Blackfan anemia, and Schwachman-Diamond syndrome, can be found in Table 1.5

Distinguishing among these conditions clinically is challenging, as all present with cytopenias and marrow hypoproliferation. A systematic assessment - including disease course, exposures, phenotype, and family history - is essential, although a negative family history does not exclude inherited or congenital disease. Clinical clues, such as limb anomalies seen with Fanconi anemia or pulmonary/hepatic findings observed with TBD, may help but are not always present. In adult patients, typical phenotypic features of IBMFS may be absent or subtle. The expanding use of genetic sequencing, in particular whole genome sequencing, has further broadened the spectrum of IBMFS, with new disorders regularly being described. Disease-specific assays (including telomere length and diepoxybutane [DEB] chromosomal breakage) are used to evaluate for TBD and Fanconi anemia, the commonest IBMFS. Germline genetic testing should be strongly considered in patients with iAA or MDS, particularly in younger individuals (under 40 years of age), and whenever an inherited bone marrow failure syndrome is suspected, in accordance with expert recommendations and guideline-based approaches.27, 28, 29

Interpreting genetic reports can be tricky. Germline variants are classified (benign, pathogenic, or of uncertain significance) using criteria from the American College of Medical Genetics. If there is any concerning mutation found on a genetic report (even if classed as “uncertain significance”), expert consultation is advised, as these may still represent relevant variants. Standard myeloid mutation panels may also inadvertently detect a germline variant (for example RUNX1, which can be either germline or somatic). Typically, a low VAF (under ∼30%) favors somatic origin, while higher VAFs suggest germline mutations. Testing non-hematopoietic tissue, most commonly skin fibroblasts, is the gold standard to confirm germline status if there is uncertainty, as somatic mutations will be absent in non-hematopoietic tissue. There are no universally accepted germline genetic panels. The cassette of genes involved in IBMFS and germline predisposition continues to evolve, and targeted sequencing should be undertaken using a CLIA certified panel.5,30 Given the variable penetrance of germline disorders and the recognition that many germline predisposition variants can occur across a broad age range, the overall approach to genetic evaluation is often similar in younger and older adults. Careful evaluation of the family pedigree, together with cascade genetic testing of relatives in selected cases, can further help determine whether a variant is inherited and support its clinical relevance and pathogenicity.

In this case, BCOR was identified, which is a typical somatic mutation seen in iAA, supporting this diagnosis. Flow cytometry for PNH clones is also useful, as PNH clones are virtually absent in inherited syndromes and support immune-mediated disease.24

Immune bone marrow failure: aplastic anemia

Aplastic anemia is the prototypical acquired BMFS, characterized by T-cell mediated immune destruction of hematopoietic stem (HSCs), resulting in a hypocellular marrow and pancytopenia. It is the most common cause of acquired BMF in adolescents and adults and reflects a quantitative stem cell depletion.3 The incidence varies geographically, estimated at approximately 2–3 cases per million per year in North America and Europe, with rates two- to three-fold higher in parts of East Asia (∼4–7 cases per million per year).2 Across populations, a bimodal age distribution is observed, with peaks in young adults (∼15–25 years) and older adults (≥60 years).3 There is no specific diagnostic test for iAA; therefore, other causes of BMF must be excluded, as discussed above.

Adolescents and young adults are particularly prone to diagnostic delay, as symptoms such as fatigue due to anemia, infection in the setting of neutropenia, or bleeding as a result of thrombocytopenia are often attributed to more common conditions such as nutritional deficiencies or infections. This is a critical period, as early treatment - including IST or HSCT when appropriate - has a significant impact on outcome.31, 32, 33

The diagnosis of AA requires integration of peripheral blood findings and bone marrow examination: a hypocellular marrow (typically <25%, or <50% with <30% hematopoietic cells) with at least two cytopenias: ANC < 1.5 × 109/L, platelet count <100 × 109/L, or hemoglobin <10 g/dL. Disease severity is classified according to modified Camitta criteria: Severe AA (SAA) is defined by marrow hypocellularity with at least two of the following: ANC < 0.5 × 109/L, platelet count <20 × 109/L, or ARC <20 × 109/L; very severe AA (VSAA) is defined by an ANC < 0.2 × 109/L. These thresholds correlate with infectious risk and urgency of treatment, with delays beyond 3–6 months associated with worse outcomes.3,34,35 Patients with VSAA require urgent (ideally within 1–2 weeks) therapy due to their high risk of infection.

Immune-mediated marrow failure can be associated with clonal hematopoiesis, most notably paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH), caused by somatic mutations in PIGA. Small PNH clones are detected in approximately 40–60% of patients with AA using high-sensitivity flow cytometry,36 and their presence is associated with improved response to IST. Certain somatic mutations (BCOR/L1, PIGA) add further support to an iAA diagnosis.37 Overall, the specific pattern of somatic mutations differs between iAA, MDS and each IBMFS and thus may be helpful diagnostically.

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH)

PNH is a clonal hematopoietic disorder caused by acquired mutations in the PIGA gene, resulting in deficiency of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored proteins, including the complement regulators CD55 and CD59. Loss of these surface proteins render erythrocytes susceptible to complement-mediated predominantly intravascular hemolysis, although extravascular hemolysis may also occur. PNH exists along a biological continuum with immune-mediated bone marrow failure, such as iAA. The diagnosis relies on high-sensitivity flow cytometry demonstrating GPI-deficient populations in granulocytes and monocytes.

PNH is rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1–2 cases per million annually. The disorder most commonly affects young adults, although it can occur at any age.36 PNH clones are frequently identified in the context of bone marrow failure, as mentioned previously. Generally, these clones are small and do not cause hemolytic PNH (instead termed AA/PNH overlap), but may expand or recede over time and require monitoring.25 Larger clones (>30% in granulocytes) are associated with higher risk of hemolysis and increased thrombotic risk and often require anti-complement therapy.38

Treatment strategies are guided by underlying disease biology

The patient was diagnosed with immune-mediated VSAA. Her germline genetic panel was negative and myeloid gene panel showed BCOR gene mutation, which is not associated with evolution to leukemia. There were no concerning features for MDS, with normal BM morphology and karyotype. DEB chromosomal breakage testing and telomere length (TL) assessment, were normal, effectively excluding FA and TBD. Short TL in granulocytes is not specific for TBD and can also be found in AA. However, TL shorter than the 1st percentile in lymphocytes is highly suggestive of TBD.39,40 Presence of a PNH clone also supported the diagnosis of iAA. In the absence of a matched sibling donor and given the patient’s desire to preserve fertility, standard medical therapy with horse ATG, cyclosporine, and eltrombopag was initiated.34 At the same time, parallel planning for potential matched unrelated donor HSCT was initiated in the event of non-response, with ongoing longitudinal reassessment.

This case illustrates the need to define the underlying biology of marrow failure before treatment, balancing diagnostic uncertainty with the urgency of life-threatening cytopenias. Management is guided by age, donor availability and clinical context. Patients over 40 years of age or without a matched sibling donor receive immunosuppression (horse ATG, cyclosporine, eltrombopag). Younger patients with a matched donor are considered for upfront HSCT given its curative potential.3,41

The disease course is variable. Untreated severe aplastic anemia carries high early mortality, most commonly from infection or hemorrhage. With therapy, five-year survival exceeds 80–90%.34,42 Long-term outcomes are influenced by response, relapse, clonal evolution to MDS/AML (approximately 11–20%),43,44 and development of PNH.

Distinction of IBMFS versus iAA has direct therapeutic implications. The standard Immunosuppressive therapy (IST) regimen used to treat iAA is generally ineffective in IBMFS and thus should be avoided. IBMFS require allogeneic HSCT when severe cytopenia occur. IBMFS are heterogenous, and each has a distinct therapeutic approach that is outside the scope of this review. One important unifying consideration is that while HSCT can be used in either iAA or IBMFS, the optimal conditioning (chemotherapy used to prepare the bone marrow) differs, and potential family donors must be carefully evaluated in IBMFS cases to avoid reintroducing the genetic defect. Early recognition allows appropriate donor selection and timely transplantation before clonal progression.45 Inherited forms are characterized by increased susceptibility to treatment-related toxicity, a distinct spectrum of organ dysfunction, and a substantially higher and earlier risk of leukemic transformation compared with immune-mediated disease. These differences necessitate tailored long-term surveillance strategies that extend beyond hematologic monitoring alone.45

Treatment of PNH is indicated for patients with clinically significant disease, including active hemolysis (LDH ≥ 1.5 × the upper limit of normal), thrombosis, anemia or substantial fatigue. While larger clones (>30% versus <5% granulocyte clone size) are associated with greater disease burden and more clinical symptoms,38 clone size alone does not always mandate treatment. However, granulocyte clone sizes exceeding 50% have been linked to an increased risk of thrombosis, even in the absence of overt hemolysis, and should prompt careful consideration of treatment.24,38 Therapy reduces cytopenias and thrombotic risk, the primary causes of mortality. The development of complement inhibitors in the early 2000s revolutionized therapy; however, most patients require these medications life-long. Eculizumab, a C5 inhibitor, was the first approved therapy; this requires administration IV every 2 weeks. The longer-acting agent ravulizumab is administered IV every 8 weeks. More recently, proximal complement inhibitors have expanded patients’ options, including pegcetacoplan, a subcutaneous C3 inhibitor, and the first oral factor B inhibitor iptacopan. Compared to the proximal inhibitors, iptacopan has shown superior efficacy, achieving transfusion avoidance in 95% of PNH patients compared to 51–68% with C5 inhibitors like eculizumab and just 26% with continued C5 therapy. Now, choice is guided by patient preference, logistics, and clinical context.

Management of case #1

Her care required multidisciplinary coordination, including transfusion support, infection management, hormonal therapy for menstrual suppression, and reproductive planning. Treatment initiation was briefly delayed due to an intercurrent infection, highlighting the complexity of managing critically cytopenic patients.

This case underscores several key principles: early consideration of immune versus inherited etiologies, cautious interpretation of molecular findings, the supportive but non-definitive role of PNH clones, and the necessity of initiating therapy despite incomplete diagnostic certainty. Aligning clinical presentation, evolving molecular data, and treatment strategy is critical to optimizing outcomes in young patients with bone marrow failure.

Case #2: a 70-year-old with pancytopenia

Clinical presentation and initial differential diagnosis

A 70-year-old man presented with progressive pancytopenia identified on routine laboratory testing and a six-month history of worsening fatigue without constitutional symptoms. His history was notable for untreated macrocytic anemia documented two years earlier. Laboratory studies showed a hemoglobin of 9.2 g/dL (MCV 104 fL), white blood cell count of 2.8 × 109/L (ANC 1.2 × 109/L), and platelets of 85 × 109/L. Common nutritional deficiencies were excluded, as were renal and hepatic function abnormalities.

A bone marrow biopsy and aspirate demonstrated decreased cellularity for age (15%), 4% myeloblasts, and dysplasia in erythroid and myeloid lineages. Cytogenetic analysis revealed isolated loss of the Y chromosome, which may be age-related but also can reflect clonal hematopoiesis.

The initial workup, similarly to the younger patients, includes a detailed history to establish the chronicity of symptoms, and to assess for reversible factors similar to the previous case. Along with medication-induced myelosuppression. A peripheral blood smear, in conjunction with hemolytic markers, should be obtained to assess cellular morphology and phenotype and to help exclude causes such as microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, followed by bone marrow biopsy.

In older adults, pancytopenia more commonly reflects clonal hematopoietic disorders, including MDS, clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS), and myeloid and lymphoid cancers. IBMFS, though less common on the differential, may become manifest in the sixth or seventh decade of life and should be considered. Longstanding macrocytosis may precede overt cytopenias and serves as an early indicator of underlying marrow pathology. This case highlights the importance of integrating clinical history, laboratory findings, and marrow evaluation when assessing cytopenias in older patients (Fig. 1. Common bone marrow failure and clonal disorders in older adults

Clonal hematopoiesis and CCUS

Age-related clonal hematopoiesis has emerged as a common biological phenomenon in older adults and represents an important precursor state to frank myeloid malignancy. These entities carry a variable but measurable risk of progression to myeloid neoplasia.

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is defined by the presence of somatic mutations associated with hematologic malignancies, most commonly in DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1, without cytopenias or morphologic evidence of a hematologic disorder. Its prevalence increases markedly with age, affecting approximately 10–20% of individuals older than 70 years and in more than 30% over 80 years. Although often asymptomatic, CHIP carries a ∼0.5–1% annual risk of progression to myeloid neoplasia and is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity.51, 52, 53

CCUS is defined by persistent cytopenia(s) with one or more myeloid cancer-associated somatic mutations, without morphologic or cytogenetic criteria for MDS or AML. It represents an intermediate state between CHIP and overt myeloid neoplasia and carries a substantially higher risk of progression than CHIP. Progression to MDS or AML varies by risk, ranging from 6% to 37% at 2 years, with an overall rate of ∼16.5% in prospective cohorts.54, 55, 56 Higher risk is seen with increased mutation VAF, multiple mutations, or high-risk genes such as TP53, RUNX1, and spliceosome genes.57, 58, 59

Idiopathic cytopenia of undetermined significance (ICUS) is a provisional category describing persistent cytopenias that do not meet the criteria for MDS or clonal hematopoiesis. In a study by Schroeder et al. Twelve percent progressed to AML and an estimated 20–35% harbored mutations on genetic testing and were reclassified as clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS).60,61

Recognition of ICUS and CCUS has refined the evaluation of unexplained cytopenias in older adults. These entities reflect a continuum toward malignancy and the importance of integration of molecular, clinical, and morphologic findings.

Myelodysplastic syndromes

Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) represent a heterogeneous group of clonal hematopoietic stem cell malignancies characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis, morphologic dysplasia, and risk of progression to AML. They arise from somatically mutated hematopoietic stem cells that acquire proliferative or survival advantages, leading to clonal expansion and progressive marrow dysfunction. Recurrent mutations most commonly involve genes such as TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, and RNA splicing factors including SF3B1, SRSF2, and U2AF1.20,62 Diagnosis requires demonstration of dysplasia in ≥10% of cells in at least one hematopoietic lineage on bone marrow examination, in the appropriate clinical context.63,64 Presentation ranges from isolated to multilineage cytopenias, and diagnosis integrates morphology, cytogenetics, blasts, and molecular findings.

Hypoplastic MDS (hMDS) is a recognized subset of MDS in the WHO 2022 classification,64 defined by bone marrow cellularity <25% (or reduced for age) and <5% blasts, accounting for ∼10–15% of cases. Distinguishing it from aplastic anemia can be difficult, as both present with hypocellular marrow and low blasts; however, hMDS more often demonstrates dysplasia, cytogenetic abnormalities, and somatic mutations consistent with clonal hematopoiesis.3,48 There is significant biological and clinical overlap and, like with iAA, many hMDS patients respond to IST.

The clinical course of MDS is highly heterogeneous, ranging from indolent disease with stable cytopenia for many years to rapid progression to AML in others. Overall transformation rates vary widely depending on risk category, ranging from less than 10% in lower-risk disease to more than 40–50% in higher-risk subtypes. Median survival ranges from 3 to 10 years in lower-risk MDS, whereas patients with higher-risk MDS have a survival of less than 3 years.21 This biological diversity reflects the underlying complexity of clonal evolution and emphasizes the importance of accurate risk stratification in guiding management strategies.

Aplastic anemia in older adults

In older patients, pancytopenia may reflect either AA or MDS. Immune AA in older patients, typically defined as >60 years, differs from iAA younger population in some key aspects. While both are caused by an immune-mediated attack on the bone marrow, older adults harbor a higher burden of clonal hematopoiesis and higher risk of clonal evolution to MDS (∼12%).26 The diagnosis in older adults is complicated by more competing diagnoses, particularly hMDS, which outnumbers AA by approximately 2:1 in adults with a hypocellular marrow.49

Treatment approaches are influenced by age, as co-morbidities may preclude administration of ATG and high doses of cyclosporine in older adults. Efficacy data are conflicting, with some studies showing lower response rates, even with ATG-based therapy, and others showing comparable responses.65,66

Survival, however, is consistently significantly worse. In one study in which all patients received ATG-based therapy, 3-year survival was 79% in older versus 87% in younger patients. In another study where patients received a mixture of therapies (ATG, cyclosporine, eltrombopag, androgens), older patients had significantly worse survival (39%) at 5 years. IST remains the preferred initial treatment for most older patients with AA. However, transplant outcomes in older adults have improved considerably in recent years, reflecting advances in donor selection, conditioning regimens, and supportive care. While age has traditionally been viewed as a major barrier to transplantation, poorer outcomes have often been driven by comorbidities and more advanced disease. As a result, allogeneic transplantation is increasingly being considered as a viable alternative in selected older patients, particularly those with high-risk clonal hematopoiesis or signs of disease progression.41,67

Non-responders and the very old (>70 years), in particular, have the highest mortality rates, often succumbing to infectious or bleeding complications. Importantly, responders have outcomes comparable to younger patients.66 Older patients are also more likely to relapse and less likely to tolerate HSCT, the standard salvage regimen for SAA patients who are refractory or recurrently relapse. Management should therefore be individualized, with guidelines emphasizing performance status and comorbidities over age alone, and favoring IST-based approaches (including dose-attenuated ATG or TPO-agonist combinations) while reserving transplant for select cases.

Germline predisposition in older patients

Although IBMFS are often associated with younger patients, germline predisposition to myeloid neoplasia can present in later adulthood. With the increasing use of next-generation sequencing in the evaluation of cytopenias, germline variants are being identified in patients previously thought to have sporadic disease. One of the most well-described examples is DDX41, which is associated with late-onset MDS and AML, often presenting in the sixth or seventh decade.68 This highlights that inherited causes should be considered in your differential across the entire age spectrum.1

In the patient described here, NGS identified two DDX41 variants: a likely germline variant (p.M1I, VAF ∼48%) and a somatic variant (p.R525H, VAF ∼22%). Subsequent testing of skin fibroblasts confirmed germline status. This pattern is characteristic of DDX41-associated disease, in which a germline mutation is followed by acquisition of a second somatic “hit,” leading to clonal expansion and myeloid malignancy.69

Germline DDX41 mutations are the most common hereditary predisposition to adult myeloid neoplasms, accounting for 2–5% of MDS/AML cases.70,71 DDX41-associated MDS is typically characterized by hypocellular marrow, low blast burden, and paucity of co-mutations, distinguishing it from de novo MDS.72 Clinically, these patients have more favorable outcomes and treatment responses. A meta-analysis of more than 9000 patients showed superior OS (HR 0.70, p = 0.01), while single-center studies reported a median OS of 71 months and an 84% response.72,73 Notably, adverse features such as TP53 multihit mutations appear to confer less prognostic impact in this subgroup.

Diagnosis and treatment strategies are guided by biology

In low-risk or early clonal disorders, including lower-risk MDS, observation and supportive care are appropriate, focusing on monitoring, transfusions, and symptom management rather than disease modification. This includes monitoring, transfusion support, and management of complications, as indicated. Treatment for low-risk MDS includes recombinant erythropoietin for those with low or insufficiently elevated erythropoietin levels (particularly <200–500 mU/mL), Lenalidomide (for de5q subtype), Lupatercept for patients with anemia, and the more recently approved Imetelstat for anemic patients with treatment refractory disease.28 Lupatercept is approved for both erythropoietin-refractory and erythropoietin-naïve patients; however, it is most effective in treating MDS with SF3B1 mutations compared to non-mutated SF3B1 cases.74 Not all cytopenias require immediate intervention, particularly in indolent or pre-malignant states, and no prospective study has ever demonstrated that early intervention improves survival compared to therapeutic initiation dictated by transfusion dependency, risk of evolution to more serious malignancies, or poor quality of life.

In selected patients with hypocellular marrow disorders, including hypocellular MDS with immune-mediated features, IST may provide hematologic improvement.50,75 In contrast, patients with higher-risk MDS require disease-modifying approaches, including hypomethylating agents or other therapies aimed at altering disease trajectory and reducing progression to acute leukemia.

HSCT remains the only potentially curative therapy for MDS and is increasingly considered in healthy, carefully selected older patients based on physiological fitness, comorbidities, social support, and disease risk.

In the case presented, the diagnosis was most consistent with MDS with low blasts (WHO 2022) with germline DDX41 predisposition.64,76 Active surveillance was recommended, given his stable blood counts and lack of transfusion needs. Identification of a germline predisposition prompted a referral for genetic counseling, family screening, and future transplant planning to avoid affected donors in accordance with expert guidelines.71,76 This approach should be undertaken thoughtfully, as unaffected carriers may require ongoing surveillance and genetic counseling. The patient continued to undergo close monitoring with serial blood counts.

This case highlights several key clinical principles in the evaluation of unexplained cytopenias. Pancytopenia in older adults is frequently clonal in origin, with clonal hematopoiesis present in over 10–15% of individuals above age 65.53 Germline predisposition should be considered even in the absence of a family history, as hereditary syndromes account for approximately 15–20% of MDS/AML cases diagnosed at age 40 or younger, and DDX41 variants are increasingly recognized in older patients with MDS. Importantly, patients may lack overt clinical stigmata, making clinical suspicion essential.77 Molecular findings refine diagnosis and influence long-term management through improved risk stratification and transplant planning.56,63 Finally, treatment decisions must balance disease risk with patient-specific factors including age, comorbidities, and high-risk molecular features.

Case #2 (part 2): disease evolution and risk of secondary AML

The patient remained clinically stable for approximately 14 months following his diagnosis, with persistent non-transfusion-dependent cytopenias. At 18 months, he developed worsening fatigue and dyspnea on exertion. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated worsening pancytopenia with a hemoglobin of 7.8 g/dL, ANC of 0.9 × 109/L, and platelet count of 42 × 109/L. a peripheral smear revealed circulating blasts, and flow cytometry confirmed expansion of a myeloblast population. A repeat bone marrow examination showed increased cellularity (80%) with 37% myeloblasts, establishing transformation to AML.

This progression illustrates clonal evolution in myeloid disease, in which sequential genetic events drive transition from clonal hematopoiesis or MDS to secondary AML. Repeat molecular testing demonstrated persistence of the previously identified germline and somatic DDX41 variants, expansion of the somatic clone with increased VAF, and acquisition of a new mutation in ASXL1. These findings are consistent with stepwise genomic evolution resulting in leukemic transformation.68,70

Progression from clonal hematopoiesis to MDS and ultimately AML occurs with an estimated annual risk of approximately 0.5–1% in CHIP to as high as 30–40% in MDS.21,52, 53, 54,59,78 Substantially higher risks are particularly seen in the presence of high-risk mutations or increasing blast burden.53,79,80 Disease evolution reflects ongoing clonal competition and selection of subclones with proliferative advantage.

Therapeutic decisions at this stage must balance disease biology with patient factors. Given the patient’s age and functional status, treatment with a hypomethylating agent combined with venetoclax was initiated, consistent with current treatment strategies for older adults with AML who may not tolerate intensive induction therapy.81 The patient achieved complete remission after two treatment cycles and was subsequently referred for evaluation for allogeneic HSCT. Because of the known germline DDX41 mutation, careful donor evaluation was required.70,76

This case illustrates several relevant principles. Cytopenia in older adults frequently reflect underlying clonal disease with potential for progression-requiring active surveillance. Germline predisposition may present late and without a clear family history. Most importantly, disease evolution is driven by acquisition of additional somatic mutations, and clinical stability at diagnosis does not preclude subsequent leukemic transformation.

Conclusions

Pancytopenia represents a broad group of biologically distinct disorders. The cases illustrate how the underlying cause of marrow failure is influenced by patient age, clinical context, and genetic background.

Most importantly, pancytopenia is a syndrome rather than a diagnosis. Similar laboratory findings - cytopenias, macrocytosis, or marrow hypocellularity - may arise from immune-mediated marrow failure, inherited predisposition, or clonal hematopoietic disease. Careful evaluation is required to define the underlying biology before treatment decisions are made.

Age remains a key guide to diagnostic probability. In younger patients, iAA and inherited syndromes predominate, while in older adults the differential shifts toward clonal disorders. However, inherited disease cannot be excluded based on age alone.

These cases highlight the growing importance of molecular and germline evaluation in the appropriate clinical context. Accurate management of pancytopenia depends on correct disease classification through integration of clinical, pathological, and molecular findings. Identification of somatic mutations, PNH clones, or germline variants informs diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment selection, while also guiding surveillance, family counseling, and identification of patients who require early intervention or long-term monitoring for clonal evolution.
