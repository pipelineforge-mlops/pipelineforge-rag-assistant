The present study evaluated the effects of graded replacement of extruded soybean meal with Periplaneta americana residue (PAR) on laying performance, egg quality, ovarian function, and ovarian transcriptomic profiles in Guangxi Yao chickens. A total of 210 female chickens at 30 d of age were randomly assigned to 6 dietary treatments, with 5 replicate cages of 7 birds each. PAR replaced 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, or 50% of the extruded soybean meal, corresponding to dietary inclusion levels of approximately 0%, 1.82%, 3.61%, 5.42%, 7.19%, and 8.95%. The feeding trial lasted 24 wk. Laying performance and egg quality were assessed at the replicate-cage level, whereas ovarian follicle characteristics, reproductive hormones, immunoglobulins, histomorphology, and transcriptomic profiles were evaluated thereafter. Dietary treatment affected average egg weight (AEW; P = 0.004), with birds in the 10% replacement group producing heavier eggs than those in the control and 20% replacement groups. Average daily feed intake (ADFI), laying rate (LR), and feed-to-egg ratio (F:E) were not significantly affected, although ADFI exhibited significant linear and quadratic responses to increasing PAR replacement. Egg shape index (ESI) differed among treatments (P = 0.002), whereas yolk weight (YW), yolk color score (YCS), yolk proportion (YP), albumen height (AH), Haugh unit (HU), and eggshell thickness (EST) remained unchanged. Ovarian estradiol (E2) concentration differed among treatments, whereas ovarian follicle numbers did not. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) revealed treatment-associated changes in genes and pathways related to lipid metabolism, ovarian function, cellular signaling, and immune regulation, although principal component analysis (PCA) indicated substantial overlap among treatment groups. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified an exploratory module associated with EST, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) showed moderate agreement with the RNA-seq results. Overall, replacing up to 50% of extruded soybean meal with PAR did not adversely affect most production, egg-quality, or ovarian-follicle traits, whereas the clearest production response was an increase in AEW at the 10% replacement level.

Introduction

Eggs are an important dietary source of high-quality protein and other essential nutrients (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Layer production is recognized as a key component of the animal-source food supply chain (Gautron et al., 2021) and is often integrated with small-scale or region-specific production systems across different regions (Lordelo et al., 2020). The Guangxi Yao chicken (also known as the Nandan Yao chicken or Yaoshan chicken) is a distinctive local breed from Guangxi. According to records from the National Domestic Animal Genetic Resources Repository, the breed is characterized by strong adaptability, tolerance to roughage-based diets, and resistance to disease and environmental stressors (National Genebank of Domestic Animal Genetic Resources, n.d.; Sun et al., 2022).

Soybean meal is among the most widely used plant-derived protein ingredients in layer diets. Its use is influenced by supply-demand dynamics, price volatility, and competition for resources. In response, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of China issued a “Three-Year Action Plan for Reducing and Replacing Soybean Meal in Feed” to promote diversification of protein sources for feed (General Office of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, 2023). Therefore, identifying locally available alternative protein ingredients that can partially replace soybean meal without adversely affecting laying performance or egg quality is important for improving the sustainability and economic resilience of local poultry production systems.

Insect protein has been considered a promising alternative feed protein source because of its high crude protein content, the potential to upcycle by-products during production, and its relatively low environmental footprint (Morales-Ramos et al., 2024). Insect meals (e.g., mealworm and black soldier fly) typically contain 40–65% crude protein, and partial replacement of soybean meal at appropriate inclusion levels has not been associated with impaired growth performance (Hong and Kim, 2022; Belhadj Slimen et al., 2023). At certain inclusion levels, insect meal can partially substitute for soybean meal without necessarily compromising laying performance or egg quality, although specific traits may differ. The outcomes are likely to depend on insect species, processing methods, substitution level, and the dietary fatty acid profile (Romero et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Dublecz et al., 2025). However, the feeding value of insect-derived ingredients cannot be evaluated solely on the basis of crude protein content. Their nutritional value also depends on amino acid composition and balance, protein and amino acid digestibility, lipid and fatty acid profiles, mineral composition, and the concentration of structural components such as chitin (Lee et al., 2022). Insect species and developmental stage can substantially influence nutrient composition and digestibility (Belhadj Slimen et al., 2023). Processing conditions, particularly the degree of defatting, may further alter nutrient availability, metabolizable energy, and amino acid digestibility (Schiavone et al., 2017). Dietary inclusion level and the nutritional characteristics of the conventional ingredient being replaced may also affect productive responses, indicating that insect-derived ingredients should be evaluated within the context of the complete diet rather than solely according to their nominal crude protein content (Elahi et al., 2022). Chitin and residual bioactive components, including antimicrobial peptides and specific fatty acids, may influence intestinal morphology, gut microbiota, immune responses, and antioxidant-related processes, although the magnitude and direction of these effects vary with insect species, ingredient composition, inclusion level, and processing history (Malematja et al., 2023).

Periplaneta americana has long been used as a medicinal insect and contains proteins, lipids, minerals, peptides, and other biologically active components (Zeng et al., 2019). Extracts and peptides derived from P. americana have also been reported to exhibit antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and tissue-repair activities (Fu et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). Following medicinal extraction, substantial quantities of Periplaneta americana residue (PAR) are generated. This post-extraction by-product retains a proportion of the nutrients present in the original insect material and may therefore have potential for valorization as an alternative feed ingredient. Nevertheless, PAR should not be assumed to be nutritionally equivalent to intact P. americana meal because it is the solid residue remaining after medicinal extraction and therefore represents only the fraction of the original biomass retained after processing (Long et al., 2024). PAR may retain varying proportions of protein, lipid, structural components, and residual bioactive constituents, but its composition and nutritional value may differ according to the source material, extraction conditions, drying procedures, and subsequent processing history. More generally, insect species, nutrient composition, developmental stage, and processing methods can influence the digestibility and biological functionality of insect-derived feed ingredients (Lee et al., 2022). In poultry, PAR has been reported to contain appreciable amounts of crude protein and crude fat and to be associated with dose-dependent changes in immune and antioxidant indices, suggesting that both its nutritional composition and residual functional constituents may be relevant to the observed feeding responses (Zhao et al., 2025). Therefore, the source and processing history of PAR should be clearly documented, and available information on its proximate composition, amino acid profile, nutrient digestibility, chitin-related fractions, and residual bioactive constituents should be considered when interpreting its value as a feed ingredient.

Research on the use of PAR as a feed ingredient has increased in recent years, although available poultry studies remain limited. In growing Sanhuang chickens, Ou et al. (2024) reported that partial replacement of extruded soybean meal with PAR affected growth performance and several intestinal indices, suggesting that the response depended on the dietary replacement level. In early-phase pullets, Tao et al. (2025) found that graded replacement of extruded soybean meal with PAR at levels ranging from 0 to 50% did not markedly affect growth performance but increased selected intestinal immunoglobulin concentrations and induced transcriptomic responses associated with intestinal health. Zhao et al. (2025) further reported dose-dependent changes in immune, antioxidant, and transcriptomic indices in Sanhuang chickens. Collectively, these studies indicate that PAR can be incorporated into poultry diets without consistently impairing growth performance and may be associated with changes in intestinal, immune, and antioxidant responses. However, the observed effects appear to vary with inclusion level, physiological stage, and the endpoint evaluated. Existing studies have mainly focused on growing chickens, whereas evidence from birds during the laying period remains scarce, particularly in local breeds. Consequently, it remains unclear how graded substitution of soybean meal with PAR affects laying performance, egg quality, endocrine indices, ovarian morphology, and ovarian transcriptional profiles.

Therefore, the present study was conducted in Guangxi Yao chickens to evaluate the dose-response effects of replacing extruded soybean meal with PAR on laying performance and egg quality and to assess the feasibility of PAR as an alternative feed ingredient during the laying period. Serum and ovarian hormone indices, serum immunoglobulins, ovarian follicle numbers, ovarian histomorphology, and ovarian transcriptomic profiles were also examined to characterize physiological and transcriptional responses associated with graded PAR replacement. These analyses were considered exploratory and were intended to identify candidate biological processes.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The animal study was approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Dali University (No. 2024 PZ-151). The study was conducted in accordance with the local legislation and institutional requirements.

Preparation of PAR and experimental diets

PAR was obtained from Yunnan Teng Pharmaceutical Co. Mature P. americana specimens were dried at 80–90°C and subsequently pulverized to pass through a 250-μm sieve. The resulting powder was mixed with 95% ethanol at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:3 and macerated for 5 d with agitation twice daily, followed by filtration. The ethanol extraction and filtration procedures were repeated twice, after which the insoluble residue was collected and air-dried at room temperature for use as PAR.

Six experimental diets were formulated. The control diet contained no PAR, whereas in the other five diets, PAR replaced 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, or 50% of the extruded soybean meal in the control diet; these treatments were designated CON, PAR10, PAR20, PAR30, PAR40, and PAR50, respectively. The experimental diets were formulated to be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous, and all ingredients were thoroughly mixed according to the respective formulations. The experimental diets were provided in mash form. The ingredient composition and nutrient levels of all six experimental diets are presented in Table 1. The PAR used in the present feeding trial was obtained from the same batch of material previously characterized by Tao et al. (2025). Determination and calculation of dietary nutrient levels

The metabolizable energy values of the experimental diets were calculated according to the Chinese Feed Composition and Nutritional Value Tables (30th edition, 2019) (Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences et al., 2019). Crude protein, calcium, total phosphorus, lysine, methionine, and cysteine concentrations were determined in accordance with GB/T 6432—2018, GB/T 6436—2018, GB/T 6437—2018, and GB/T 18246—2019, as applicable (State Administration for Market Regulation and Standardization Administration of China, 2018a, 2018b, 2018c, 2019). Sodium was determined by atomic absorption spectrometry according to GB/T 13885—2017 (General Administration of Quality Supervision, Inspection and Quarantine of the People’s Republic of China and Standardization Administration of China, 2017), whereas water-soluble chloride, expressed as Cl, was determined according to GB/T 6439—2023 (State Administration for Market Regulation and Standardization Administration of China, 2023). Phytate phosphorus was determined using the ferric chloride precipitation method described by Wheeler and Ferrel (1971), and non-phytate phosphorus was derived from the analytically determined total phosphorus and phytate phosphorus values.

Experimental design and animal management

A total of 210 Guangxi Yao chickens at 30 d of age were randomly assigned to six dietary treatments, with five replicate cages per treatment and seven birds per replicate. Each replicate was housed in a single cage. Initial body weight did not differ among treatments (P > 0.05).

All birds were housed under identical environmental and management conditions. During the first week, the birds were maintained under a continuous 24-h lighting schedule. Thereafter, the daily photoperiod was reduced by 2 h each week until it reached 18 h, which was maintained for the remainder of the experiment. The room temperature was maintained at 32°C during the first week and subsequently decreased by 2–3°C per week until it reached 26°C. Relative humidity was maintained at 50%–60% throughout the experimental period. A 1-week adaptation period was implemented at the start of the trial; three birds showed transient poor condition and were isolated until recovery. During the trial, mash diets were offered, and feed and water were provided ad libitum; no medications were administered. The experimental period lasted 24 weeks. The composition and nutrient levels of the experimental diets are presented in Table 1.

Laying performance

Laying performance was recorded at the replicate level after the onset of lay and summarized for the laying period. Egg number was recorded daily for each replicate, and all eggs produced by each replicate were weighed using an electronic balance. Feed intake was recorded weekly as the difference between feed offered and feed refusals for each replicate. laying rate (LR, %) was calculated as total egg number divided by total hen-days and multiplied by 100. Average egg weight (AEW, g) was calculated as total egg weight divided by total egg number for each replicate. Average daily feed intake (ADFI, g/bird/d) was calculated as total feed intake divided by total hen-days. Feed-to-egg ratio (F:E, g feed/g egg mass) was calculated as total feed intake divided by total egg mass. Egg mass was calculated as the sum of the weights of all eggs produced by each replicate.

Egg quality

During weeks 20–24, eggs were collected daily from each replicate. For egg quality assessment, three normal eggs per replicate were randomly selected each week. Cracked, dirty, soft-shelled, misshapen, or otherwise abnormal eggs were excluded from egg quality measurements. For each replicate cage, the three eggs collected in a given week were averaged to obtain a weekly cage mean for each egg-quality variable. The five weekly cage means obtained from weeks 20 to 24 were subsequently averaged to generate a single overall value for each replicate cage. This cage-level value was used as the experimental observation in the statistical analysis. Eggshell thickness (EST, mm) was determined after the shell was separated from the albumen and yolk, rinsed to remove residual albumen, and air-dried. The eggshell membrane was removed before measurement. EST was then measured with a digital vernier caliper with an accuracy of 0.01 mm at the blunt end, pointed end, and equator, and the mean of repeated measurements was used. Albumen height (AH, mm) was measured using an albumen height gauge at the midpoint between the yolk margin and the outer edge of the thick albumen after the egg was broken. Yolk weight (YW, g) was determined after the yolk was separated from the albumen, gently blotted with filter paper to remove adhering albumen, and weighed using an electronic balance. Yolk color score (YCS) was assessed using the Roche Yolk Color Fan under consistent lighting conditions. Haugh unit (HU) was calculated from AH and egg weight using the equation: HU = 100 × log10 (H − 1.7W^0.37 + 7.57), where H is albumen height (mm) and W is egg weight (g). Yolk proportion (YP, %) was calculated as: YP = YW ÷ egg weight × 100. Egg length and width were measured using a digital vernier caliper with an accuracy of 0.01 mm, and egg shape index (ESI) was calculated as egg width ÷ length × 100. Egg quality data were averaged at the replicate level before statistical analysis.

Sample collection and processing

At the end of week 24, birds were fasted for 12 h with free access to water, and one bird with body weight close to the replicate mean was selected from each replicate for sample collection resulting in five sampled birds per treatment. Blood was collected from the neck vessels during exsanguination immediately after slaughter into serum separation tubes, and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, then stored at −20°C until analysis. The abdominal cavity was opened immediately after slaughter, and the ovary was carefully excised and rinsed several times with prechilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual blood. Large yellow follicles were removed before ovarian tissue sampling. For all birds, after removal of the large yellow follicles, ovarian tissue was consistently collected from the central stromal region of the residual ovary. Tissue samples were dissected on ice using sterile scissors and forceps and divided according to the subsequent analyses. Portions intended for RNA extraction and ovarian hormone measurements were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. Separate ovarian portions collected from the corresponding anatomical region were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding, sectioning, and histological examination.

Serum and ovarian hormones and immunoglobulins

Progesterone (P4), estradiol (E2), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) concentrations in serum and ovarian tissue homogenates, as well as serum immunoglobulins A (IgA), Y (IgY), and M (IgM), were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (MLBIO, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The catalog numbers were as follows: P4, ml103523; E2, ml855268; LH, ml300958; FSH, ml300959; AMH, ml005011; IgA, ml002792; IgY, ml042771; and IgM, ml890233. Although the manufacturer labels the ml042771 kit as a chicken IgG ELISA kit, the corresponding immunoglobulin is reported as IgY throughout this study for consistency with avian immunoglobulin nomenclature. Ovarian tissue samples were rinsed with pre-cooled PBS to remove residual blood, minced, and homogenized in PBS at a tissue-to-PBS ratio of 1:9 (w/v) on ice. The homogenates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for ELISA analysis. Ovarian hormone concentrations were calculated from the standard curves and expressed as concentrations in ovarian tissue homogenate supernatants. When samples were diluted before measurement, the final concentrations were corrected for the corresponding dilution factors. All standards and samples were assayed in duplicate. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated using a four-parameter logistic model.

Ovarian follicle classification and counting

Immediately after excision of the ovary and before removal of follicles for subsequent ovarian tissue sampling, all macroscopically visible follicles with diameters of at least 4.0 mm were counted. Follicles were classified (Johnson, 2015) according to color, diameter, and position within the follicular hierarchy as large white follicles (LWF, 4.0 to <6.0 mm), small yellow follicles (SYF, 6.0 to <8.0 mm), large yellow follicles (LYF, 8.0 to 12.0 mm), and hierarchical follicles (HF, >12.0 mm). Follicular diameters were measured using a digital caliper. Follicle counts were expressed as the number of follicles per ovary. One bird was sampled from each replicate cage, resulting in five birds per treatment, and each bird was considered an independent experimental unit. Follicle counting was performed at the terminal sampling point and was not synchronized to the individual ovulatory cycle.

Ovarian histological examination

Ovarian samples were collected from three biological replicates per treatment. Ovarian tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated using an automated tissue processor (Servicebio, ATD-405), embedded in paraffin (Servicebio, SWE-JBL5), and sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using dewaxing solution I and II followed by graded ethanol (100% to 75%). Staining was performed using an H&E kit (Servicebio, G1076). Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiated for 3–5 s, blued, and rinsed in running water, followed by eosin staining for 15 s. Stained sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared with n-butanol and xylene, and mounted using neutral balsam. Images were acquired using a bright-field microscope (Servicebio, SWE-CX63) to evaluate ovarian histomorphological features. Representative images were selected for presentation.

Ovarian transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

For ovarian transcriptomic analysis, three biological replicates were selected from each treatment group, resulting in a total of 18 ovarian samples. Total RNA was extracted from ovarian tissues. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, RNA integrity was examined by agarose gel electrophoresis, and the RNA quality number (RQN) was determined using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer. Only RNA samples with a total amount of at least 1 μg, a concentration of at least 30 ng/μL, an RQN greater than 6.5, and an OD260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.2 were used for library construction.

Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched from total RNA using oligo(dT)-coupled magnetic beads and fragmented into approximately 300-bp fragments. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from the fragmented mRNA using random primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was subjected to end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, fragment selection, and PCR amplification. After library preparation, high-throughput sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq platform. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sequencing Data Quality Control and Read Mapping. Raw sequencing reads were adapter-trimmed and quality-filtered using fastp (version 0.23.4) to obtain clean reads. Sequencing quality was evaluated based on the sequencing error rate, Q20 and Q30 values, and GC content. The clean reads were aligned to the specified Gallus gallus reference genome. Total mapping rates and uniquely mapped read rates were calculated for each sample to evaluate mapping quality. The mapped reads were subsequently assembled against the reference genome, and potentially novel transcripts were predicted by comparison with the reference annotation.

Gene and Transcript Expression Quantification. Gene- and transcript-level expression abundances were quantified using RSEM (version 1.3.3). Read counts were generated for each gene and transcript and were subsequently converted into transcripts per million (TPM) values to represent normalized expression abundance. TPM values were used to describe gene-expression distributions and compare expression profiles among samples.

Differential Expression Analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.42.0) based on the read-count data. Each PAR replacement group was compared separately with the CON group. P values were adjusted for multiple testing, and genes with an adjusted P value below 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes. No additional fold-change cutoff was applied, corresponding to the criterion of ∣log2FC∣≥1reported in the project report.

Functional Annotation and Enrichment Analysis. Differentially expressed genes were annotated using the Gene Ontology (GO; version 2023.07) database and classified into the biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; version 2023.09) database was used to annotate the metabolic and signaling pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes.

For each comparison between a PAR replacement group and the CON group, upregulated and downregulated genes were treated as separate gene sets for functional enrichment analysis. The whole transcriptome with the corresponding functional annotations was used as the background gene set. Fisher’s exact test was performed to evaluate the overrepresentation of differentially expressed genes in individual GO terms or KEGG pathways. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and GO terms or KEGG pathways with an adjusted P value below 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. GO enrichment analysis of genes/transcripts in the gene set was performed using Goatools software (Version 1.4.4), and KEGG pathways enrichment analysis was conducted using a custom script written in R (Version 4.5.1).

Representative GO terms and KEGG pathways were selected from each comparison to visualize and compare functional enrichment patterns across the five PAR replacement levels. For the side-by-side presentation of the five groups, the corresponding P values were used as a consistent visualization metric to indicate the relative enrichment significance of each term or pathway. These P values were used for graphical comparison and presentation rather than as an additional criterion for identifying differentially expressed genes.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software version 4.3.2 through the Majorbio Cloud Platform (Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The RSEM-derived gene-level TPM expression matrix of the 18 ovarian samples was used as the input dataset. For each pairwise comparison, genes were ranked using the Signal2Noise metric, GO and KEGG gene sets derived from the functional annotation results of the present transcriptomic dataset were used as the predefined gene sets. Only gene sets containing 15–500 genes were included.

Gene-set permutation was applied, with 1,000 permutations performed for each comparison. The enrichment score (ES), normalized enrichment score (NES), nominal P value, adjusted P value, rank at the maximum enrichment score, and leading-edge genes were calculated for each gene set. Positive and negative NES values represented enrichment toward the two opposite ends of the ranked gene list, respectively, and the group indicated in the GSEA output was used to determine the direction of enrichment.

Gene sets with an adjusted P value below 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. For visualization, gene sets from the two enrichment directions were ranked according to the absolute NES value, and the top 20 gene sets in each direction were displayed. When no permutation produced an enrichment score as extreme as the observed value, the nominal P value reported as 0 by the software was expressed as nominal P < 0.001 rather than P = 0.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA R package (version 1.63) through the Majorbio Cloud Platform (Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The RSEM-derived gene-level TPM expression matrix of the 18 ovarian samples was used as the input dataset. Before network construction, genes with a mean TPM value below 1 or a coefficient of variation below 0.1 across the 18 samples were removed.

A signed weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using a soft-thresholding power of 5. The adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix, and genes were hierarchically clustered according to topological overlap dissimilarity. Modules were identified with a minimum module size of 20 genes. The minimum module eigengene-based connectivity required for retaining a gene within a module was set to 0.2 (minKMEtoStay = 0.2), and modules with similar eigengene expression profiles were merged using a cut height of 0.15 (mergeCutHeight = 0.15). Using these parameters, two co-expression modules were identified, whereas genes not assigned to either module were placed in the grey set.

Module eigengenes were calculated to represent the overall expression profile of each module. Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the module eigengenes and the continuous phenotypic variables measured in the corresponding 18 samples. The PAR replacement level was entered as a continuous numerical variable according to the actual replacement proportion assigned to each treatment group. Other phenotypic variables included laying-performance indices, egg-quality traits, ovarian and serum hormone concentrations, and serum immunoglobulin levels.

Module-trait relationships were evaluated using Spearman correlation coefficients and the corresponding P values. Because WGCNA was conducted as an exploratory analysis and the number of biological replicates was limited, module-trait associations with a P value below 0.05 were considered potentially relevant rather than confirmatory. The turquoise module, which showed a nominal association with eggshell thickness, was selected for subsequent functional interpretation.

All genes assigned to the turquoise module were used as the target gene set for GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Enrichment significance was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test, and P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. GO terms and KEGG pathways with an adjusted P value below 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

For visualization of the co-expression network within the turquoise module, the 30 genes with the highest intramodular connectivity were selected as network nodes, and gene-gene connections with an edge weight greater than 0.02 were retained.

RT-qPCR validation

To evaluate the reliability of the RNA-seq data, eight differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected for reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) validation based on their involvement in major enriched biological pathways and their potential associations with key phenotypic traits. The selected genes included CD36, FABP4, and PCK1, which are involved in lipid transport and metabolism; IL6 and SPP1, which are associated with immune–inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling; PGR, which participates in hormone signaling; and MAPK10 and RPS6KA2, which are involved in MAPK signaling. The same 18 ovarian samples used for RNA sequencing, comprising three biological replicates per treatment group, were used for RT-qPCR validation.

Ovarian tissue samples (5–20 mg) were placed in grinding tubes containing 1 mL of RNA extraction reagent and three 3-mm zirconia grinding beads and homogenized using a three-dimensional cryogenic grinder. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and mixed with 100 μL of chloroform substitute. The samples were mixed thoroughly, incubated for 3 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Subsequently, 400 μL of the aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube and mixed with 550 μL of isopropanol. RNA was precipitated at −20°C for 15 min and collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The RNA pellet was washed twice with 1 mL of 75% ethanol, air-dried for 3–5 min, and dissolved in 15 μL of RNA dissolution buffer. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA samples were diluted to 200 ng/μL for reverse transcription.

First-strand cDNA was synthesized using SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for qPCR with One-Step gDNA Remover (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; catalog no. G3337) in a total reaction volume of 20 μL. The reaction mixture contained 4 μL of 5× SweScript All-in-One SuperMix for qPCR, 1 μL of gDNA Remover, 10 μL of total RNA, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 μL. Reverse transcription was performed in a conventional thermal cycler at 25°C for 5 min, 42°C for 30 min, and 85°C for 5 s.

Quantitative PCR was performed using 2× Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio; catalog no. G3326) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Each 15-μL reaction contained 7.5 μL of 2× qPCR Master Mix, 1.5 μL of primer mixture (2.5 μM), 2 μL of cDNA, and 4 μL of nuclease-free water. The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s. Melt-curve analysis was subsequently performed from 65 to 95°C, with fluorescence signals recorded at increments of 0.5°C to assess amplification specificity.

Each biological sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and the 0% PAR replacement group was used as the calibrator. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The expression patterns obtained by RT-qPCR were compared with the corresponding RNA-seq results. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS Statistics 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). When a significant treatment effect was detected, differences among treatment means were further evaluated using Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were used to evaluate the linear and quadratic responses to increasing dietary replacement levels of PAR. Results are presented as the mean and pooled standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Effects of PAR replacement on laying performance

In the present study, PAR replacement affected AEW, whereas LR and F:E did not differ among the dietary treatments. PAR10 had a higher AEW than CON. No significant differences in LR or F:E were detected among the treatments under the present experimental conditions.

Similar results have been reported in laying hens fed black soldier fly larvae meal. Zawisza et al. (2023) found that the inclusion of 5%, 10%, or 15% defatted Hermetia illucens meal as a partial replacement for soybean meal did not affect LR, egg weight, daily egg mass, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio. Bovera et al. (2018) also found that replacing 25% or 50% of soybean meal protein with H. illucens larvae meal did not affect egg weight, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio. The 25% replacement level increased LR and egg mass, whereas the 50% replacement level reduced dry matter, organic matter, and crude protein digestibility. Heuel et al. (2021) reported that black soldier fly larvae meal and fat could completely replace soybean cake and soybean oil while maintaining an LR of approximately 98%. These results indicate that insect-derived ingredients can maintain laying performance when the diets provide an appropriate nutrient supply.

The increase in AEW in the present study was mainly observed in PAR10, whereas the response was less evident at the higher replacement levels. This suggests that the nutrient balance of the PAR10 diet may have been more suitable for egg formation. Previous studies have reported different effects of insect meals on egg weight. Zawisza et al. (2023) found no change in egg weight when 5–15% defatted H. illucens meal was included in laying-hen diets. Heuel et al. (2021) found that larvae materials from different producers resulted in different egg weights. The commercial larvae material produced heavier eggs than the material obtained from small-scale production. Marono et al. (2017) found that the complete replacement of soybean meal with H. illucens larvae meal reduced AEW and egg mass. Dörper et al. (2024) also reported lower egg weight during the final 2 weeks in hens fed processed black soldier fly larvae meal and oil. Therefore, the effect of insect-derived ingredients on egg weight appears to depend on the ingredient source, processing method, inclusion level, and nutrient composition of the complete diet.

The supply of digestible amino acids may partly explain these differences. Heuel et al. (2021) found that the commercial larvae material supplied more digestible lysine and sulfur-containing amino acids than the small-scale material. It also resulted in higher egg weight, daily egg mass, and feed efficiency. Patterson et al. (2021) found that diets containing 8% or 16% black soldier fly larvae meal did not reduce laying performance. In contrast, the 24% diet had limited arginine and tryptophan contents and reduced feed intake, body weight, and egg production. Mwaniki et al. (2020) also found that the complete replacement of soybean meal with 10% or 15% defatted black soldier fly larvae meal did not affect hen-day egg production or feed intake, but egg mass decreased and feed conversion became less favorable. These findings indicate that diets with similar crude protein levels do not necessarily provide equivalent amounts of digestible amino acids for egg production.

The experimental diets were formulated to be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous. However, similar calculated metabolizable energy and crude protein levels do not necessarily ensure equivalent digestible amino acid supply, nutrient availability, or energy utilization. PAR was the insoluble residue obtained after repeated ethanol extraction of Periplaneta americana. Its nutrient composition and availability may therefore differ from those of whole insect meal. Ethanol extraction may remove part of the fat and other soluble compounds, while the remaining protein and structural components may also differ in digestibility. The higher AEW in PAR10 may be related to an appropriate balance between PAR and extruded soybean meal. Amino acid digestibility, metabolizable energy, and nutrient retention were not measured; therefore, this explanation requires further investigation.

ADFI showed significant linear and quadratic responses as the PAR replacement level increased, and PAR50 had the highest mean value. This result indicates that ADFI varied among the replacement levels, but the response was not uniform. Similar variations have been reported in other studies. Mwaniki et al. (2018) found that increasing defatted black soldier fly larvae meal from 0 to 7.5% linearly increased feed intake and feed conversion ratio. Hens fed 7.5% larvae meal maintained hen-day egg production and egg mass at levels similar to those of the control group, but their feed conversion ratio was less favorable. In contrast, Marono et al. (2017) found that the complete replacement of soybean meal reduced feed intake, LR, egg weight, and egg mass. Dörper et al. (2024) also found lower feed intake in hens fed black soldier fly larvae products. Patterson et al. (2021) reported no adverse effects at inclusion levels of 8% or 16%, whereas the 24% level reduced feed intake and egg production.

The different feed-intake responses may be related to the source and processing method of the insect material, the physical properties of the diet, feed acceptance, and nutrient balance. Heuel et al. (2021) found differences in feed acceptance among larvae products from different producers. Bovera et al. (2018) found that the diet containing 50% protein replacement reduced nutrient digestibility, which was associated with its higher chitin contribution. In the present study, the chitin content, particle size, amino acid digestibility, metabolizable energy, and palatability of PAR were not measured. These factors may have affected ADFI at the higher replacement levels.

The higher mean ADFI in PAR50 was not accompanied by a significant change in F:E. This suggests that the hens maintained feed utilization within a similar range across the dietary treatments. The increased ADFI at the highest replacement level may have helped the hens meet their nutrient requirements when a larger proportion of extruded soybean meal was replaced. Further measurements of nutrient digestibility, amino acid balance, metabolizable energy, feed preference, and PAR composition are needed to explain this response.

Overall, the partial replacement of extruded soybean meal with PAR did not adversely affect LR or F:E. Among the tested treatments, PAR10 resulted in the highest AEW, whereas further increases in the PAR replacement level did not produce additional improvements in laying performance. Among the tested levels, 10% replacement produced the clearest favorable response in average egg weight, whereas higher replacement levels provided no additional improvement in laying performance.

Effects of PAR on egg quality in Guangxi Yao chickens

Dietary replacement of extruded soybean meal with PAR had limited effects on egg quality in the present study. YW, YCS, YP, AH, HU, and EST did not differ significantly among treatments, and none of these variables showed significant linear or quadratic responses to increasing PAR replacement. ESI was the only egg-quality trait significantly affected by treatment. Therefore, the present results indicate that the principal internal egg-quality traits and EST were generally maintained across the tested replacement levels. Although YW, AH, and HU were numerically higher in PAR10, these differences were not statistically significant and should not be interpreted as evidence that low-level PAR replacement improved egg quality.

The overall stability of egg quality is broadly consistent with studies evaluating different forms of black soldier fly larvae in laying-hen diets. Montalbán et al. (2024) found that the incorporation of whole dried black soldier fly larvae into an alternative layer diet generally maintained egg-quality characteristics, although HU varied during an individual experimental period. Tahamtani et al. (2021) reported that providing live black soldier fly larvae did not affect egg weight, EST, eggshell breaking strength, or HU. Similarly, Cattaneo et al. (2025) found no substantial effects of live larvae supplementation on the principal egg and eggshell quality traits, although the yolk fatty acid profile was modified. Zhao et al. (2022) also found that long-term replacement of fish meal with defatted black soldier fly larvae meal did not alter eggshell weight, EST, eggshell strength, albumen weight, yolk height, YCS, or HU. Although these studies differed from the present experiment in insect species, ingredient form, and the conventional feed ingredient being replaced, they show that appropriately formulated diets containing insect-derived ingredients do not necessarily compromise the principal physical characteristics of eggs.

Nevertheless, the effects of insect-derived ingredients on individual egg-quality traits are not uniform. Khan et al. (2024) reported that replacing soybean meal with increasing levels of black soldier fly larvae meal increased eggshell strength, whereas AH and YW remained unchanged. Chen et al. (2025) observed greater EST at 3% inclusion and greater eggshell strength at 6% inclusion but found no significant changes in ESI, AH, HU, YCS, or YP. In contrast, Ruhnke et al. (2018) found that unrestricted access to dried larvae under free-range conditions was associated with lower egg weight, eggshell weight, and EST, as well as lower YCS. Romero et al. (2024) reported that defatted larvae meal did not affect EST or eggshell proportion but reduced YCS and HU at some inclusion levels. Secci et al. (2018) further showed that complete replacement of soybean meal with black soldier fly larvae meal altered YP, yolk pigmentation, carotenoid concentrations, and cholesterol content. Collectively, these findings indicate that responses of individual egg-quality traits depend on insect species, processing method, inclusion level, feeding system, experimental diets composition, and the nutrient or feed ingredient being replaced.

PAR differs substantially from the intact, dried, defatted, or live insect materials used in most previous studies. It is the insoluble fraction remaining after repeated ethanol extraction of Periplaneta americana. The extraction process may remove or reduce part of the lipids, pigments, minerals, and other ethanol-soluble constituents of the original insect material. Therefore, the absence of significant changes in YCS and EST in the present study should not be regarded as inconsistent with studies reporting greater pigment deposition or improved eggshell traits after the inclusion of intact insect meal or insect oil. Rather, it suggests that the residual components of PAR, under the present dietary formulation, were sufficient to maintain the measured egg-quality traits but did not produce clear pigmenting or eggshell-enhancing effects.

ESI was lower in PAR20 than in CON, PAR10, and PAR30, whereas PAR40 and PAR50 showed intermediate values. However, ESI exhibited neither a significant linear nor a significant quadratic response to increasing PAR replacement. The response was therefore confined to a single treatment rather than representing a consistent dose-dependent effect. Kowalska et al. (2021) demonstrated that dietary protein source can affect ESI and other egg characteristics and that these responses may interact with hen age. In the present study, however, the isolated reduction in ESI in PAR20 was not accompanied by changes in YW, YP, AH, HU, or EST. It should consequently be interpreted as a difference in external egg geometry rather than evidence of a general deterioration in egg quality. The current measurements do not provide sufficient information to determine the biological basis of this isolated response.

EST was numerically lower in all PAR groups than in CON, but the overall treatment effect and the linear and quadratic contrasts were not significant. It would therefore be inappropriate to state that PAR reduced EST. Moreover, eggshell quality is a multidimensional characteristic involving EST, eggshell breaking strength, eggshell weight, eggshell proportion, mineral composition, and ultrastructural organization. Because only EST was measured in the present study, the experiment cannot determine whether PAR affected eggshell mechanical strength or mineralization. Future studies should include eggshell breaking strength, eggshell weight and proportion, eggshell ash, calcium and phosphorus retention, mineral digestibility, and eggshell ultrastructure.

An additional methodological consideration is that cracked, dirty, soft-shelled, misshapen, and otherwise abnormal eggs were excluded before egg-quality measurements. Consequently, the present data describe the characteristics of apparently normal eggs and cannot establish whether PAR replacement affected the incidence of abnormal eggs. Recording the proportions of cracked, soft-shelled, shell-less, and misshapen eggs would provide a more complete assessment of external egg quality and commercial acceptability.

Overall, replacing up to 50% of extruded soybean meal with PAR maintained YW, YCS, YP, AH, HU, and EST in the normal eggs evaluated. The reduction in ESI in PAR20 was not accompanied by a dose-dependent response or deterioration in other egg-quality traits. Thus, PAR did not exert a clear adverse effect on the principal egg-quality variables under the present experimental conditions.

Effects of PAR on hormones and immunoglobulins in Guangxi Yao chickens

Dietary PAR replacement changed several reproductive hormones and serum immunoglobulins, but the responses differed among replacement levels. Ovarian E2 was higher in PAR40 than in PAR50, while the other groups showed intermediate values. Serum LH was higher in PAR40 than in CON, PAR10, and PAR50. Serum FSH was highest in PAR30, and PAR20 was also higher than PAR10. Serum AMH was highest in PAR10 and lowest in PAR30. Ovarian P4, LH, FSH, and AMH did not differ among groups. Serum P4 and E2 also showed no significant overall treatment effects, although serum P4 and FSH showed significant quadratic responses. Serum IgA, IgY, and IgM were all highest in PAR50 and showed significant linear and quadratic responses. These results show that PAR affected the reproductive endocrine system and humoral immune indices, but the effects did not follow the same dose-response pattern.

Follicular development in hens is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis and local factors in the ovary. LH, FSH, E2, and P4 act at different stages of follicular growth, selection, maturation, and ovulation. Follicle selection is also linked to changes in granulosa-cell sensitivity to gonadotropins and the start of steroid production (Hernandez and Bahr, 2003; Rangel et al., 2014; Johnson, 2015; Johnson and Lee, 2016). In the present study, PAR40 had higher serum LH than CON, PAR10, and PAR50. Ovarian E2 was also higher in PAR40 than in PAR50. However, serum E2 and ovarian LH did not differ among groups. LR also remained unchanged. Thus, the changes in PAR40 may reflect a change in endocrine regulation or ovarian steroid metabolism, but they do not show that follicular maturation or ovulation was improved.

The serum FSH response was different from the LH response. Serum FSH was highest in PAR30, while ovarian FSH did not differ among groups. FSH promotes the growth of prehierarchical follicles and increases granulosa-cell proliferation and FSH responsiveness. It also supports the expression of steroidogenic genes and P4 production during follicle selection (Hernandez and Bahr, 2003; Johnson and Lee, 2016; Ghanem and Johnson, 2019). Ma et al. (2020) found that FSH treatment promoted prehierarchical follicle development, follicular angiogenesis, cell proliferation, steroid synthesis, and yolk deposition in low-yield laying chickens. These studies show that FSH is important for follicular development. However, a higher serum FSH concentration alone does not show that more follicles were recruited. Serum FSH may also change because of ovarian feedback, hormone use by target tissues, or the distribution of follicles at different stages. In this study, LR, ovarian FSH, and the numbers of HF, LYF, SYF, and LWF were unchanged. Therefore, the higher serum FSH concentration in PAR30 should be interpreted as a difference in circulating endocrine status rather than evidence of enhanced follicular recruitment.

AMH is mainly expressed in the granulosa cells of small follicles in hens. Its expression is generally high in small prehierarchical follicles and decreases as follicles grow and enter the preovulatory hierarchy (Johnson et al., 2008, 2009). AMH and AMHR2 are linked to FSH responsiveness during follicle selection. BMP6 can increase both FSHR and AMH expression in granulosa cells from prehierarchical follicles, which also shows that AMH acts together with other local ovarian signals (Ocón-Grove et al., 2012). Huang et al. (2021) found that AMH affected follicular development in a concentration-dependent way. A high AMH treatment reduced ovarian and hierarchical follicle weight and inhibited P4 production, while a lower dose produced a different response. Francoeur et al. (2024) found that lowering AMH expression in granulosa cells did not change FSHR, STAR, CYP11A1, or BMPR2 expression or the response to FSH. These results show that AMH is related to follicular stage, but its role in hen follicular development is not controlled by one simple pathway.

In the present study, serum AMH was highest in PAR10 and lowest in PAR30, while ovarian AMH did not differ among groups. This pattern did not show a linear or quadratic response. The higher serum AMH in PAR10 may be related to the number or activity of small follicles, AMH secretion, or hormone clearance. The lower value in PAR30 may reflect a different follicular distribution or endocrine feedback. However, serum AMH alone cannot show which follicle class changed. The lack of a difference in ovarian AMH also limits the interpretation. Future studies should measure AMH and AMHR2 expression in follicles of different sizes.

Serum P4 showed a significant quadratic response, but the overall treatment effect was not significant. P4 and LH change rapidly during the ovulatory cycle. Plasma P4 rises before ovulation, and its concentration depends strongly on the time between blood sampling and ovulation or oviposition (Kappauf and van Tienhoven, 1972). The effect of P4 on LH release also changes with the stage of the ovulatory cycle (Wilson and Sharp, 1975). Hierarchical follicles have different roles in P4 production and the preovulatory LH surge (Rangel et al., 2014). Changes in P4, E2, and ovulation have also been observed when ovarian steroid synthesis was experimentally altered in laying hens (Sekiguchi and Imai, 1988). In the present study, blood was collected once at the end of the trial and was not matched to the oviposition time of each hen. Differences in the stage of the ovulatory cycle may therefore have increased variation in serum P4 and other hormones.

The immunoglobulin response was clearer than the hormone response. Serum IgA, IgY, and IgM were all highest in PAR50. IgA also increased across the middle and high replacement groups, while the large increases in IgY and IgM were mainly found in PAR50. This pattern shows that the highest PAR replacement level changed the humoral immune status of the hens. A similar effect has been reported in Sanhuang chickens. Zhao et al. (2025) found that PAR replacement increased IgA, IgG, and IgM in serum and spleen and also changed several cytokines and antioxidant indices. However, the responses in that study were not proportional to every increase in PAR level. This supports an immune-regulating effect of PAR, but it also shows that the response can depend on the replacement level and the physiological stage of the birds.

Tao et al. (2025) also found that PAR replacement increased IgA in the duodenum and jejunum and increased IgM and IgG in the ileum of pre-breeding Nandan Yao chickens. These results were obtained from intestinal tissues rather than serum, but they show that PAR can affect immune indices in more than one tissue. Other insect-derived feeds have produced similar changes. Full-fat black soldier fly larvae meal increased ileal mucosal sIgA in layer chicks (Chu et al., 2020). Microwave-dried black soldier fly larvae meal increased IgA and IgY in broilers (Sjofjan et al., 2025). Dietary COS also increased serum immunoglobulins and antibody titers against avian influenza and Newcastle disease in laying hens (Youssef et al., 2023). These studies show that insect-derived nutrients or structural compounds can affect humoral and mucosal immune responses in poultry.

PAR is different from intact insect meal and purified COS. It is the insoluble material left after repeated ethanol extraction. It may contain residual proteins, structural carbohydrates, chitin-related fractions, and other compounds, but the amounts of these components were not measured in the present study. Thus, the higher immunoglobulin concentrations cannot be linked to one specific PAR component. They may result from several components acting together or from changes in nutrient digestion and intestinal immune signaling.

Higher serum immunoglobulin concentrations should be described as a humoral immune response rather than direct proof of stronger disease resistance. The present study did not include vaccination, pathogen challenge, lymphocyte analysis, or clinical disease records. High immunoglobulin concentrations can reflect greater antibody production, but they can also occur after greater antigen exposure or immune stimulation. The ovarian transcriptomic results also showed changes in immune-related pathways, including the intestinal immune network for IgA production, phagosome, chemokine-related processes, and responses to inflammatory signals. At the same time, some immune-related genes and pathways were downregulated. This suggests that PAR changed immune regulation rather than increasing all immune functions in the same direction.

The hormone and immunoglobulin results also agree in part with the ovarian transcriptomic changes. PAR40 showed changes in PPAR signaling, calcium signaling, ECM–receptor interaction, and immune-related pathways. PAR50 showed changes in PPAR signaling, oxidative-stress-related processes, arachidonic acid metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis. These pathways are related to lipid use, cell signaling, tissue structure, steroid metabolism, and immune responses. However, pathway enrichment does not show a direct cause of the serum or ovarian changes. The transcriptomic results should therefore be used as support for possible biological processes rather than as proof of a specific mechanism.

Overall, PAR replacement caused level-specific changes in reproductive hormones and serum immunoglobulins. PAR10 had the highest serum AMH, PAR30 had the highest serum FSH, and PAR40 had the highest serum LH and a higher ovarian E2 concentration than PAR50. PAR50 had the highest serum IgA, IgY, and IgM. These hormone changes were not accompanied by a higher LR. Moderate PAR replacement may affect endocrine feedback related to follicular development, while high replacement may produce a stronger humoral immune response. More work is needed using blood sampling matched to the ovulatory cycle, ovarian hormone receptors and steroidogenic enzymes, immune-cell analysis, and vaccination or pathogen-challenge tests.

Effects of PAR replacement on ovarian follicle numbers

In the present study, dietary PAR replacement did not significantly affect the numbers of HF, LYF, SYF, or LWF. No significant linear or quadratic responses were detected for any follicle category. Numerically, HF and LYF numbers were highest in the PAR30 group, whereas SYF and LWF numbers were highest in the PAR20 group. However, these numerical differences were not statistically significant. Nie et al. (2022) reported approximately 11.83 LWF, 6.83 SYF, and 1.00 LYF per ovary in laying hens. The mean LWF, SYF, and LYF counts observed in the present study were within a broadly similar numerical range to those reported by Nie et al. (2022). However, follicle counts may be influenced by breed, age, follicle-classification criteria, and the stage of the ovulatory cycle at sampling. Under the present experimental conditions, no statistically detectable differences were observed in the numbers of the four predefined categories of macroscopically visible follicles.

Follicular development in laying hens is tightly regulated. Prehierarchical follicles develop from LWF to SYF, and one follicle is usually selected each day from the SYF pool to enter the preovulatory hierarchy (Johnson, 2015; Wang et al., 2017). This selection process helps maintain a relatively stable number of hierarchical follicles. Changes in reproductive hormones do not always cause an immediate change in follicle numbers. The lack of significant differences across the four predefined follicle categories indicates that no treatment-related alteration in the counts of macroscopically visible follicles was statistically detected at the terminal sampling point. However, this finding does not establish equivalence among treatments and cannot exclude smaller changes in follicular recruitment, selection, maturation, or atresia. However, the present data cannot exclude cellular or molecular changes in follicular recruitment, selection, or maturation.

Serum FSH was highest in the PAR30 group, and this group also had numerically higher numbers of HF and LYF. During follicle selection and early growth, granulosa cells become increasingly responsive to FSH and acquire greater steroidogenic capacity. Exogenous FSH has also been shown to promote prehierarchical follicle growth, granulosa-cell proliferation, and yolk deposition (Johnson and Lee, 2016; Ma et al., 2020). In the present study, ovarian FSH concentration, follicle counts, and LR did not differ significantly among treatments. Therefore, the higher serum FSH concentration observed in PAR30 should be interpreted as a change in circulating endocrine status rather than as evidence of increased follicular recruitment. The numerically higher HF and LYF counts in PAR30 did not reach statistical significance.

Serum AMH was highest in the PAR10 group, but the numbers of LWF and SYF were not significantly increased. FSHR and AMHR2 are expressed at relatively high levels in small prehierarchical follicles, and AMH may participate in follicle selection by regulating follicular responsiveness to FSH in a concentration-dependent manner (Huang et al., 2021). Serum AMH concentration alone cannot therefore be used as a direct measure of the number of small follicles. Serum LH was higher in PAR40, and ovarian E2 was higher in PAR40 than in PAR50. However, PAR40 did not have significantly more LYF or HF, and laying rate was not improved. These hormone differences were not accompanied by statistically significant differences in LYF or HF counts or by an improvement in LR.

The effects of dietary nutrients on follicle numbers can differ according to the ingredient and its active components. Long et al. (2017) found that dietary octacosanol increased the numbers of LWF and SYF and altered serum reproductive hormones and hormone-receptor expression in ovarian follicles. In the present study, PAR replacement changed several reproductive hormones but did not significantly change follicle numbers. The different responses between the two studies may be related to differences in ingredient composition, active components, dietary dose, breed, and physiological stage. Although changes in several reproductive hormones were observed in the present study, the data do not establish whether PAR acted through endocrine regulation, metabolic alterations, or local ovarian responses. PAR is a post-extraction residue, and its nutrient profile and residual active components differ from those of a purified bioactive compound.

The follicle-count data should also be considered together with the ovarian histological findings. Ovarian architecture was generally preserved in the CON, PAR10, PAR20, and PAR30 groups, which agrees with the stable follicle numbers. Relatively prominent stromal regions were observed in some PAR40 sections, and separation-like spaces within the follicular contents were observed in some PAR50 sections. These changes were not accompanied by a significant reduction in follicle numbers. Follicle counting reflects the number of macroscopically visible follicles, whereas histological and transcriptomic analyses reflect local tissue and cellular changes. Stable follicle numbers therefore do not exclude local changes in tissue structure, metabolism, or cellular status. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that PAR treatment was associated with biological processes related to lipid metabolism, PPAR signaling, ECM–receptor interaction, and oxidative stress. These molecular responses may reflect alterations in local ovarian metabolism or cellular status, but they do not constitute direct evidence of follicular damage.

Overall, PAR replacement did not markedly disturb the distribution of ovarian follicles at different developmental stages. The unchanged follicle numbers and laying rate, together with the generally preserved ovarian architecture at low-to-moderate replacement levels, suggest that 10%–30% PAR replacement did not exert clear adverse effects on follicular stage distribution or ovarian morphology under the present experimental conditions. PAR replacement at 40%–50% likewise did not significantly reduce follicle numbers or laying rate, but it did not confer clear reproductive benefits and was accompanied by several descriptive histological changes that require quantitative verification. Only five birds per treatment were used for follicle counting, and sampling was performed at a single time point. Future studies should standardize sampling according to the ovulatory cycle and measure follicular atresia, FSHR expression, and AMHR2 expression to clarify the effects of PAR on follicular recruitment and selection.

Effects of PAR on ovarian histomorphology in Yao chickens

In the present study, follicles at different developmental stages were observed in all treatment groups, and the follicular walls and surrounding stromal tissues remained identifiable. The representative sections from the CON, PAR10, PAR20, and PAR30 groups generally showed preserved ovarian architecture and multiple developing follicles. The chicken ovary normally contains follicles at different developmental stages because follicle recruitment, growth, selection, ovulation, and regression occur continuously during the laying period. Follicles of different sizes and developmental states have also been identified in histological studies of native chickens (Johnson, 2015; Mohd et al., 2024). Thus, the preserved architecture observed at 0–30% PAR replacement indicates that low-to-moderate replacement did not cause obvious structural disruption of the ovary under the present experimental conditions.

At higher replacement levels, relatively prominent stromal regions were observed in some sections from the PAR40 group, while separation-like spaces within the follicular contents were present in some PAR50 sections. These changes were not observed throughout the entire ovary, and the follicular wall remained identifiable in all groups. A more prominent stromal region may reflect a local change in the relative distribution of follicular and connective tissues or an increase in tissue remodeling. The separation-like spaces in PAR50 may also reflect localized changes in the organization of the follicular contents. However, these features alone are not sufficient to identify follicular atresia. Follicular atresia in chickens is a complex process that involves structural changes in granulosa and theca cells, reduced cell proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, and gradual removal of follicular contents (Mohd et al., 2024; Ru et al., 2024). Therefore, the present findings are more appropriately interpreted as localized morphological changes at high PAR replacement levels rather than direct evidence of extensive follicular damage.

Several cellular processes are known to participate in the maintenance and regression of chicken ovarian follicles. Oxidative stress can promote granulosa-cell apoptosis and follicular atresia. Bao et al. (2022) found that naringin reduced oxidative stress and helped maintain small white follicles in aging laying hens. Endoplasmic reticulum stress has also been detected in atretic small white follicles and can reduce granulosa-cell proliferation while increasing apoptosis (Huang et al., 2022). In addition, atretic large white follicles show reduced oxidative phosphorylation, altered mitochondrial dynamics, and increased apoptosis, which indicates that mitochondrial energy production is important for normal follicular maintenance (Hu et al., 2025). These studies show that follicular structure is sensitive to oxidative, metabolic, and cellular stress. These mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study, so they should be regarded as biological background rather than a direct explanation for the changes observed in PAR40 and PAR50.

The ovarian response may also be related to the effective nutritional value of PAR. Although the experimental diets were formulated to be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous, similar formulated nutrient levels do not always provide the same amounts of digestible amino acids and available energy. This difference may become more important when the replacement level increases. PAR is the insoluble residue remaining after repeated ethanol extraction and is therefore different from intact P. americana meal and other insect meals in nutrient composition and digestibility.

Studies of alternative protein ingredients in laying hens have shown that reproductive and productive responses depend on the ingredient, processing method, inclusion level, and nutrient balance. Lu et al. (2023) reported that 4% fermented soybean meal or fermented miscellaneous meal increased relative ovarian weight and the numbers of large white and small yellow follicles in aged laying hens. However, 8% fermented miscellaneous meal reduced productive performance. Attivi et al. (2023) found that replacing dietary fish meal with black soldier fly larvae meal at levels equivalent to up to 8% of the diet did not significantly affect follicle number, while ovarian weight and circulating reproductive hormone concentrations increased with the replacement level. These findings show that a properly balanced alternative protein ingredient can maintain or support reproductive development, but the response cannot be extended directly from one ingredient to another.

High inclusion levels of insect-derived ingredients may also alter nutrient utilization. Complete replacement of soybean meal with black soldier fly larvae meal reduced feed intake and several production indices in laying hens, although feed conversion showed a different response (Marono et al., 2017). In another study, complete replacement reduced the apparent digestibility of dry matter, organic matter, and crude protein and changed intestinal morphology and digestive enzyme activity (Cutrignelli et al., 2018). Bovera et al. (2018) also found that replacing 50% of soybean meal protein with black soldier fly larvae meal reduced dry matter, organic matter, and crude protein digestibility, whereas the 25% replacement level produced more favorable laying responses. These results do not directly describe the effects of PAR, but they show that the biological response to insect-derived protein may change when the replacement level becomes high.

Overall, ovarian architecture was largely maintained when PAR replaced 0–30% of extruded soybean meal. The relatively prominent stromal regions in some PAR40 sections and the separation-like spaces in some PAR50 follicles suggest that localized ovarian changes may occur at high replacement levels. Because the present histological evaluation was descriptive, the biological importance of these changes cannot be determined from H&E staining alone. Further studies should include quantitative counts of follicles at different stages, standardized follicular-atresia scoring, measurements of granulosa-cell and follicular-wall thickness, collagen staining, TUNEL analysis, and markers of oxidative stress and mitochondrial function. These measurements would help determine whether the observed features represent normal variation, active ovarian remodeling, or an early reduction in follicular maintenance capacity.

Effects of PAR on the ovarian transcriptome in Yao chickens

The ovarian transcriptomic changes observed in the present study indicate that lipid handling and metabolic reprogramming may be important components of the biological differences between the experimental groups. In particular, the differential expression of genes such as CD36, FABP4, and PCK1 suggests coordinated changes in fatty-acid utilization, intracellular lipid transport, and carbon-substrate metabolism. Transcriptomic analysis of chicken granulosa cells has shown that genes related to ribosomal function, lipid metabolism, and protein synthesis are progressively upregulated during the transition from prehierarchical to hierarchical follicles (Zhu et al., 2019). Broader transcriptomic and chromatin analyses have also demonstrated extensive stage-dependent regulatory reprogramming across chicken folliculogenesis, including marked changes among prehierarchical, preovulatory, and postovulatory granulosa cells (Li et al., 2022). These findings support the interpretation that the metabolic differences identified in the present study may reflect changes in follicular developmental activity rather than isolated alterations in individual metabolic genes.

Previous functional studies further support a close relationship between ovarian lipid metabolism and steroidogenesis. Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyses showed that PPAR signaling and glycerolipid metabolism were altered during chicken sexual maturation, while LPL silencing reduced lipid-droplet accumulation and the expression of steroidogenic genes in granulosa cells (Cui et al., 2022). In goose granulosa cells, FABP4 silencing significantly reduced E2 and P4 secretion and decreased the expression of CYP11A1 and CYP19A1. FABP4 overexpression significantly increased E2 and the expression of both steroidogenic genes, whereas the increase in P4 did not reach statistical significance (Fan et al., 2021). Therefore, the expression pattern of FABP4 in the present study may indicate altered ovarian lipid handling and steroidogenic capacity. Nevertheless, gene-expression data alone cannot establish changes in lipid flux or hormone production, and this interpretation requires validation through lipid-metabolism and steroid-hormone measurements.

The enrichment of signaling pathways related to follicular development should also be interpreted as evidence of coordinated regulatory activity rather than as proof that a single pathway determines the observed phenotype. Silencing SLC5A5 in chicken granulosa cells inhibited cell proliferation and steroid-hormone synthesis while promoting apoptosis, demonstrating that granulosa-cell metabolism, survival, and steroidogenesis are functionally interconnected (Shen et al., 2024). WIF1 has also been shown to regulate chicken granulosa-cell proliferation and progesterone production through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Nie et al., 2024). In addition, the differentiation of avian granulosa cells involves stage-dependent changes in gonadotropin responsiveness and intracellular signaling networks (Johnson and Woods, 2009). Recent transcriptomic profiling further identified stage-specific changes in PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, Wnt signaling, and glycerophospholipid metabolism during early chicken follicle development (Wadood et al., 2026). Collectively, these studies support the view that the signaling pathways identified in the present analysis may participate in follicular growth, granulosa-cell differentiation, and steroidogenic regulation, but they do not establish a direct causal mechanism for the phenotypic differences between groups.

The GSEA results additionally indicated changes in immune-response and extracellular-matrix-related processes. However, enrichment of these pathways should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of pathological inflammation or ovarian injury. In chickens, postovulatory follicle regression is accompanied by increased expression of several cytokines and chemokines, immune-cell infiltration, apoptosis, and tissue remodeling, indicating that inflammatory-like signaling is a physiological component of follicular regression (Sundaresan et al., 2008). Accordingly, the immune- and extracellular-matrix-related signatures identified here may represent coordinated remodeling associated with follicular maturation, ovulation, or regression. Without histological evidence, circulating inflammatory markers, or direct measurements of ovarian immune-cell infiltration, the present results cannot distinguish normal reproductive remodeling from pathological inflammatory activation.

WGCNA identified a turquoise module showing a nominal negative association with eggshell thickness (r = −0.486, P = 0.0409). This moderate correlation indicates a statistical association but does not demonstrate that the ovarian module directly regulates eggshell formation. Because eggshell mineralization occurs primarily in the uterus, the identified ovarian genes may instead reflect endocrine, metabolic, or reproductive-stage signals that are indirectly associated with uterine function. Consistent with this interpretation, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of uterine tissues from Yaoshan chickens and their crossbreeds identified calcium signaling, neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, arachidonic-acid metabolism, and prostaglandin-related gene–metabolite pairs as processes associated with eggshell-quality differences (Miao et al., 2025). Therefore, genes within the turquoise module should be regarded as components of a broader ovary–uterus regulatory network that may be associated with eggshell-related phenotypes rather than as direct determinants of eggshell thickness.

Overall, the present results suggest that the observed ovarian transcriptomic differences involve an integrated network of lipid metabolism, steroidogenesis, intracellular signaling, immune-related remodeling, and extracellular-matrix organization. CD36, FABP4, PCK1, and genes within the turquoise module may therefore serve as candidate molecular indicators of ovarian metabolic and functional status. However, the current transcriptomic evidence is primarily associative. Future studies should combine ovarian and uterine gene expression with measurements of E2, P4, lipid composition, relevant enzyme activities, follicular-stage distribution, and eggshell-mineralization indices. Although large yellow follicles were removed and ovarian tissues were collected from a consistent anatomical region, the remaining samples still contained heterogeneous ovarian components. Therefore, the bulk RNA-seq results could not distinguish transcriptional changes among specific ovarian cell types or follicular stages. Functional interference or overexpression experiments in granulosa cells, together with cross-tissue correlation or mediation analyses, will be required to determine whether these candidate genes contribute causally to reproductive and egg-quality traits.

Conclusion

Under the present experimental conditions, no statistically significant treatment effects were detected for laying rate or feed-to-egg ratio. PAR10 increased average egg weight relative to CON and PAR20, whereas higher replacement levels provided no additional performance benefit. ADFI showed significant linear and quadratic responses and was numerically highest in PAR50. Most egg-quality traits were not significantly affected, although ESI was lower in PAR20. Hormonal, immunoglobulin, histological, and ovarian transcriptomic responses varied among inclusion levels. Because the histological evaluation was descriptive and the RNA-seq and WGCNA analyses included only three biological replicates per treatment, these findings should be regarded as exploratory. The present data identify 10% replacement as the level with the clearest production response but do not establish an optimal replacement range or justify recommending replacement up to 50%.
