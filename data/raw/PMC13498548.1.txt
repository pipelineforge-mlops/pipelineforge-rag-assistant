It has been 4 years since we launched npj Mental Health Research as a venue for high-quality and innovative research on mental health. We are very grateful to our hard-working editorial staff, prestigious editorial board members, pioneering authors, and expert reviewers who have volunteered their time to support the journal to reach its current success. This year (2026), we were excited to learn that the journal achieved a first impact factor of 9.1, making it one of the top 10 journals in Psychiatry by Journal Impact Factor (JIF). The Journal’s articles were downloaded over one million times in 2025, further demonstrating its reach and is a credit to everybody’s work. This milestone arrives at a moment when artificial intelligence (AI) and intelligence machines are rapidly transforming the field we serve and it is impossible to ignore their emergence. These technologies are poised to reshape mental health research, care delivery, and the very nature of human experience. While AI offers unprecedented tools for diagnosis, prediction, and intervention, it also raises profound questions about what aspects of behavioral health care will remain fundamentally human. Therefore, we take this opportunity to reflect with our readership about the role of these technologies on the future of mental health and the importance of our adaption and evolution to truly keep “humans-in-the-loop.”

Opportunities to be more human

As a foundational background, we posit that AI has the potential to allow humans the time and energy to focus on being human. AI is going to automate many routine, repetitive, and even cognitively demanding tasks in healthcare, from documentation to diagnostics. Inevitably, more sophisticated systems, including advanced large language models, swarms of AI agents, and autonomous robotics, will assume higher-order responsibilities. This shift will bring workforce displacement across sectors and deepen existing digital divides between those who can harness these technologies and those who cannot. These developments warrant careful preparation as a society, and therein lies our humanity. How can we ensure these technologies maximize benefits for human lives? Could AI liberate humans from drudgery and allow us greater investment in human flourishing? Will there be a future in which humans can engage in more relational and meaning-making activities?

These are important questions that we must contend with and prepare for as a human civilization as we learn to co-exist with AI. Not every technology has been or will be positive, and they will not automatically lead to deeper humanity. One challenge for our field is to study and research how technological progress can best serve human mental health.

Intelligence does not equate to human connection

Decades of research have documented the importance of human connection for mental health, whether as a buffer against stress or by instilling meaning and purpose in life1–3. As Albert Einstein observed, “Only a life lived for others is a life worthwhile.”4 Humans uniquely emote, empathize, and motivate one another in ways that machines cannot fully replicate. Intelligence does not automatically grant the ability to connect with living beings. An AI may be able to simulate compassion, but it does not feel genuine joy at a patient’s recovery, share vulnerability in a therapeutic alliance, or convey the subtle nonverbal cues that build trust. Mental health, at its core, is a human domain. Many mental health difficulties often stem from deep relational wounds involving other humans that require human presence to heal. Our emotions, behaviors, and sense of self largely emerge in response to other humans. Preserving and enhancing these critical human interactions should remain a central priority as we integrate AI and develop new AI-human relationships. There will be great ways to develop robot-human interactions in our future co-existence without losing sight of the importance of other humans. In npj Mental Health Research, we have published studies that demonstrate the value of deep human interactions for advancing behavioral health5–7, and we will continue to do so.

Power of human collectives

In addition to individual human connections, there are unique and compelling features of human collectives that AI may not be able to replace, although could certainly disrupt. Peer support programs, group therapies, and community-based interventions, for example, continue to demonstrate robust effects3,8,9, that algorithms or robotics alone may not be able to match. Throughout history, humans have organized themselves into various collectives, as families, neighborhoods, companies, etc. and so we should expect that human inclination to continue, while respecting the AI innovations that are to come or researching ways for AI to facilitate human collectives. Of course, there are numerous promising directions to be studied, some written about by our authors, such as hybrid models that leverage AI for scale while centering human connection, investigations into how AI might augment human empathy and therapeutic presence, and examination of the long-term societal impacts of AI on group dynamics and networks10–12.

AI is infinite, humans are finite

AI is recursive, continuously learning, has an ever-increasing memory, and will evolve with no biological decay. In contrast, humans are limited by time and energy; it is our finitude that makes our time precious and our lives cherished. As AI increasingly integrates into our lives, our contrast with AI can sharpen awareness of what makes us human and connect us to our existential identity, our role in the world, and our response to loss and changes due to AI. Certainly, part of what makes us human is our mortality and our mental health is intertwined with our awareness of biological death. As the famous anthropologist Ernest Becker stated, “the idea of death… is the mainspring of human activity.”13 This is a reality that we need to continue considering in the future of human mental health.

Humans need to design the guardrails

We certainly cannot be Pollyannish and ignore the risks of AI. Humans are needed to judge how, what, and where to design the guardrails for AI. Many experts have already expressed concerns about algorithmic biases, data privacy, and surveillance systems14–16. As AI develops, there will be evolving new concerns that we cannot yet anticipate and some will be in the realm of mental health. AI can drive isolation or erode authentic human connections; it may widen health disparities or lead to dehumanized care. If AI displaces large segments of populations, there may be rises in purposelessness, depression, or “deaths of despair” unless societies proactively create new avenues for contribution and meaning. Even if there is extreme abundance in the future, there may be unintended consequences to consider in a world with endless choices and less scarcity to drive ambition and desire. All these are critical cultural, moral, and ethical issues that will need to be decided by humans, and not AI. In the field of mental health, we must push for operating frameworks that keep humans in the loop- not just as supervisors, but as an irreplaceable core of human mental health and well-being.

Conclusion

In closing, this discussion of “impact factor” to “human factor” reflects our journal’s evolving mission. As AI reshapes the landscape, npj Mental Health Research will continue to champion rigorous science that harnesses technology while elevating what makes us distinctly human: connection, empathy, creativity, and shared meaning. We expect journal submissions related to AI will continue to grow in the coming years as the AI revolution ramps up. We welcome high-quality work in this area, but are committed to publishing not only technical AI studies, but also research that examines the necessary human elements of mental health and mental health services. We invite researchers, clinicians, and thinkers to contribute work that explores these topics, especially from interdisciplinary perspectives and methodologies. Ultimately, our greatest innovations may not be the machines we build, but the humanity we preserve and expand through them.
