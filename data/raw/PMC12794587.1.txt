Purpose:

To evaluate organs-at-risk (OARs) segmentation variability across eight commercial AI-based segmentation software using independent multi-institutional datasets, and to provide recommendations for clinical practices utilizing AI-segmentation.

Methods:

160 planning CT image sets from four anatomical sites: head-and-neck, thorax, abdomen and pelvis were retrospectively pooled from three institutions. Contours for 31 OARs generated by the software were compared to clinical contours using multiple accuracy metrics, including: Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), 95 Percentile of Hausdorff distance (HD95), surface DSC, as well as relative added path length (RAPL) as an efficiency metric. A two-factor analysis of variance was used to quantify variability in contouring accuracy across software platforms (inter-software) and patients (inter-patient). Pairwise comparisons were performed to categorize the software into different performance groups, and inter-software variations (ISV) were calculated as the average performance differences between the groups.

Results:

Significant inter-software and inter-patient contouring accuracy variations (p<0.05) were observed for most OARs. The largest ISV in DSC in each anatomical region were cervical esophagus (0.41), trachea (0.10), spinal cord (0.13) and prostate (0.17). Among the organs evaluated, 7 had mean DSC >0.9 (i.e., heart, liver), 15 had DSC ranging from 0.7 to 0.89 (i.e., parotid, esophagus). The remaining organs (i.e., optic nerves, seminal vesicle) had DSC<0.7. 16 of the 31 organs (52%) had RAPL less than 0.1.

Conclusion:

Our results reveal significant inter-software and inter-patient variability in the performance of AI-segmentation software. These findings highlight the need of thorough software commissioning, testing, and quality assurance across disease sites, patient-specific anatomies and image acquisition protocols.

1. Introduction

The segmentation of tumor and normal organs is a crucial first step in the treatment planning for radiation therapy (RT). Automation of this process has been instrumental in alleviating the burden of contouring and enhancing efficiency, accuracy, and consistency[1–3]. AI-based segmentation methods have been successfully applied to most treatment sites including the brain[4], head-and-neck[5], lung[6], cardiac[7], abdomen[8], pelvis[9], and extremities[10]. Various vendors have begun to incorporate AI algorithms into their clinical planning systems or have developed standalone AI-based segmentation platforms. At the initiation of this study, there were multiple commercially available platforms that provide automated AI-based segmentation for organs-at-risk (OARs) and/or clinical target(s). These platforms provide ready-to-use models for specific organs, enabling users to perform segmentation without extensive training. However, detailed information about the underlying network architectures of these models is often unknown to end users. This lack of transparency makes it challenging for users to understand the inner workings of the algorithms and evaluate their suitability for specific clinical scenarios.

Additionally, limited information is available regarding the performance metrics of these algorithms. It is crucial for clinicians and researchers to know the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and other performance indicators of the segmentation models to assess their reliability and make informed decisions. Moreover, the clinical implementation limitations of these pre-trained models are not adequately documented. Factors such as applicability to different patient populations, variations in image quality, and potential biases in the training data are essential considerations for deploying these algorithms in real-world clinical settings. The enhanced understanding of these AI segmentation platforms would also provide significant benefits to clinical implementation in radiation therapy. Consistent and accurate segmentation across different institutions participating in multi-center trials would ensure the robustness of trial results, eliminating one of the potential sources of variability.

The performance of AI segmentation should be validated and thoroughly evaluated prior to its clinical implementation [1, 11–13]. This evaluation is a multi-factorial process, requiring the uses of multiple metrics to quantify the similarities or differences from the ground truth contours [12]. Commonly used performance categories include accuracy, efficiency, and dosimetric impact [1, 11–14]. Both qualitative and quantitative metrics should be employed, as each validation metric targets specific aspects of contour performance (i.e., volume- or distance-based evaluation). A combination of evaluation approaches and metrics is recommended for comprehensive performance evaluation [1]. Ensuring the accuracy and efficiency of these AI platforms in organ contouring will not only streamline clinical workflows but also enhance the quality assurance process in radiation therapy.

Previous studies have evaluated the performance of multiple AI segmentation platforms [3, 15–18]. Most of these studies reported comparable accuracy across software without rigorous statistical analysis [15, 19–21]. One study [22] performed one-way ANOVA test on data from a single-institution and found no significant differences for most organs. However, the generalizability of such findings is limited due to variations in contouring practices across institutions [23, 24]. AI models frequently struggle with cross-institution performance differences, which can be influenced by differences in patient anatomy, image quality, implanted devices, and contouring conventions [21, 23, 24]. These observations highlighted the need for multiple-institutional data for a comprehensive and unbiased evaluation. This study aimed to address the gap by conducting a rigorous statistical analysis of inter-software and inter-patient variability.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Evaluation data set

The evaluation dataset consisted of CT scans from 160 diverse cancer patients, with 40 cases from each of four anatomical sites including head-and-neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. The data were gathered from three different institutions: Virginia Commonwealth University, University of California Los Angeles, and Mayo Clinic Arizona, which contributed 20, 10 and 10 cases, respectively, for each anatomical site. These cases were randomly selected from the clinical database from patients treated during the previous 5 years at each institution. Internal Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from each institution to share the de-identified data. For each case, normal organ contours deemed clinically necessary for planning were manually delineated by or under the supervision of the treating radiation oncologist at the respective institutions for treatment planning according to each institution’s standard clinical guidelines. While most of the images for abdominal and thorax sites were average intensity projection (AIP) images derived from 4D CTs, the rest were 3D or single phase 4DCT images. The imaging protocols represented a wide spectrum of typical CT protocols and types, including scans with oral and intravenous contrast and without contrast. The scans also represented a range of image quality in terms of spatial resolution, ranging from 0.8–1.3 mm in-plane corresponding to the field-of-view from 40 cm to 65 cm and 1–3 mm slice thicknesses, some of which included image artifacts typically seen in CT simulation studies.

Prior studies have shown that the differences in image acquisition protocols and quality can be a source of the inter-institutional contouring accuracy differences. To better understand these differences, we measured the image contrast for an organ by computing the difference between the mean Hounsfield unit (HU) value inside the OAR and within an 8 mm-wide ring surrounding the OAR, which is equivalent to an image voxel size of 6–10. We plotted the inter-institution comparisons of the CT acquisition protocols, including image contrast, slice thickness, and resolution in Figure A.1 in Supplemental File A.

Normal organs commonly evaluated in radiation therapy planning were contoured automatically on these datasets using the eight commercially available software platforms listed in Table A.1 in Supplementary File A. These AI-based contouring platforms and their versions were selected due to their availability to the authors at the time of analysis. The manual contours were used as the ground truth when evaluating the accuracy of the AI contours. The number of contours available for each organ and software are listed in Table B.1 in Supplementary File B. As the numbers of modeled organs varied across different software platforms, we only considered the organs that were segmented by at least two AI platforms and with available manual contours for at least 20 (50%) of cases. In total, contouring accuracy and variations for 31 representative OARs (15 from head-and-neck, 6 from thorax, 4 from abdomen, and 6 from pelvis) were analyzed. Serial OARs (i.e., esophagus and spinal cord) spanning more than one anatomical region were analyzed separately for each region.

2.2. Auto segmentation algorithms

All auto-segmentation platforms utilize certain type of deep learning neural networks (DLNN). While U-Net is at the core of all platforms, several variants are also implemented, including an ensemble of neural networks (Carina, TheraPanacea) and Generative Adversarial Network (Syngo.via). In addition, common to many platforms are a pre-segmentation network to identify a proper region-of-interest and a post-processing step to reduce contouring artifacts. These software platforms also differ in how they are integrated into the workflow. Some implementation features of the platforms are listed in Table A.1. All software platform evaluated in this study have received FDA 510(k) clearance. More specifics information about each software and evaluation results for some individual software have been published elsewhere [17, 18, 23, 25–29].

2.3. Contouring accuracy and efficiency metrics

2.3.1 Accuracy metrics

Auto-segmentation accuracy was evaluated by comparing the agreement between the AI and manually delineated clinical contours. A comprehensive set of geometrical metrics were calculated to quantify contour agreement, such as volume, distance and surface-based metrics [1, 12, 14]. These metrics are listed in Table 1. They are complementary and represent different aspects of contouring accuracy. These metrics were calculated in 3D using Python “surface-distance” module by google DeepMind (https://github.com/google-deepmind/surface-distance).

2.3.2 Efficiency metrics

The added path length (APL) was utilized to assess the potential efficiency afforded by AI segmentation, as this metric has been shown to correlate well with the time spent to manually edit contours [30, 31]. The APL is defined as the length of the AI contour that is more than a certain threshold distance (i.e., 3 mm in this study) away from the ground truth [30]. Since the absolute APL depends on the volume of the organ, we further calculated a relative APL (RAPL), which is the ratio between the APL and the total contour length of the organ on all slices.

2.4. Statistical analysis of contouring accuracy

The quantitative contouring metrics were analyzed across all platforms in three steps: 1) evaluating organ-specific contouring accuracies, 2) assessing the significance of variations in contouring accuracy, and 3) performing pairwise comparisons of contouring accuracy.

In the first step, the mean and standard deviation of the contouring accuracy metrics were pooled across all software platforms and all patients to generate a single value for each OAR. This step was used to identify OARs for which commercial software performed well and, more importantly, those that yielded poor contouring accuracy.

Variation in contouring accuracy could be affected by either the software’s performance or the organ’s imaging characteristics across different patient cases, such as geometrical complexity, proximity to nearby organs and image contrast, etc. To distinguish between these two factors and identify organs showing significant variability among software platforms, a two-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to test the significance of contouring accuracy differences across software platforms (inter-software comparison) and across patients (inter-patient comparison). A p-value less than 0.05 indicated significant differences. For bilateral organs, such as kidneys and femoral heads, the left and right organs were pooled together in the ANOVA tests. In addition to the p-values, the Root Mean Square for the software (RMSs) and for the patient (RMSp) were computed for each organ, quantifying variability attributable to software and patient differences, respectively. The Tukey procedure evaluates all pair-wise differences while adjusting p-values for multiple comparisons [32]. This method provides narrower confidence intervals compared to other multiple comparison procedures, when testing all pairs of software. The inter-software variation (ISV) is defined as the difference between the better and worse metrics, which indicates the spread of the contouring accuracy across the software packages.

2.5. Overall ranking of the software based on all metrics

We further evaluate the performance of software platforms by assessing how many organs contoured by each software were ranked in either the best or worst performance groups, based on a combined assessment of all contouring accuracy metrics. Since the number of organs evaluated varied across different anatomical sites, we calculated the ratio of the number of organs in either group to the total number of organs evaluated for each anatomical site to enable comparison across different sites.

3. Results

3.1 Contouring accuracy of specific organs

The distribution of accuracy metrics for each organ are shown as boxplots in Figure 1(a)-(d) for representative accuracy metrics including DSC, HD95, SrfD2 and RAPL, respectively, with separate plots for each anatomical region. These statistics are also shown in Table B.2 in Supplementary File B. Among the 31 organs, 7 had mean DSC larger than 0.9, including brain, lung, while 15 had DSC ranging from 0.7 to 0.89 (e.g., brainstem, spinal cord). The remaining organs, (e.g., optic nerves and seminal vesicle) had DSC less than 0.7. In term of HD95, 12 of the 31 OARs had mean HD95 less than 5mm, including lens, eye, etc. The results for SrfD2 were lower in value than DSC, but generally matched the trend of DSC.

The eight software platforms were regrouped into the better/worse/equivalent performance groups for each organ based on pairwise comparisons. The detailed processes of the comparisons for some example organs are shown in Supplemental File A. The better/worse performance metrics extracted from the two groups respectively are plotted in Figure 1 as green and red dots. While the box plots show the combined variation of metrics over all software and patients, the separation between the better and worse metrics represents the variation of the contouring accuracy among the software only. In general, statistically significant variations (p<0.05) in contouring consistency across the software were observed for 28 organs (90%) in term of DSC, 28 organs (90%) in term of HD95, and 25 organs (81%) in term of SrfD2. The organ that had the largest ISV in DSC in each anatomical region (i.e., head-and-neck, thoracic, abdominal, and pelvic) was cervical esophagus (0.41), trachea (0.10), spinal cord (0.13) and prostate (0.17), respectively. These four organs also had the largest ISV within each region in term of SrfD2: cervical esophagus (0.49), trachea (0.14), spinal cord (0.17) and prostate (0.21), respectively. In term of HD95, the organ that had the largest ISV for each site is: optic nerves (0.35 cm), trachea (0.45 cm), stomach (0.29 cm) and prostate (0.51 cm), respectively.

As shown in Supplementary Table B.2, most of the organs had statistically significant inter-patient variations (p<0.05) in terms of the three contouring accuracy metrics. The root mean square per patient (RMSp) is one indication of the inter-patient variability. The organ which had the largest RMSp in term of DSC in each region is: oral cavity (0.35) for H&N, trachea (0.24) for lung, stomach (0.25) for abdomen and seminal vesicle (0.34) for pelvis.

To identify the influence of the poor performing software on the analysis of ISV, ANOVA testing was repeated for the software after excluding data in the worse performing group. As shown in Table B.3, the p-values increased and the RMS decreased which indicated less inter-software variation (i.e., ISV). While 19 organs still showed statistically significant ISV in term of DSC, some organs, such as optical nerve, trachea and stomach, no longer had statistically significant ISV within the group after excluding the worse performance software. These organs were generally the ones with lower DSC in each anatomical site.

The RAPL represents the portion of AI contour which need to be manually edited. As shown in Fig. 2(d) and the Supplementary Table B.2, 16 of the 31 organs (52%) had RAPL less than 0.1, indicating less than 10% of the contours required edits of >3mm for the majority of organs, which implied the time saving by using AI-segmentation. Note that the heart had a relatively high mean DSC value of 0.91, however 15% of its contours required edits to align with the manual contours within a 3mm margin.

Figure 2 shows examples of the AI-generated contours by the eight software platforms and the manual contours from each of anatomical sites on the CT images, including a representative case of parotids, esophagus, liver, and bladder. Significant contouring accuracy variations were clearly seen across the software platforms. It is worthwhile to point out that not only the contouring variations, but also the location of inaccuracy may have subsequent dosimetric impact.

3.2 Overall ranking of the software based on all metrics

Figure 3 shows the relative numbers of organs contours in the best and worst performance groups to the total number of the organs evaluated for each anatomical site using the combined contouring accuracy metrics. The order of the software in the figure has been re-arranged to avoid disclosing the names of the software. The software #6 appears to have the highest proportion of contours in the best performing group and software #7 has the highest proportion in the worst-performing group.

4. Discussion

AI-based segmentation platforms are rapidly increasing in use in radiation treatment planning and other tasks. While prior studies have evaluated the contouring accuracy metrics among various platforms, this is the first study to systematically compare multiple commercial platforms using an independent multi-institutional dataset across different anatomic sites. Significant inter-software and inter-patient performance variations were identified by a two-factor ANOVA. Incorporating inter-patient variation into the statistical analysis increased the sensitivity of the ANOVA test [33]. In addition, we used the ISV to quantify the variability between the software, instead of the sample range commonly used in prior studies. Range is a unreliable estimate when outlier is present [34]. This allowed us to have statistically robust estimation of variability. In fact, our study indicted statistically significant contouring accuracy variations (p<0.05) in 80% to 90% of organs across the software. The significant inter-patient variations for some organs corroborated the importance of case-specific QA tests. It also necessitates a cautious approach in reviewing and editing each case by clinicians [11].

In general, the variation in DSC increased across software platforms as the mean DSC and organ volume decreased. In addition, organs lacking a well-defined caudal/cephalad location (e.g., spinal cord, esophagus, trachea, larynx) often demonstrated large inter-software variation in DSC. Excluding poorly performing software from the ANOVA comparisons could only mitigate some of these significant differences across software. However, it should be noted that our study did not pinpoint a specific software that consistently performed better or worse for all OARs.

As an example of the new findings from this multi-institutional study, we compared the ISV of DSC obtained from this study with the ranges reported in two prior multiple software, multiple anatomical sites, single institutional studies [15, 21](Table A.2 in Supplementary file A). Only the organs which have been studies in all three studies are listed in the table. Note that not the same set of software were evaluated in the three studies, although there are some overlaps between them. As shown in the table, while it appears that the organs which have the largest inter-software variability tend to be the same groups of organs as those with the largest inter-software range in prior studies, e.g., lens, spinal cord and prostate in head/neck, thorax/abdomen and pelvic regions, respectively, the DSC from our study are significantly lower than prior studies for a number of organs, including parotids, esophagus, spinal cord and prostate. It is possibly due to the variability of CT imaging protocol (e.g., different slice thickness), patient anatomy and treatment techniques (e.g., use of hydrogel spacer) from multiple institutional data.

Significant inter-patient variability also existed for most OARs. As shown in Appendix Table B.2, while the inter-patient variation RMSp was generally smaller than inter-software variation RMSs, larger RMSp were seen for some organs. Two primary reasons may account for the elevated RMSp: First is the different contouring criteria adopted at the three institutions, as in the case of the oral cavity. On the other hand, the contouring criteria employed by different software exhibited closer similarity, potentially influenced by shared public data or collective adoption of a common contouring standard. Another contributing factor was observed in organs that were challenging to define due to shape and contrast variations, such as the stomach and seminal vesicle.

The HD95 was shown to be independent of DSC and provided additional information about the distance of discrepancy between the contour and ground truth. Variations in HD95 across software tended to be larger than 0.5 cm for most organs. Only organs with clearly defined perimeters such as brain, eye, spinal canal, and lung achieved HD95 less than 0.5cm.

In reviewing these commercial software packages, we noticed great variations in the documentation of the details of the AI models. Of particular importance to clinical users are details such as contouring guidelines used for each structure, training data size and diversity, and most importantly how they validated and tested their model. It has been shown that AI model suffers from lack of generalizability when tested on data from different institutions[23, 24]. Finally, some of the discrepancies observed in the superior/inferior extent of serial organs could have been due to variations in defining border (e.g., cervical esophagus). Vendors should provide such information to users upfront to ease adoption.

This study had the following limitations. While we used the clinically used manual organ contours as the ground truth, variability across clinicians and institutions are well-documented [3]. There have been numerous studies to investigate inter-observer variabilities (IOV) in diverse contours spanning across various disease sites[35–38]. Despite diligent endeavors to mitigate these discrepancies, discernible variations persist. The documented inter-observer variability pertaining to each individual contour can serve as an informative gauge, reflecting the attainable average precision of the contour in question. Moreover, the juxtaposition of auto-segmentation outcomes with inter-observer variabilities can function as an efficacious measure for evaluating the segmentation platform [3, 39]. The IOV for some example organs compiled from multiple publications are listed in the Supplemental Table A.3 [39–46]. It can be seen from the table that the large inter-software variation for cervical esophagus and abdominal spinal cord may reflect the large IOV for these organs. For other organs, the AI contours are actually in better agreement with the manual contours than the IOV. This indicates the benefit of AI contours to reduce contouring variations.

The evaluation was confined to a subset of organs supported across all eight commercial platforms, and for which manual contours were available. Furthermore, a sample of 40 cases were collected per anatomic site. This sample size is unlikely to capture the full spectrum of patients encountered in clinical practice, such as those with resected organs, or presenting with implants and/or treatment aids. Secondly, our current analysis focused on the geometric agreement metrics and revealed statistically significant inter-software and inter-patient variation for most organ. The quality of contours can be further assessed in terms of the dosimetric impact on treatment, which may carry more direct clinical significance. Some organs, such as the brain and lung, demonstrated very small RMSp and RMSs (<0.05), leading to the question of whether the observed variation would have dosimetric or clinical impact. Also, certain special clinical cases were not included in the dataset and thus not evaluated. For example, some patients have one kidney. The AI platforms should be evaluated to assess if certain algorithms produce contours even in the absence of organs. Our next step will be to delve into the dosimetric impact of contouring variations. Lastly, the readiness of integration of the AI platforms to existing treatment planning workflows was not explicitly evaluated. Instead, each platform was evaluated by various authors given the availability of the AI-segmentation platform at their institutions.

APL was used in this study as a metric for contouring efficiency. However, as APL was calculated slice-wise, it may not accurately represent the actual contouring editing time in some cases. Its accuracy depends on the method used for delineation, e.g., axial contouring vs. 3D contouring, or the use of interpolation algorithms, etc. In certain cases, the 3D distance-based metrics may serve as more accurate indicators of editing time.

Differences in CT imaging protocols (Supplemental Figure A.1) and the contouring conventions across institutions may have resulted in contouring accuracy variability. Prior study has found that higher DSC generally correlates with higher organ volume [47]. We also observed that lower HD95 is associated with higher organ contrast and smaller slice thickness in our data. However, as the primary goal of this study was to evaluate the performance and variability of AI contouring software, data from multi-institution were combined. As a result, inter-institution variabilities were incorporated into the inter-patient variation in the two-factor ANOVA analysis. As pointed out above, including inter-patient variation in the statistical analysis enhanced the sensitivity of statistical tests.

As AI algorithms and AI segmentation platforms continue to evolve rapidly, it is essential to note that the current study serves as a “snapshot” in a period of fast development. New organ-specific AI models and improvements for existing ones may emerge from vendors. However, our findings revealed performance variability stemming from intrinsic problems related to patient anatomical complexity and the quality of CT images. These challenges are likely persist with newer releases. There is no consistent trend indicating if any one software platform is superior and consistently outperforms the others. The performance of AI models varies depending on the organ, anatomical site, imaging modality, and acquisition protocols. The goal of our study was not to identify the superior platform but to demonstrate the variability across platforms when applied in clinical datasets. This underscores the necessity for careful acceptance testing, commissioning and QA procedures for AI platforms, along with human review for every AI-segmented case.

5. Conclusions

AI-based software demonstrates great promise in improving accuracy and consistency in segmentation tasks for radiotherapy. Factors such as organ volume, imaging acquisition protocol, specifically resolution, slice thickness and image contrast across organ borders, may affect the accuracy of AI generated contours. While introduction of standards for the quality and acquisition of input data may help to address these issues, human intervention remains critical at this time. Physicians and physicists need to review contours prior to planning, particularly for unusual cases where the anatomy is atypical.

The methodology and data presented may guide clinical physicists and clinicians in evaluating AI for broader clinical implementation. Caution should also be taken in AI-model development and commissioning of AI-based segmentation tools. The process of widespread deployment and adherence to standards should be transparent and endorsed by the community. Importantly, the data used to train and validate these systems must represent the diverse populations they aim to serve [48].

The recommendations for the model development and implementation of AI-segmentation are summarized in Table 2 with the detailed findings to supporting these recommendations.

  4. Standard contouring guidelines need to be followed and training AI model from multiple-institutional data may reduce this variation.
  5. AI models should be constantly evaluated for bias and must reflect the broadest possible distribution of people.
  6. Rigorous review of radiotherapy structures for multicenter clinical trial quality assurance is essential in the era of wide adoption of AI platforms.
  7. Publicly available AI data sets are suggested to provide access for those interested in the confirmation of models’ validity and robustness.	• This lack of transparency of AI-segmentation algorithms on commercially available platforms makes it challenging for users to understand the inner workings of the algorithms and evaluate their suitability for specific clinical scenarios.
• AI contour accuracy varied with imaging quality.
• AI-model accuracy varied with image acquisition parameters, such as organ contrast, slice thickness and in-plane resolution.
• Variations of AI contour accuracies may also result from different contouring protocols or guidelines.
• The most important feature affecting DSC was organ volume, while contrast and slice thickness were the two top features affecting HD95.	
	
Implementation of AI-models		
  1. The process of broad deployment and adherence to standards must be transparent and accepted by the community.
  2. Extensive commissioning and testing of AI-based auto-segmentation platforms should be performed for implementation, including across image modalities, disease sites, patient specific anatomy, and applicable other clinical scenarios.
  3. A rigorous QA program, including software maintenance, update and monitoring of AI-segmentation platforms should be established.
  4. Multiple metrics are recommended to quantify the similarities/differences from the ground truth contours. This consensus recommends accuracy metrics from both volume-based metrics (i.e., DSC) and distance-based metrics (i.e., HD95, SrfD2) followed by expert review.
  5. Contour variations characterized by accuracy metrics, as well as the location of inaccuracy of the organ, should be considered for clinical implementation of AI software.
  6. Significant inter-software and inter-patient variations in AI-segmentation highlight the need for thorough software commissioning, testing, and quality assurance across disease sites, patient-specific anatomies, image acquisition protocols, and various clinical scenarios.
  7. Human review is recommended for every AI-segmented case.	• Quantitative evaluation metrics are available, but they are not completely independent. Multiple metrics, such as DSC, HD95, SrFD2, should be used to assess contour accuracy.
• Variation of contour accuracy depended on different anatomical sites and organs across different AI-based software platforms. Among the 31 organs evaluated, 7 organs had mean DSC >0.9 (e.g., including brain, lung, heart, liver, kidney, femoral head, and bladder, etc.), while 15 organs had DSC ranging from 0.7 to 0.89 (e.g., brainstem, spinal cord, parotid, esophagus, stomach and rectum). The remaining organs, (e.g., optic nerves and seminal vesicle) had DSC less than< 0.7. In term of HD95, 12 of the 31 OARs had mean HD95 less than 5mm, including lens, eye, spinal canal, spinal cord and femoral heads, etc.
• Statistically significant inter-patient contouring accuracy variability existed for most of the organs. The organ which had the largest Root Mean Square for the patient in term of DSC in each region is: oral cavity (0.35), trachea (0.24), stomach (0.25) and seminal vesicle (0.34).
• Statistically significant inter-software contouring accuracy variations were observed for most organs in term of DSC, HD95, and SrfD2. The organ that had the largest ISV in DSC in each anatomical region (i.e., head-and-neck, thoracic, abdominal and pelvic) was cervical esophagus (0.41), trachea (0.10), spinal cord (0.13) and prostate (0.17), respectively. The organ that had the largest ISV in HD95 for each site is: optic nerves (0.35 cm), trachea (0.45 cm), stomach (0.29 cm) and prostate (0.51 cm), respectively.
