Abstract

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, reflecting the global epidemic of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. However, MASLD is not a homogeneous condition; rather, it encompasses a broad clinical and biological spectrum ranging from simple steatosis to progressive steatohepatitis, advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. This heterogeneity is driven by complex interactions between metabolic dysfunction, genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and extrahepatic comorbidities. Recent advances have shifted the paradigm from a “one-size-fits-all” approach toward risk stratification and personalized care. Non-invasive tests (NITs), including FIB-4, transient and shear wave elastography, and novel biomarkers, have enabled scalable fibrosis assessment and prognostic stratification in real-world settings. At the same time, emerging therapies targeting metabolic pathways, inflammation, and fibrosis are paving the way for individualized treatment strategies. This review provides an updated and clinically oriented synthesis of MASLD by integrating clinical heterogeneity, phenotype-based disease trajectories, multidimensional risk stratification, and personalized management into a practical framework for precision hepatology. In addition, we summarize recent therapeutic advances, discuss current controversies and implementation challenges, and highlight future directions toward individualized care.

MASLD as a Systemic and Heterogeneous Disease

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) should no longer be regarded as a simple disorder of hepatic fat accumulation. The contemporary view is broader and more clinically meaningful: MASLD is a systemic disease arising from the interaction between disordered energy metabolism, adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and individual susceptibility factors. In this framework, the liver is not an isolated target organ, but rather a central hub within a complex metabolic network linking the gut, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, pancreas, kidney, and cardiovascular system. This concept is now strongly reflected in recent international guidance, which places MASLD within the wider context of cardiometabolic disease rather than treating it as a purely hepatic entity.1,2

The systemic nature of MASLD is important for at least two reasons. First, liver injury in MASLD is closely intertwined with extrahepatic morbidity. Patients frequently carry a substantial burden of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, atherogenic dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and cardiovascular disease. In many cohorts, cardiovascular events and non-hepatic malignancies account for a large proportion of long-term mortality, often exceeding liver-related deaths in earlier disease stages.3,4 Second, these extrahepatic disorders are not merely comorbid conditions occurring in parallel; they participate directly in disease initiation and progression through shared mechanisms such as insulin resistance, lipotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, endothelial injury, and systemic inflammation. Thus, MASLD should be approached as a multisystem disorder with hepatic and extrahepatic consequences, not as an incidental radiologic finding of steatosis. The global burden of MASLD continues to increase in parallel with obesity and type 2 diabetes, creating substantial healthcare and economic challenges worldwide. Beyond liver-related morbidity, MASLD contributes considerably to cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and healthcare utilization. Increasing evidence also suggests important differences according to sex and ethnicity. Premenopausal women generally exhibit a lower prevalence than men, whereas the risk increases markedly after menopause. Furthermore, Asian populations frequently develop MASLD despite a relatively low BMI, highlighting the importance of visceral adiposity rather than obesity alone.

A major challenge in MASLD is its pronounced clinical heterogeneity. The term MASLD encompasses a broad histologic and prognostic spectrum ranging from isolated steatosis with relatively low short-term liver-related risk to steatohepatitis, progressive fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Yet even this spectrum does not fully capture the heterogeneity observed in practice. Two patients with apparently similar body mass index, glycemic status, and aminotransferase levels may have markedly different liver phenotypes and long-term outcomes. One may remain metabolically stable with non-progressive steatosis for years, whereas another may experience silent fibrosis progression and present only when advanced liver disease has already developed. This divergence illustrates a central point: MASLD is not a single disease with a uniform course, but a syndrome composed of multiple biological and clinical trajectories.

This heterogeneity is driven by several interrelated layers. The first is metabolic heterogeneity. Although obesity and type 2 diabetes are strong drivers of disease burden, MASLD is not confined to patients with overt obesity. In Asian populations in particular, substantial disease may occur in individuals with lower body mass index but increased visceral adiposity, sarcopenia, insulin resistance, and adverse body fat distribution. The concept of “lean MASLD” is therefore clinically relevant, especially in settings where body mass index alone may underestimate metabolic risk. In these patients, the absence of overt obesity can lead to delayed recognition despite meaningful fibrosis risk. The second layer is genetic heterogeneity. Variants in genes such as PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and MBOAT7 help explain why some individuals accumulate liver fat and develop inflammation or fibrosis disproportionate to their apparent metabolic burden.4,5 The third layer is environmental and behavioral heterogeneity, including dietary pattern, sedentary lifestyle, sleep disturbance, alcohol consumption even below traditional thresholds, and gut microbiome-related influences. Together, these factors shape not only disease susceptibility but also the tempo and direction of progression.

Another important source of heterogeneity lies in the dissociation between biochemical activity and structural liver injury. Aminotransferase levels may be normal in patients with significant fibrosis, while some patients with elevated ALT may not have advanced disease. Likewise, the amount of steatosis does not linearly predict fibrotic burden, and the presence of metabolic dysfunction does not translate into equivalent hepatic risk across all individuals. This is one reason why a purely liver enzyme-based or imaging-based approach is inadequate. In real-world practice, the clinically relevant question is not simply whether steatosis is present, but which patient is likely to progress, decompensate, or develop liver-related and extrahepatic complications. Fibrosis remains the most important histologic determinant of liver-related outcomes, but even fibrosis should be interpreted within a wider systemic context.6,7 The same fibrosis stage may not carry identical risk in all patients. For example, the prognostic meaning of moderate fibrosis may differ between a young patient with isolated metabolic dysfunction and an older patient with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, central obesity, and persistent inflammatory activity. In other words, hepatic staging remains essential, but it is not sufficient on its own. A patient-centered interpretation of MASLD must integrate liver fibrosis with age, diabetes status, cardiometabolic burden, renal function, and competing cardiovascular risk. This is precisely why contemporary guidelines emphasize case-finding in populations with metabolic risk factors and recommend the use of non-invasive tools to identify those with clinically significant fibrosis rather than focusing only on steatosis detection.

From a conceptual standpoint, MASLD can therefore be understood as the hepatic manifestation of broader metabolic ill health, modified by genetics, environment, and time. This interpretation has important implications for clinical care. It explains why the traditional “one-size-fits-all” model is inadequate; why many patients require multidisciplinary management beyond hepatology alone; and why risk stratification must move beyond the binary presence or absence of fatty liver. It also supports a more nuanced clinical language: rather than describing MASLD as a homogeneous diagnosis, it is more accurate to think in terms of phenotypes, trajectories, and individualized risk profiles. Such a view naturally leads to the next two pillars of contemporary care—risk stratification and personalized management.

For Asian countries, including Vietnam, this systemic and heterogeneous view is particularly relevant. The coexistence of rising obesity, increasing type 2 diabetes, persistent viral liver disease burden, variable alcohol exposure, and a substantial proportion of non-obese but metabolically unhealthy individuals creates a uniquely complex clinical landscape. In this context, MASLD is unlikely to behave as a uniform disease entity. Instead, it will present across multiple phenotypes, often with overlapping metabolic and liver-specific risk factors. A concise way to summarize this section is that MASLD should be viewed through three complementary lenses: as a liver disease, because fibrosis and cirrhosis remain central determinants of hepatic outcomes; as a systemic metabolic disease, because extrahepatic morbidity and mortality substantially shape prognosis; and as a heterogeneous syndrome, because patients differ markedly in biological drivers, disease trajectory, and therapeutic needs. This tripartite view provides the conceptual foundation for the remainder of this review, which integrates clinical heterogeneity, phenotype-driven disease trajectories, multidimensional risk stratification, and personalized management into a practical framework for precision hepatology. Although several recent reviews have addressed individual aspects of MASLD, including epidemiology, non-invasive fibrosis assessment, or emerging therapies, relatively few have integrated these concepts into a unified clinical framework. In this review, we emphasize that understanding MASLD requires linking disease heterogeneity with phenotype-specific trajectories, multidimensional risk stratification, and individualized management. By integrating these interconnected concepts, we aim to provide a practical framework that bridges current evidence with everyday clinical decision-making and supports the transition toward precision hepatology.

Clinical Phenotypes and Disease Trajectories

One of the most clinically relevant advances in MASLD is the recognition that patients do not follow a single, predictable disease course. Instead, MASLD is better understood as a spectrum of distinct but overlapping clinical phenotypes, each characterized by different biological drivers, progression patterns, and risk profiles. This shift - from a purely histological classification to a phenotype-based framework - has important implications for both risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.2,6

At the most fundamental level, MASLD phenotypes can be conceptualized along two interacting axes: metabolic burden and fibrogenic activity. However, in real-world practice, this binary framework is insufficient. Patients often exhibit discordance between metabolic severity and liver outcomes, suggesting that additional modifiers - genetic background, body composition, inflammatory signaling, and environmental exposures - play a critical role in shaping disease trajectory.

Several Clinically Meaningful Phenotypes can be Identified

The classical metabolic phenotype represents the majority of MASLD cases and is strongly associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and insulin resistance. These patients typically exhibit a gradual progression from steatosis to steatohepatitis and fibrosis over time. While the trajectory is often slow, the cumulative burden of metabolic dysfunction increases both hepatic and cardiovascular risk, making long-term management essential (Figure 2). In contrast, the lean MASLD phenotype, which is particularly relevant in Asian populations, challenges the traditional association between MASLD and obesity and has emerged as an increasingly recognized clinical entity with distinct metabolic and genetic characteristics.8–10 These patients may have a normal or only mildly elevated body mass index but display increased visceral adiposity, sarcopenia, or unfavorable fat distribution. Clinically, they are often under-recognized, and diagnosis may be delayed. Importantly, despite a seemingly “low-risk” metabolic profile, a subset of these patients develops significant fibrosis, suggesting a stronger contribution from genetic and environmental factors.

A third group, often encountered in tertiary care, is the inflammatory or fibrogenic phenotype. These patients demonstrate disproportionate liver injury relative to their metabolic burden, with persistent elevation of aminotransferases, active steatohepatitis, and accelerated fibrosis progression. This phenotype may reflect heightened inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, or genetic predisposition. From a clinical standpoint, these patients represent a high-risk group that requires closer monitoring and early intervention.

The MetALD phenotype - characterized by the coexistence of metabolic dysfunction and moderate alcohol consumption according to current MetALD definitions - adds another layer of complexity.1,10 Increasing evidence suggests a synergistic effect between metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-related liver injury, resulting in accelerated fibrosis progression and worse clinical outcomes compared with either condition alone.1,10,11

Finally, the advanced disease phenotype, encompassing cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), represents the end stage of multiple possible trajectories rather than a distinct starting point. Notably, HCC may develop even in the absence of cirrhosis in MASLD, further emphasizing the heterogeneity of disease progression.12

A key concept is that these phenotypes are dynamic rather than static. Patients may transition between phenotypes over time, driven by changes in weight, glycemic control, lifestyle, aging, or treatment exposure. For example, a patient with a classical metabolic phenotype may evolve into an inflammatory phenotype with progressive fibrosis, or a lean MASLD patient may accumulate metabolic risk factors and shift toward a more typical metabolic profile. This dynamic nature underscores the need for longitudinal reassessment, rather than a one-time classification.

From a trajectory perspective, MASLD progression is highly variable. While a proportion of patients remain stable with simple steatosis, others experience progressive fibrosis, leading to cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and HCC. The rate of progression is influenced not only by fibrosis stage but also by metabolic control, presence of diabetes, inflammatory activity, and coexisting conditions. Importantly, regression is also possible, particularly with sustained weight loss or effective metabolic intervention, reinforcing the concept that MASLD is a modifiable disease.

Another clinically important observation is the dissociation between histological severity and clinical outcomes. Fibrosis stage is the strongest predictor of liver-related events; however, cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of mortality across many MASLD phenotypes. This highlights the need to consider disease trajectories beyond the liver alone. In practice, a patient with moderate fibrosis but high cardiovascular risk may have a worse overall prognosis than a patient with more advanced liver disease but fewer systemic comorbidities.

Taken together, these observations support a shift toward a phenotype-driven and trajectory-based approach to MASLD. Rather than asking only “What stage is the liver disease?”, clinicians should also ask:What phenotype does this patient belong to?

What is the likely trajectory?

Which outcomes are most relevant - hepatic, cardiovascular, or both?

Risk Stratification: from Concept to Clinical Practice

Stepwise Non-Invasive Assessment

The implementation of risk stratification in MASLD has evolved toward a stepwise, non-invasive, and scalable approach, designed to be applicable across different levels of care. This model reflects a pragmatic balance between diagnostic accuracy and real-world feasibility, particularly in the context of a highly prevalent disease where universal specialist evaluation is neither practical nor necessary.

At the core of this approach is the principle of sequential testing, where an initial, simple screening tool is used to exclude advanced fibrosis in low-risk individuals, followed by more specific tests in patients with indeterminate or elevated risk. Among first-line tools, the FIB-4 index has emerged as the most widely adopted instrument due to its simplicity, low cost, and acceptable performance in ruling out advanced fibrosis. Importantly, its strength lies not in definitive diagnosis, but in triage - identifying patients who can be safely managed outside hepatology settings versus those requiring further evaluation.6,13

Patients with low FIB-4 values can generally remain in primary care or metabolic clinics with periodic reassessment. In contrast, individuals falling into the indeterminate or high-risk categories require second-line testing. This second step aims to improve specificity and better define structural liver disease. Vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) is widely used for secondary fibrosis assessment because it provides rapid and non-invasive evaluation of liver stiffness. Two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) has also emerged as a reliable alternative with comparable diagnostic accuracy in experienced centers. The enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) test provides a blood-based alternative, whereas magnetic resonance elastography offers the highest diagnostic accuracy but remains limited by cost and availability.13,14

Although non-invasive tests have substantially reduced the need for liver biopsy in routine clinical practice, liver biopsy remains the histological reference standard for diagnosing steatohepatitis, accurately staging fibrosis, and excluding alternative or coexisting liver diseases. Current international guidelines recommend liver biopsy in selected patients when non-invasive tests yield inconclusive or discordant results, when diagnostic uncertainty persists, or when histological confirmation is required before initiating disease-specific therapy or enrollment into clinical trials. Therefore, liver biopsy continues to play an important complementary role rather than being replaced entirely by non-invasive assessment.

The strength of this layered approach lies in its ability to optimize resource allocation. By filtering a large population through an initial low-cost test, it reduces unnecessary referrals while maintaining sensitivity for clinically significant fibrosis. In doing so, it addresses a central challenge in MASLD care: distinguishing the relatively small group of patients at risk of progression from the much larger population with stable disease.

However, it is important to recognize that no algorithm is perfect. Indeterminate zones remain a persistent limitation, and test performance may vary across populations, particularly in older patients or those with multiple comorbidities. Therefore, non-invasive assessment should not be interpreted in isolation but integrated into a broader clinical context. Ultimately, the value of stepwise assessment lies not in replacing clinical judgment, but in structuring it in a reproducible and scalable manner.14,15

Beyond Fibrosis: Multidimensional Risk

While fibrosis stage remains the most robust predictor of liver-related outcomes, a fibrosis-centric model alone does not fully capture the complexity of MASLD. Increasingly, it is recognized that risk in MASLD is multidimensional, shaped by the interaction between hepatic disease, metabolic dysfunction, and systemic comorbidity (Figure 3). From a hepatic perspective, fibrosis defines the risk of cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and liver-related mortality. However, MASLD-related outcomes extend beyond the liver. Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death in many patient populations, followed by malignancy and other metabolic complications. This disconnect highlights a critical point: the patient’s overall prognosis cannot be inferred from liver disease alone.3,16

Several factors contribute to this broader risk architecture. Type 2 diabetes is perhaps the most important modifier, consistently associated with more advanced fibrosis and worse outcomes. Age, obesity severity, and renal dysfunction further compound risk, while persistent inflammatory activity may signal ongoing disease progression even in the absence of advanced fibrosis. Alcohol exposure, even at levels below traditional thresholds, can synergize with metabolic dysfunction and accelerate liver injury in selected patients.17

In this context, risk stratification should be viewed not as a single axis but as an integrated framework combining hepatic and extrahepatic dimensions. Practically, this means that two patients with similar liver stiffness measurements may require very different management strategies depending on their cardiometabolic profile. A patient with moderate fibrosis but poorly controlled diabetes and high cardiovascular risk may warrant more aggressive intervention and closer follow-up than a metabolically stable counterpart.

Another important dimension is the dynamic nature of risk. MASLD is not a static condition, and risk categories may change over time. Worsening metabolic control, weight gain, or aging may shift a patient into a higher-risk trajectory, whereas effective lifestyle or pharmacological interventions may stabilize or even regress disease. This underscores the importance of longitudinal reassessment, rather than reliance on a single baseline evaluation.6,14

Looking forward, the concept of multidimensional risk is likely to expand further with the integration of novel biomarkers, omics-based approaches, and artificial intelligence–driven models. These tools hold the potential to refine risk prediction beyond fibrosis and align clinical decision-making more closely with underlying disease biology. However, even in their absence, a structured approach that integrates fibrosis assessment with metabolic and systemic risk already represents a meaningful step toward precision care (Table 3). Emerging technologies, including multi-omics, circulating biomarkers, and artificial intelligence-based prediction models, may further improve individualized risk prediction. Nevertheless, external validation, interpretability, and cost-effectiveness remain important barriers before widespread clinical implementation. Unlike current international guidelines, which primarily emphasize the identification of advanced fibrosis through stepwise non-invasive assessment, the framework proposed in this review further integrates clinical phenotype, disease trajectory, and multidimensional cardiometabolic risk to support individualized management. Personalized Management: Toward Precision Hepatology

The management of MASLD is undergoing a fundamental transition—from a uniform, lifestyle-centered paradigm to a more nuanced, risk-adapted and phenotype-driven strategy. This shift is not merely conceptual; it reflects a growing recognition that MASLD encompasses diverse biological pathways and clinical trajectories, and therefore requires individualized therapeutic approaches. In this context, personalized management does not imply complexity for its own sake, but rather the alignment of treatment intensity and modality with the patient’s specific risk profile and disease drivers (Figure 4). At its core, personalized care in MASLD is built upon three interconnected pillars: phenotype identification, fibrosis staging, and cardiometabolic risk profiling. These elements provide complementary information that together define the patient’s position within the disease spectrum and guide clinical decision-making.

Lifestyle Intervention: Foundational but not Uniform

Lifestyle modification remains the cornerstone of MASLD management. However, its role should be understood more precisely - not as a universal prescription applied uniformly, but as an intervention whose intensity, targets, and expected benefits vary across phenotypes and disease stages.

Weight reduction remains the most effective non-pharmacological intervention. Sustained weight loss of 5–7% is associated with improvement in steatosis, while greater reductions (≥10%) may lead to histological improvement in steatohepatitis and even regression of fibrosis in selected patients. Nevertheless, the response to lifestyle intervention is highly variable. Some patients achieve substantial metabolic and hepatic improvement, whereas others show limited response despite similar weight loss. This variability likely reflects differences in genetic susceptibility, baseline fibrosis, inflammatory activity, and adherence.6,18

In lean MASLD, where excess weight is not the dominant driver, the therapeutic focus shifts toward improving metabolic fitness, body composition, and insulin sensitivity, rather than absolute weight reduction. Similarly, in patients with advanced fibrosis, lifestyle intervention alone is often insufficient and should be integrated with additional strategies.

Thus, lifestyle therapy should not be viewed as a static recommendation, but as a tailored intervention aligned with the underlying phenotype and therapeutic goals.

Pharmacological Therapy: Matching Treatment to Biology

The emergence of pharmacological therapies marks a critical step toward precision hepatology in MASLD. Unlike earlier approaches that targeted single pathways, newer agents are increasingly designed to address specific components of disease pathogenesis, including metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and fibrosis.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are particularly relevant in patients with type 2 diabetes or obesity, where they provide dual metabolic and hepatic benefits.19,20 These agents improve glycemic control, promote weight loss, and have been associated with reductions in liver fat and markers of steatohepatitis.

More recently, thyroid hormone receptor-β (THR-β) agonists, such as resmetirom, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing hepatic steatosis and improving histological features of MASH, representing one of the first targeted therapies with Phase 3 evidence. Importantly, these agents are most relevant in patients with active disease and significant fibrosis, where pharmacological intervention is more likely to alter clinical outcomes.21 Despite these encouraging advances, current pharmacological therapies remain associated with several limitations, including high cost, limited accessibility, uncertainty regarding long-term outcomes, and restricted indications. Therefore, lifestyle modification continues to represent the foundation of MASLD management.

This evolving therapeutic landscape reinforces a key principle: treatment should be matched to disease biology and risk, rather than applied uniformly across all patients with MASLD. Not all patients require pharmacological therapy, but those with higher-risk phenotypes or progressive disease may benefit substantially from early intervention.22 Nevertheless, important uncertainties remain. Although current pharmacological therapies improve histological features of MASH, robust evidence demonstrating sustained reductions in liver-related events or overall mortality is still lacking. In addition, high cost, limited accessibility, uncertainties regarding long-term safety, and optimal patient selection continue to constrain their widespread implementation in routine clinical practice.

Risk-Guided Surveillance and Follow-Up

Personalized management in MASLD extends beyond treatment selection to include risk-adapted surveillance and follow-up strategies. The intensity and frequency of monitoring should reflect both hepatic and extrahepatic risk.

Patients with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis require structured surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma and complications of portal hypertension. However, surveillance strategies should also consider that MASLD-related HCC can occur in non-cirrhotic patients, particularly in those with diabetes and other metabolic risk factors. While universal screening in non-cirrhotic MASLD is not currently recommended, risk-enriched approaches are likely to emerge.

Equally important is the management of extrahepatic risk. Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality in MASLD, and therefore aggressive management of cardiovascular risk factors—including lipid control, blood pressure, and glycemic status—is essential. In this sense, MASLD management cannot be confined to hepatology alone; it requires integration with broader cardiometabolic care pathways.

Multidisciplinary and System-Based Care

Given its systemic nature, MASLD is ideally managed through a multidisciplinary model involving hepatologists, endocrinologists, cardiologists, nutritionists, and primary care physicians. Such an approach allows for coordinated management of liver disease and its associated comorbidities, reducing fragmentation of care.

This is particularly relevant in real-world settings where patients often present with multiple overlapping conditions. A siloed approach - treating liver disease separately from metabolic or cardiovascular disorders—risks missing opportunities for early intervention and comprehensive risk reduction.

From a health system perspective, the implementation of structured care pathways - anchored in non-invasive risk stratification and linked to multidisciplinary management - represents a scalable strategy to address the growing burden of MASLD.

Toward Precision Hepatology

The concept of precision hepatology in MASLD reflects a broader evolution in medicine: moving from population-based averages to individualized risk prediction and targeted intervention. In this model, clinical decisions are informed not only by disease stage, but also by phenotype, trajectory, and patient-specific risk factors.

Future directions are likely to include integration of multi-omics data, advanced imaging, and artificial intelligence to refine risk prediction and therapeutic selection. However, even with currently available tools, a meaningful shift toward personalized care is already achievable.

Challenges and Future Directions

Despite remarkable advances in the understanding and management of MASLD, several important challenges remain before precision hepatology can be fully implemented in routine clinical practice. First, although fibrosis remains the strongest predictor of liver-related outcomes, current non-invasive tests have limited ability to accurately identify active steatohepatitis, predict individual disease trajectories, or monitor treatment response. Additional validation of emerging biomarkers across diverse populations is therefore required.

Second, the integration of genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and gut microbiome profiling has the potential to improve patient phenotyping and individualized risk prediction. However, these multi-omics approaches remain costly, technically demanding, and insufficiently standardized for widespread clinical application. Similarly, artificial intelligence and machine learning have shown promising performance in integrating clinical, imaging, and molecular data for risk prediction, yet their clinical utility requires prospective validation, external reproducibility, and improved interpretability.

Implementation also remains challenging, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to advanced imaging, molecular diagnostics, and novel pharmacological therapies may be limited. In these settings, pragmatic strategies based on simple non-invasive tests, stepwise risk stratification, and multidisciplinary care pathways are likely to provide the greatest immediate clinical benefit.

Future research should focus on validating phenotype-driven management strategies, identifying robust biomarkers for disease activity and treatment response, integrating artificial intelligence into clinical workflows, and evaluating the cost-effectiveness of precision medicine approaches. Ultimately, translating these advances into accessible and scalable care models will be essential to improve outcomes for the growing global population affected by MASLD. Prospective validation of phenotype-driven management strategies across diverse populations and healthcare systems remains an important unmet need before widespread implementation.

Conclusion

MASLD has evolved from a descriptive diagnosis of hepatic steatosis to a complex, systemic, and heterogeneous disease that demands a more refined clinical approach. The traditional “one-size-fits-all” model is no longer adequate in the face of marked variability in disease biology, progression, and outcomes. Instead, contemporary management requires a shift toward risk-based and phenotype-driven care, where clinical decisions are guided not only by the presence of disease but by its trajectory and systemic context. The principal contribution of this review is the integration of clinical heterogeneity, phenotype-driven disease trajectories, multidimensional risk stratification, and personalized management into a single clinically applicable framework.

Non-invasive risk stratification has provided a practical and scalable framework to identify patients at risk of advanced fibrosis and liver-related complications, enabling earlier and more targeted intervention. However, fibrosis alone does not fully define prognosis. MASLD should be approached within a multidimensional framework that integrates hepatic severity with cardiometabolic risk and extrahepatic disease burden.

At the same time, emerging therapies and improved understanding of disease mechanisms are paving the way toward precision hepatology, where treatment is tailored to individual patient profiles. In this model, lifestyle intervention, pharmacological therapy, and surveillance strategies are no longer applied uniformly but are aligned with disease phenotype, risk level, and patient-specific factors.

Ultimately, the goal of MASLD management is shifting - from reactive treatment of advanced disease to proactive identification and interception of high-risk trajectories. Achieving this will require not only advances in diagnostics and therapeutics, but also the implementation of integrated, multidisciplinary care pathways that reflect the systemic nature of the disease. Future research should focus on validating phenotype-based strategies, integrating artificial intelligence and multi-omics into clinical workflows, and developing cost-effective precision care pathways that are applicable across diverse healthcare settings, particularly in resource-limited countries.
