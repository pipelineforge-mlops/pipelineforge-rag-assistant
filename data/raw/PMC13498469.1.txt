Pain is a key determinant of quality‐of‐life following breast cancer surgery, the most common cancer operation in the United States. Gabapentinoids are frequently prescribed for pain management, yet their respiratory, cardiovascular, and opioid‐related adverse effects remain unclear. This target trial emulation, analyzing US commercial claims (2013–2023), evaluated these safety outcomes under gabapentin–opioids initiation vs. opioids only during the first postoperative month following breast cancer surgery, with sustained 1‐year follow‐up. Women with nonmetastatic breast cancer were eligible on surgery dates if they had no prior outcomes, nor gabapentinoids or opioid prescriptions. Outcomes included time‐to‐first respiratory complications, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), arrhythmia, and high‐dose opioid utilization (≥ 50 or ≥ 90 morphine ‐milligram equivalent [MME]). We estimated 1‐year outcome risks and 95% CIs under gabapentin–opioids and opioids‐only strategies using weighted pooled logistic regressions with bootstrapping, including stabilized inverse probability weighting to adjust for baseline and time‐varying confounders. Of 86,290 eligible individuals, 1709 initiated gabapentin and opioids, and 39,372 initiated opioids only. Risks of respiratory complications (RR 1.24, 95% CI: 0.26–3.02), MACE (RR 0.95, 95% CI: 0.31–1.85), and arrhythmia (RR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.24–1.33) were comparable under gabapentin–opioids and opioids‐only. Risk of receiving opioid dosage ≥ 50 MME (RR 1.23, 95% CI: 0.85–1.72) and ≥ 90 MME (RR 1.09, 95% CI: 0.43–2.23) were comparable. This study challenged the limited evidence regarding safety concerns of adding gabapentin for pain management, finding that gabapentin–opioids initiation carried comparable respiratory and cardiovascular risks to opioids only following breast cancer surgery. However, we did not find evidence that adjunctive gabapentin reduces subsequent high‐dose opioid utilization.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Pain is a key determinant of health‐related quality of life in patients undergoing breast cancer surgery. Gabapentinoids are frequently prescribed as adjuncts to opioids for pain management. Although some studies have linked gabapentinoids to respiratory complications, cardiovascular diseases, and opioid overdose, existing evidence remains limited and inconsistent. No studies have evaluated these safety concerns following breast cancer surgery, despite it being the most common category of cancer operations in the United States.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

Are there differences in the risks of respiratory, cardiovascular, and opioid‐related adverse events under postoperative initiation of gabapentin and opioids vs. opioids only following breast cancer surgery?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

This target trial emulation found that, among US patients undergoing breast cancer surgery, postoperative risks of respiratory and cardiovascular adverse events were comparable under initiation of gabapentin and opioids vs. opioids only. However, we did not find strong evidence that adjunctive gabapentin use provides an opioid‐sparing effect on subsequent high‐dose opioid use during postoperative pain management.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

Our study may advance clinical pharmacology and translational science by applying a causal inference framework to provide evidence on the safety profile of adding gabapentin to opioids for pain management following breast cancer surgery, the most common category of cancer operations in the United States.

Pain is a key determinant of physical and psychological functioning and overall quality‐of‐life following breast cancer surgery, the most common category of cancer operations in the United States. 1 , 2 Among those undergoing mastectomy, nearly 50% experience moderate‐to‐severe acute pain followed by pain syndromes (e.g., fibromyalgia). 3 More than 30% of breast cancer survivors developed neuropathic pain following breast‐conserving surgery. 4 Opioids remain a primary option for pain management; however, following the 2016 guideline changes in opioid prescribing, their use, especially high‐dose and long‐acting formulations, have decreased. 5 , 6 Gabapentinoids, particularly gabapentin in the United States, are commonly prescribed off label for non‐indicated pain and as a strategy to limit opioid consumption. 7 , 8 Their use has increased from approximately 8.0% in 2010 to 14.0% in 2020 among US Medicare beneficiaries with cancer. 5 A single‐institution observational study reported that approximately 20% of patients undergoing minor breast and/or axillary procedures received gabapentin as part of their preoperative multimodal analgesic regimen. 9 Despite their use in these settings, the utility of gabapentinoids as opioid‐sparing postoperative analgesics remains controversial. 10

Gabapentinoids may exert synergistic respiratory depressive effects with opioids and exacerbate cardiovascular risks by altering arterial myogenic tone and calcium signaling in cardiomyocytes. 11 , 12 , 13 However, current evidence on their risks of respiratory complications, cardiovascular diseases, and opioid overdose remains limited and inconsistent. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 First, most prior studies focused on single indications for gabapentinoids (e.g., epilepsy), 16 , 17 , 19 , 20 which limit the relevance to understanding their safety profile in pain management following breast cancer surgery. Second, existing evidence is limited by methodological limitations and potential biases. The 2019 US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) warning regarding respiratory complications with gabapentinoids relied on case reports, single‐center observational studies, and clinical trials involving only 8–12 healthy volunteers. 14 Previous observational studies yielded inconsistent findings on cardiovascular risks, 16 , 17 , 20 , 21 , 22 with many limited by inadequate confounder adjustment, selection bias, and protopathic bias. 23 Heterogeneous outcome definitions have also led to divergent interpretations.

To address current gaps and limitations, this study aimed to first evaluate the risks of respiratory and cardiovascular adverse events under postoperative initiation of gabapentin and opioids vs. opioids only following breast cancer surgery. We also evaluated the effects of adjunctive gabapentin on the risk of high‐dose opioid utilization during the postoperative year. We applied the target trial emulation framework to address intervention‐focused questions and minimize biases common in observational studies. 24

Target trial specification

In the target trial, women are eligible on the day of breast cancer surgery if they are aged 18 years or older, have been diagnosed with breast cancer within the prior year, have no distant metastases and no history of study outcome events (defined below) in the preceding year, and have no gabapentinoid or opioid prescriptions in the past 6 months. Enrollees are randomly assigned on the date of surgery to either the gabapentin–opioids strategy or opioids‐only strategy and adhere to their assigned strategy throughout the first postoperative month (detailed in Table 1 ). The gabapentin–opioids strategy prescribes both gabapentin and opioids, without any pregabalin prescription, during the first postoperative month. The opioids‐only strategy prescribes opioids without either gabapentinoid during the first postoperative month. As a pragmatic target trial, 27 specific choices regarding dosage, types, and formulations of gabapentin and opioids are allowed to vary across patients depending on clinicians' discretion, patients' needs, availability, and guideline recommendations. 28 During the first postoperative month following surgery, other non‐gabapentin and non‐opioid pain management modalities can be used under either treatment strategy. Beyond the first postoperative month, patients can receive any analgesic modality regardless of their treatment arms.

Study outcomes are defined as the time‐to‐first inpatient or emergency department encounter for the following: (1) composite respiratory adverse events (e.g., acute respiratory failure, respiratory arrest, or administering mechanical ventilation); (2) three‐point major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure (HF), and ischemic stroke; (3) cardiac arrhythmia; (4) opioid overdose diagnosed by clinicians. 33 Follow‐up starts on the date of surgery when treatment is assigned (time zero) until the occurrence of an outcome, loss to follow‐up (e.g., death, missing scheduled visits, voluntary withdrawal), or the end of 12 months of follow‐up.

The causal estimand for the target trial and its emulation are the differences in 1‐year postoperative risks of the study outcomes comparing the gabapentin–opioids strategy and opioids‐only strategy under the condition of no loss to follow‐up, including continuous health insurance coverage and no death. Death is expected to be an extremely rare event during the specified 1‐year follow‐up in the study population. 34

Data source

To emulate the target trial, we analyzed Merative MarketScan® Commercial Claims and Medicare Supplemental data between 2013 and 2023. MarketScan datasets include de‐identified records for inpatient and outpatient services and outpatient prescriptions from more than 26 million individuals with employer‐sponsored private health insurance. The data include sociodemographic characteristics, diagnoses encoded using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10), procedures encoded using Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and healthcare utilization and expenditures. Information on deaths was ascertained from MarketScan Mortality data.

Target trial emulation

We emulated the target trial by operationalizing each component of the trial protocol using claims data (Table 1 ). Following the eligibility criteria, we identified the study population using diagnosis and procedure codes (Tables S1 and S2 in Data S1 ). We used previously validated algorithms 25 to identify breast cancer diagnoses between 2014 and 2021. We identified patients undergoing breast cancer surgery within 1 year following diagnosis. 26 Individuals were required to have at least 1 year of continuous health insurance coverage before surgery. 27 We excluded patients having prescriptions for either gabapentinoid or opioids in the 6 months before surgery.

Following the target trial protocol, for each treatment strategy, we identified individuals whose data were compatible with the eligibility criteria and estimated outcomes using data from all eligible individuals, censoring them when their observed data deviated from the strategy under consideration, either due to their received analgesics in the first postoperative month or due to loss to follow‐up. 35 , 36 For the gabapentin–opioids strategy, individuals were censored if they did not receive postoperative prescriptions for both gabapentin and opioids. For the opioids‐only strategy, they were censored if they did not receive a postoperative prescription for opioids or if they additionally received a gabapentin prescription. In either strategy, individuals were censored at loss to follow‐up. The overall approach for applying the two treatment strategies conceptually aligns with the clone‐censor‐weight method, 35 although, because follow‐up time is discretized into monthly time intervals, any particular individual's data can only be consistent with at most one of the treatment strategies. We identified prescriptions of gabapentin and/or opioids using pharmacy claims and national drug codes. We measured opioid types and dosages in morphine milligram equivalent (MME), calculated using established formulas. 37

We identified study outcome events from inpatient and emergency department claims using diagnosis and procedure codes validated in previous studies analyzing administrative data (Tables S1 and S2 in Data S1 ). 30 , 31 , 32 We measured opioid‐related adverse events in two ways: (1) opioid dosage ≥ 50 or 90 MME, separately, after the first postoperative month; 6 , 28 and (2) opioid overdose based on diagnosis codes. 31 We used the two MME thresholds as indicators for high‐dose opioid utilization and potential risk of overdose, consistent with clinical guideline concensus. 6 , 28 Opioid overdose identified using diagnosis codes is subject to a high risk of misclassification, and overdose events are expected to be rare among opioid naïve individuals treated in well‐controlled and closely monitored healthcare environments, including the US commercial healthcare setting in this study. Previous studies using claims data have reported a very low rate of opioid overdose when measured using diagnosis codes alone (< 0.01%). 18 , 19 , 38 Follow‐up was accrued monthly from time zero. Loss to follow‐up was defined as health insurance disenrollment or death. We expected the competing effects from all‐cause mortality to be minimal because patients with early‐stage breast cancer have near 100% one‐year overall survival. 34

Covariates

To control for measured confounding and assume conditional exchangeability to hold in the emulation, we included a rich set of baseline and time‐varying covariates (Table 1 , Data S2 ). We considered potential confounders using Single World Intervention Graphs (SWIGs), 39 which describes the assumed causal structure relating treatment, baseline, time‐varying, censoring, and outcome variables. The choice of baseline covariates was based on their potential impact on prescribing adjunctive gabapentin with opioids or opioids only and their relevance to study outcomes.

We identified baseline covariates measured at or within the year before time zero, including sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, region, employment status), healthcare utilization and expenditure, surgery type (mastectomy vs. lumpectomy), and oncology‐related proxies in place of breast cancer stage and subtype, including axillary lymph node involvement, diagnostic features (e.g., estrogen receptor [ER] status), and neoadjuvant therapies including chemotherapy (e.g., anthracyclines, taxanes), radiotherapy, hormone therapy (e.g., aromatase inhibitors, tamoxifen), and targeted therapy (e.g., antihuman epidermal growth factor receptor‐2 [HER2] therapy). We captured multimodal pain management outside of gabapentinoids and opioids at the time of surgery or within the prior year, including prescription acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), physical therapy, psychosocial therapy, and nerve blocks.

Other clinical characteristics included a history of neurological and pain disorders (e.g., sleep disorder), diagnostic procedures (e.g., electrocardiography), comorbidities (e.g., metabolic diseases), mental disorders (e.g., depression), respiratory diseases (e.g., asthma), diabetes, history of individual cardiovascular disease (e.g., hypertension), and obesity. We also measured the use of various medications, including outpatient medications (e.g., benzodiazepines), cardiovascular medications (e.g., anticoagulants), and vaccinations. Disease severity was summarized using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Diagnosis, procedure, and MarketScan‐specific codes are provided in Tables S1 – S3 in Data S1 .

We also identified time‐varying covariates, measured monthly over follow‐up, including use of opioids, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, postoperative breast cancer treatments, postoperative pain management modalities, other medications, and vaccinations. Adjustment for time‐varying covariates was necessary for confounding control because treatment strategies involved remaining under sustained follow‐up (primarily implying maintenance of health insurance coverage). We considered these variables that may affect both no loss to follow‐up and outcomes over the study period.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics and standardized mean differences (SMD) to summarize baseline characteristics for patients initiating gabapentin and opioids or opioids only.

To adjust for confounding by baseline and time‐varying covariates, we used stabilized inverse probability weights (IPWs) derived from separate logistic regressions, with the censoring models pooling over monthly time intervals. 36 , 40 IPWs were a product of treatment weights for the received treatment strategy conditional on 99 baseline covariates and censoring weights for those remaining uncensored at each month of follow‐up conditional on both baseline and 22 time‐varying covariates. We used the sample‐size‐based capping method to truncate extreme weights, which provides a theoretically more favorable bias‐variance trade‐off than conventional truncation methods, such as using percentile or fixed weight thresholds. 41 Selection of covariates for logistic models considered substantive knowledge about variables influencing treatment strategy choice, loss to follow‐up, and outcomes. We excluded variables if they were instrumental‐like variables 42 (e.g., postherpetic neuralgia, FDA‐approved indication for gabapentin) or if there were concerns about multicollinearity 43 (variance inflation factor > 10; e.g., cyclophosphamide) or near‐positivity violations (e.g., less than 25 observations). 44 We explicate causal assumptions using SWIG 39 and further provide technical details regarding IPW estimator in Data S2 .

We estimated the 1‐year risk (cumulative incidence) of each outcome over 12 months of follow‐up using weighted pooled logistic regressions, including treatment strategy indicators, monthly time terms, and interactions between treatment indicators and time terms (Data S2 ). 45 The 1‐year risks under the two treatment strategies were contrasted using risk ratios (RR) and differences (RD). We calculated the corresponding 95% CIs as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles obtained from nonparametric bootstraps with 500 iterations. 40

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

We re‐estimated outcome risks stratified by surgery type (mastectomy), no history of circulatory system diseases (MarketScan‐Major‐Diagnosis‐Code = 05), prior chemotherapy use, and diagnosis of axillary lymph node involvement. We conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of main findings: (1) weight truncation using a 99th percentile threshold; (2) outcomes recorded in the principal position of claims; (3) risk estimation using the parametric g‐formula 46 (Data S2 ); and (4) additional adjustment for baseline covariates with SMD > 0.1 in the weighted marginal structural models to produce doubly adjusted (though not doubly robust) risk estimates. 47 To provide perspective on the strength of confounding without adjustment, we conducted two analyses: (1) excluding time‐varying covariates from IPWs; and (2) unweighted analysis. We conducted an E‐value‐based sensitivity analysis 48 to quantify the magnitude of potential unmeasured or residual confounding that could explain away an observed no‐opioid‐sparing effect under adjunctive gabapentin use with opioids if the true effect was opioid‐sparing. A hypothetical true effect on the 1‐year postoperative risk of opioid use at 50 MME was operationalized as a RR of 0.8 under gabapentin–opioids initiation vs. opioids only. This sensitivity analysis presumed that unmeasured or residual confounding, if present, would result in an estimated RR of greater than 0.8 due to higher analgesic demands at baseline among individuals observed to receive adjunctive gabapentin (Data S2 ). Finally, we conducted two additional analyses by redefining the treatment strategies: (1) specifying the opioid received in both arms as oxycodone at 30–45 MME (equivalent to 5‐mg oxycodone every 4–6 hours per day) to align the opioid type and dosing regimen under each arm; and (2) initiating gabapentin–opioids or opioids‐only within 1 day after time zero to minimize potential reverse causation from delayed treatment initiation during the first postoperative month.

We performed data cleaning and analysis using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 and R version 4.5.2.

RESULTS

Of 86,290 eligible individuals (median [IQR] age, 57.0 [13.0] years), 1709 initiated gabapentin and opioids during 30 days following surgery, and 39,372 initiated opioids only (Figure S1 in Data S1 ). The median (IQR) time from surgery to treatment initiation ranged from 1 (10.0) day for gabapentin to 0.0 (1.0) day for opioids (Table S4 in Data S1 ). Most opioid types (> 80%) were oxycodone and hydrocodone. Median (IQR) dosages in MME were comparable between individuals initiating gabapentin with opioids and opioids only (37.5 [21.9] vs. 37.5 [25.0]).

Baseline characteristics differed among those initiating gabapentin and opioids vs. opioids only (Table 2 , Table S5 in Data S1 ), including age (median [IQR], 51.0 [13.0] vs. 56.0 [13.0] years), employment status (70.9% vs. 58.1%), mean (SD) inpatient costs ($13,582 [$30,452] vs. $5790 [$20,290]). Patients initiating gabapentin and opioids were more likely to receive anticancer treatments, including mastectomy (70.5% vs. 32.7%), chemotherapy (e.g., anthracycline, 9.4% vs. 4.5%), and anti‐HER2 therapy (6.3% vs. 3.2%). They also received more other pain management modalities (e.g., NSAIDs, 28.4% vs. 21.6%). The comorbidity severity (CCI, 2.5 [1.0] vs. 2.5 [1.0]) were comparable. The FDA‐approved indications for gabapentin accounted for only around 0.1%. Primary analysis

The estimated 1‐year risks of respiratory complications under the gabapentin–opioids strategy (1.5%, 95% CI: 0.3% to 3.4%) and opioids‐only strategy (1.2%, 95% CI: 0.9%–1.5%) were comparable (RR 1.24, 95% CI: 0.26–3.02; Table 3 , Figure 1a ). The estimated 1‐year risks of MACE, including CAD, HF, and ischemic stroke, were comparable under the two treatment strategies (RR 0.95, 95% CI: 0.31–1.85; Table 3 , Figures 1b and S2 in Data S1 ). The estimated 1‐year risk for cardiac arrhythmia was comparable (RR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.24–1.33; Table 3 , Figure 1 c ). The estimated 1‐year risk of receiving opioid dosage ≥ 50 MME (RR 1.23, 95% CI: 0.85–1.72; Table 3 , Figure 1 d ) or ≥ 90 MME (RR 1.09, 95% CI: 0.43 to 2.23; Table 3 , Figure 1 e ) after the first postoperative month was comparable under gabapentin and opioids initiation vs. opioids only. The estimated 1‐year risk of opioid overdose based on diagnoses could not be estimated due to rare events (N = 3 [0.2%] vs. N = 5 [0.01%]).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Findings from subgroup and sensitivity analyses were generally consistent with the primary analysis (Tables 4 and S8 , Figures S3 – S9 in Data S1 ). However, in the subgroup of axillary lymph node involvement, a more than twofold increased risk of receiving opioids ≥ 50 MME was observed with gabapentin–opioids initiation compared with opioids only (RR 2.16, 95% CI: 1.04–3.60). The estimated risk of opioid dosage ≥ 50 MME was 20% higher with gabapentin–opioids initiation in analyses using 99% percentile truncation and unweighted methods, suggesting that, without adjustment, strong confounding may be present for this outcome. Risk estimates obtained using the parametric g‐formula and additionally adjusted for baseline covariates with SMD > 0.1 in the weighted marginal structural models were aligned with the main findings. The E‐value for the outcome of high‐dose opioid use at 50 MME was 2.45, which indicates that the unmeasured or residual confounding would need to be associated with both the treatment and the outcome by a RR of at least 2.45 each, conditional on the included measured confounders, to produce the observed estimate (1.23) if the hypothetical true RR was 0.8 (Figure S9 in Data S1 ). Results from additional analyses specifying oxycodone at 30–45 MME as the opioid in both arms and initiating gabapentin–opioids or opioids‐only within 1 day after time zero were qualitatively similar to the main findings (Tables S9 and S10 in Data S1 ).

DISCUSSION

In this target trial emulation, we estimated that the risks of respiratory and cardiovascular adverse events and subsequent high‐dose opioid utilization were comparable with postoperative initiation of gabapentin and opioids vs. opioids only following breast cancer surgery. However, uncertainty was high due to a low probability of outcome events, evidenced by wide confidence intervals.

Comparison with previous studies

Our study is the first to evaluate initiation of gabapentin and opioids for postoperative pain management after breast cancer surgery, accounting for over 30% of cancer operations performed in the United States. 2 Prior observational studies in other populations and settings 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 38 were potentially limited by selection bias, inadequate handling of confounding, and lack of a clearly defined time zero and causal estimand, which could introduce time‐related biases like immortal time bias. 23

Respiratory and opioids‐related adverse events

The 2019 US FDA warning regarding breathing difficulties with gabapentinoids relied on 49 case reports, three observational studies of general surgeries from the Mayo Clinic, and two clinical trials enrolling only 10–12 healthy males aged ≥ 60 years. 14 Several studies examined surrogate endpoints, such as end‐tidal carbon dioxide, which do not necessarily translate to major adverse events. 49 , 50 Additionally, gabapentinoids were used concomitantly with high‐potency or long‐acting opioids (e.g., 40%–50% remifentanil, sustained‐release oxycodone) and ketamine, 50 , 51 both of which are known risk factors for respiratory depression. In our study, use of these medications was absent or minimal, with long‐acting opioids < 0.1%.

Several nested case–control studies reported 1.6‐ to 2‐fold risks of respiratory complications, opioid overdose, or opioid‐related death associated with gabapentinoids and opioids vs. opioids only or nonuse. 18 , 19 , 38 However, these findings were either limited to narrow populations (e.g., Medicare beneficiaries with neuropathic pain) or were at high risk of confounding bias from imbalanced characteristics between cases and controls, including long‐acting opioid use (62% vs. 40%) and MME ≥ 200 (40% vs. 21%). A US cohort study of inpatient procedures found gabapentinoids and opioids vs. opioids only carried 1.7‐ to 2‐fold risks of opioid overdose and respiratory complications, though residual confounding from broad procedure categories is possible. 15 Even without confounding, these findings may not be applicable to outpatient elective surgeries, whereas nearly 80% of breast cancer operations and most gabapentinoid prescriptions occur in outpatient settings, 52 underscoring the relevance of our study.

Cardiovascular adverse events

Previous studies reported inconsistent findings on cardiovascular risks, with some showing no association 21 , 22 and others suggesting increased risk. 16 , 20 A cohort study of patients with diabetic neuropathy found increased risks of six cardiovascular diseases associated with gabapentin use over 5 years. 16 However, in their 3‐month sensitivity analysis, gabapentin showed only 10%–15% increases in heart failure and myocardial infarction risks. Time‐varying confounders may have affected the results over the long follow‐up. Another nested case–control study of patients with epilepsy found that pregabalin, but not gabapentin, was associated with a 33% increased risk of out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest. 17 However, limited covariate adjustment (only 12 cardiovascular‐related variables) and protopathic biases (reverse causation arising from pregabalin use for prodromal symptoms of cardiac events) may have confounded the findings.

Clinical implications

Gabapentinoids are considered off label for managing cancer and postoperative pain due to limited evidence supporting their efficacy and opioid‐sparing effects. 10 Their clinical use is also restricted by potential safety concerns. 14 Our study addresses several limitations of prior studies and provides evidence that postoperative initiation of gabapentin with opioids carries comparable respiratory and cardiovascular risks to opioids only, supporting its safety profile in postoperative care following breast cancer surgery. However, our findings do not support routine gabapentin use for postoperative pain management, given the absence of strong evidence for a benefit in reducing subsequent high‐dose opioid use. This finding may reflect limited or no additional analgesic efficacy of gabapentin, although residual confounding cannot be precluded. The potential analgesic benefits may be obscured by higher baseline analgesic needs among patients receiving adjunctive gabapentin. However, we applied multiple analytics to handle this potential confounding, and our subgroup and sensitivity analyses (e.g., restriction to patients undergoing mastectomy) also showed no reduction in high‐dose opioid use, supporting the robustness of these findings. To identify outcome events indicative of a high risk of opioid overdose, we adopted established definitions of high‐dose opioid utilization. 6 , 28 However, opioid use at or above the two MME thresholds may reflect patient need for pain relief after breast cancer surgery and appropriate multimodal analgesic treatment by clinicians. The observed extremely low number of opioid overdose events measured using diagnosis codes, albeit subject to possible misclassification, may also reflect appropriate pain management practices in US private healthcare settings.

The findings of this study indicate comparable risks of respiratory and cardiovascular events. Our study was not designed to formally test non‐inferiority or equivalence hypotheses. 53 Uncertainty around the estimated risks was nontrivial given the disparity in sample size between the gabapentin–opioids and opioids‐only arms and the infrequency of outcome events. This is especially important when interpreting the null findings in subgroup analyses with wide confidence intervals due to the low number of outcome events in these strata, such as the estimated more than twofold increased risk of receiving opioids at ≥ 50 MME among patients with a history of axillary lymph node involvement. This finding may, on the other hand, reflect greater analgesic needs in the adjunctive gabapentin arm in this subgroup (unmeasured or residual confounding by indication).

Further studies are needed to evaluate the benefits of gabapentinoids for pain control and related indications (e.g., insomnia, anxiety), and the possibility of other adverse effects (e.g., delirium, fall).

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, our study sample represented breast cancer surgical patients with US commercial health insurance, which limits generalizability to other populations, such as Medicaid or regions where pregabalin use is more common. We were not able to evaluate effects in subgroups defined by race/ethnicity or include uninsured individuals due to the absence of data in MarketScan, which limits the generalizability of our findings to underrepresented populations. Disproportionality in access to healthcare, income, education, and other health equity factors may have divergent effects on postoperative pain management and use of analgesic drugs, especially in the era of opioid crisis in the United States. Future studies using data sources that include key health equity variables, such as SEER‐Medicare or linkages to national or state cancer registries, are warranted to investigate whether the safety risks of adjunctive gabapentin with opioids after breast cancer surgery vary across racial/ethnic groups and other healthcare settings.

Second, several variables are not available in MarketScan, including laboratory results (e.g., spirometry), breast cancer subtypes, and pain severity, which may have confounded our findings. However, we included multiple proxies, such as surgery type, anti‐HER2 therapy, and pain management modalities, to address confounding. Third, baseline characteristics were imbalanced, especially surgery type, breast cancer treatments, and analgesic modalities, which, despite adjustment, may be subject to residual confounding. However, subgroup analyses restricted to patients undergoing mastectomy and baseline chemotherapy, along with sensitivity analyses including the parametric g‐formula and additional adjustment for baseline covariates in the outcomes models, showed qualitatively consistent results. The magnitude of the calculated E‐value for the outcome of opioid utilization at 50 MME suggests that fairly strong unmeasured or residual confounding would need to be present after adjustment for the rich set of potential confounders considered in this study to produce the observed estimate if adjunctive gabapentin was truly opioid‐sparing. Fourth, the extreme weights, especially in the opioids‐only arm, may have increased the variance and uncertainty of risk estimates. However, we applied two truncation methods: the sample‐size‐based capping method in the primary analysis and the 99th percentile truncation method in the sensitivity analysis. Results remained qualitatively consistent across both approaches. Fifth, Individuals who initiated gabapentin and opioids were identified in more recent years, which might induce the residual confounding from temporal period imbalance. However, we have included this variable in the adjustment and this temporal trend reflects the unavoidable real‐world pattern of increasing adjunctive gabapentin prescription to reduce opioid use after the change in opioid guidelines in 2016. 6

CONCLUSIONS

This study challenged the limited existing evidence regarding safety concerns of adding gabapentin to opioids for pain management, finding that postoperative initiation of gabapentin and opioids carried comparable respiratory and cardiovascular risks to opioids only among US patients undergoing breast cancer surgery. However, we did not find strong evidence that adjunctive gabapentin use reduces subsequent high‐dose opioid utilization.
