Keywords: Type 1 diabetes, Diabetic ketoacidosis, Continuous glucose monitoring, Patient navigation

==============================
Although diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is more frequent among racial and ethnic minority populations, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) use is lower in such groups. We sought to determine if care navigation support increases CGM utilization in patients with recurrent admissions for DKA. Using Technology to Address Disparities and Promote Healthcare Equity in Type 1 Diabetes (EquiT1D) is a prospective, single-arm study conducted at an academic medical center. Adult patients with type 1 diabetes (T1D) at high risk for DKA were recruited for participation. From November 2020 to April 2022, patients were consented to participate in an intervention which involved regular contact and care navigation to address barriers to CGM utilization. The primary study outcome was CGM utilization (percentage of days with sensor data). At 12 months, navigation support was associated with an increase in model-estimated mean (SE) CGM utilization from 17.5% (6.8%) at baseline to 51.4% (6.9%) at 12 months (P < 0.001). This study suggests that navigation support may be an effective way to increase CGM utilization among patients at high risk for DKA recurrence. Additional studies are needed to address barriers to CGM utilization.

Trial Registration:clinicaltrials.gov, NCT06899984 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06899984?term=equit1d&rank=1#study-overview).

Highlights

• Navigation support is associated with increased continuous glucose monitoring utilization in patients with type 1 diabetes.

• Hospitalizations decreased, and diabetes-related quality of life increased.

• Care navigation through insurance and other barriers potentially addresses disparities.

Introduction

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is dangerous and costly [1]. Significant health disparities exist in diabetes outcomes, including DKA recurrence [2], [3]. In the Chicago-area, readmissions for DKA occur with higher frequency in individuals from racial and ethnic minority groups and in those with unmet social needs [2]. Wearable technology use, including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), improves glycemic metrics [4] and is associated with reduced hospitalization for DKA [5]. While these technologies are available, utilization is low in some groups [6]. A recent analysis of CGM use conducted by the American Diabetes Association reported that, “The greatest access barrier shows up when we combine income with race.” [7] Promoting technology use by assisting with insurance challenges and other barriers has been identified as a potential intervention to overcome disparities [8]. A systematic review of over 60 patient navigation studies found that patient navigation improves processes of care [9]. Here, we describe an intervention designed to increase CGM utilization and potentially reduce rates of DKA readmission by identifying and addressing barriers to CGM use via patient navigation support.

Methods

This is a prospective single-arm interventional study, Using Technology to Address Disparities and Promote Healthcare Equity in Type 1 Diabetes (EquiT1D), designed to understand the impact of offering patient navigator support on CGM utilization for people living with T1D who are at high risk for DKA admissions. We recruited non-pregnant adults ages 18–64 years with T1D who had a history of two or more hospitalizations for DKA in the five years preceding enrollment and agreed to share CGM data with the study team via system-specific smartphone applications and software platforms. The trial was not registered prospectively but the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Chicago on October 9, 2020, before enrollment of the first participant.

Participants were prescribed real-time CGM as part of the standard of care and received instructions to share the data with the study team via an application on a smartphone. If a participant did not have a compatible smartphone, they were provided one by the study. After the initial contact, participants were contacted regularly by a patient navigator for one year. The patient navigator in this intervention is a certified diabetes care and education specialist (CDCES) able to address multiple aspects of type 1 diabetes care. The protocol was revised to increase the number of contacts from an original maximum of eight scheduled contacts per year (week 2, month 1 (M1), M2, M3, M5, M6, M9, M12) to a maximum of 14 (week 2 (W2), W4, W6, W8, month 3 (M3), M4, M5, M6, M7, M8, M9, M10, M11, M12). A total of three attempts were made for each participant contact. Participants were offered the option to reach out to the patient navigator if issues arose between scheduled contacts. Methods of communication included phone calls, text messages, email, patient portal messages, in-person clinic visits, and during hospitalizations. The patient navigator tailored the contact method to each participant's preferences.

At each contact, participants were queried about access to and barriers to CGM use to develop an individualized action plan that addressed restarting or continuing CGM use, as necessary. Discussion topics included a review of recent hospitalizations for DKA or other indications, confirming that participants had sufficient medications and supplies, and confirming date, time, and transportation to their next endocrinology appointment. If needed, the patient navigator would help call technical support with the patient to troubleshoot issues with CGM technology, work with the pharmacy to ensure that participants were able to receive CGM and other diabetes supplies, help schedule follow-up appointments, discuss other diabetes technology including CSII, and offer navigation support to social work and mental health resources. If urgent issues were identified, the patient navigator would message the participant's endocrinologist to follow-up on potential adjustments in the insulin regimen or to address issues that were outside of the scope of the intervention.

The primary outcome was CGM utilization, as CGM use is correlated with T1D outcomes, including diabetes-related hospitalizations [5], [10]. CGM utilization was defined as the percentage of days of CGM wear (i.e., days with data) over a lookback period based on scheduled patient navigator contacts. The date of enrollment was defined as time zero. Baseline CGM utilization was defined as the percentage of sensor wear days in the 14 days prior to enrollment. For the first eight weeks of study participation, CGM utilization was defined as the percentage of wear days using a 14-day lookback period (e.g., if the sensor was worn for 10 of the 14 days, CGM utilization was calculated as 71.4%). For the remaining ten months of participation, a 30-day lookback period was used. CGM utilization was determined by reviewing participants' data on CGM-specific software platforms designed to share data and identify utilization, patterns, and statistics. If the patient navigator could not reach the participant at the time of scheduled contact, the CGM utilization data was reviewed and uploaded to the study database.

Secondary outcomes included self-reported hospitalizations, comparing 12 months before to 12 months after enrollment, and changes in diabetes self-care and diabetes-related quality of life questionnaires, which were measured at baseline, six months, and twelve months. Diabetes self-care was measured using the Self-Care Inventory-revised (SCI) [11]. Diabetes-related quality of life was measured using the Diabetes Quality of Life Brief Clinical Inventory (D-QOL) [12] scores can range from 15 to 75 with a higher score indicating a more negative perception of a problem and lower patient satisfaction. Covariates included age, sex, insurance coverage, duration of diabetes, diabetes complications, insulin administration regimen (e.g., twice daily premixed insulin, multiple daily injections, CSII), and number of hospitalizations with DKA at baseline. Survey data, CGM utilization data, details from CGM report, and other study data were collected and managed in a secure web platform (REDCap™) hosted at the University of Chicago [13], [14]. To evaluate the intervention effect (difference from baseline), we used a linear mixed model (LMM) to model the continuous outcomes (monthly CGM utilization rate, A1c, D-QOL, and SCI). We adjusted for each baseline outcome and covariates. We checked correlation coefficients to avoid multi-collinearity and then conducted the backward model selection until the Bayesian information criterion, the model fit criterion, was no more decreased. We conducted a mediator analysis by adding CGM utilization into the final reduced model. For the analysis of count data (hospitalizations), we utilized the generalized estimation equation (GEE).

Primary outcome: CGM utilization

At enrollment, 23 of 30 participants (77%) reported having been prescribed CGM at some point before study entry, whereas 7 of 30 participants (23%) reported never having been prescribed a CGM. Hospitalizations

To evaluate the impact of the intervention, we compared the number of hospitalizations for DKA in the year prior to enrollment to the number during the year of enrollment. The mean number (SD, median, IQR) of hospitalizations declined from 2.90 (2.95, 2, 4) to 2.07 (2.51, 1, 3) (P = 0.04) during the 12 months after enrollment.

Glycemic outcomes

Changes in HbA1c could be analyzed in 28 of the 30 participants; two participants are not included because one died from diabetes complications and the other has a hemoglobinopathy that precludes use of HbA1c as a reliable glycemic indicator. Participants' mean (SD) HbA1c was reduced from 10.9%, 95.6 mmol/mol (2.8%) at baseline to 10.4%, 90.2 mmol/mol (2.6%) at 12 months but these findings were not significant (P = 0.09). Fourteen percent of participants had a 1% or greater increase in HbA1c. Notably, a participant with an over 4% increase in HbA1c reported several incidents of severe hypoglycemia prior to enrollment and reported the incidence of severe hypoglycemia decreased after enrollment. Twenty-nine percent of participants had a > 1% decrease in HbA1c. CGM utilization over the study period was found to be a mediator for reduction in HbA1c.

Diabetes-related quality of life and self-care

Study participants had better diabetes-related quality of life (D-QOL) after the intervention. Mean (SD) of participants' D-QOL was 33.9 (5.3) at baseline, 31.8 (7.7) at 6 months, and to 29.2 (6.2) at 12 months (P = 0.002 for baseline vs 12 months). A lower D-QOL score indicates better quality of life. There was no change in reported self-care over time. Participants' SCI was 61.9 (11.1) at baseline, 68.3 (11.2) at 6 months, and 64.1 (16.1) at 12 months (P > 0.05).

Discussion and conclusions

These results support the need for continuing efforts to develop and study interventions that optimize diabetes technology utilization in populations with recurrent admissions for DKA. Disparities in prescribing practices have been documented [6]. Efforts to reduce barriers to uptake have been impactful [15], however, prescribing diabetes technology alone is insufficient for many patients, as more than 75% of study participants reported being prescribed CGM prior to enrollment but less than 25% were using CGM at the time of enrollment. Work is ongoing in this area regarding the sustained use of diabetes technology in underserved populations. Wolf et al. conducted a qualitative study in which participants identified barriers and solutions to technology use. The solutions were then incorporated into the development of a diabetes navigator toolkit [16]. We found that patient navigation support provided by a certified diabetes care and education specialist was associated with higher CGM utilization and reduced hospitalizations for DKA when comparing the year before study enrollment to the year of enrollment. Key aspects of the intervention included assisting participants with overcoming insurance and other CGM access issues, contacting participants based on participants' preferred method of communication, repeat attempts at each scheduled contact, providing smartphones, addressing issues such as behavioral health needs and food insecurity, as well as serving as a knowledgeable resource regarding living with type 1 diabetes. Although the focus of this intervention was on CGM utilization, methods of insulin administration were also documented and, when appropriate, CSII use was encouraged during patient navigator contacts. Seven of 24 participants that were using multiple daily injections of insulin at the time of enrollment transitioned to CSII during the study period.

A notable limitation of this initial phase of the study was lack of a control group. This weakens the interpretation of results. This study highlights the need for additional research to further understand factors affecting CGM utilization, strategies to optimize sustained technology use, and the type and intensity of diabetes management support necessary to improve outcomes among persons living with type 1 diabetes.
