Summary

Thalassemias impose a substantial but highly uneven global health burden. Using Global Burden of Disease 2023 estimates, we quantified the prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of thalassemias across 204 countries and territories from 1980 to 2023 and evaluated health system performance with frontier analysis. Although the global age-standardized DALY rate declined by 44.8% from 1990 to 2023, this overall reduction masks stark regional and demographic divergences. Specifically, the burden surged among women aged 20–54 years in the Caribbean and those aged >55 years in southern sub-Saharan Africa. In sharp contrast, China achieved a 78.2% reduction in age-standardized DALY rates during the same period. These divergent trajectories highlight critical gaps in global health system performance and underscore the imperative for tailored, equitable public health strategies focused on comprehensive screening, chronic disease management, and reproductive health.

Introduction

Thalassemias, a group of common inherited hemoglobinopathies caused by deficient globin chain synthesis, constitute a significant global disease burden.1 Severe clinical phenotypes are driven by ineffective erythropoiesis and transfusion-induced iron overload, leading to life-threatening cardiac, endocrine, and hepatic complications.2,3,4 In 2021, the age-standardized incidence was 1.93 per 100,000, with the burden highest among young children and in regions across Southeast and South Asia.5,6,7

The management of thalassemias has undergone a paradigm shift over the past few decades. For years, the standard of care was supportive, relying on regular blood transfusions to correct anemia, and iron chelation therapy to mitigate the toxic effects of iron overload.8 More recently, the therapeutic landscape has been revolutionized by breakthroughs that target the underlying pathophysiology.8,9,10

Previous Global Burden of Disease (GBD) analyses show that from 1990 to 2021, a significant global decline in age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) for thalassemias paradoxically coincided with increased prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), particularly evident in high-burden regions such as South and Southeast Asia, which underscores the disease’s transformation into a manageable chronic condition with a demographic burden shift toward older age groups.6,7 However, these studies often neglect the subnational heterogeneity, demographic-specific disease patterns, and formal evaluations of health system efficiency crucial for guiding targeted resource allocation. To address these critical knowledge gaps, our study conducts a granular spatiotemporal analysis of the GBD 2023 data across 786 regions and introduces a key methodological innovation: the application of frontier analysis to benchmark health system performance.

Regarding mortality, the global ASMR decreased from 0.4 (0.3–0.5) in 1980 to 0.2 (0.1–0.3) in 2023, with the highest rate observed in the <20 years population (Table 1). Socioeconomically, the low SDI region bore the heaviest burden with an ASMR of 0.3 (0.2–0.5) (Figure 1 and Data S1). Regionally, among 21 GBD regions, Southeast Asia, western Sub-Saharan Africa, and central Sub-Saharan Africa reported the highest ASMR, with rates of 0.41 (0.28–0.56), 0.41 (0.20–1.14), and 0.33 (0.03–1.70), respectively. However, contrary to the global downward trend, southern Sub-Saharan Africa and the Caribbean exhibited concerning increases of ASMR from 1990 to 2023, with the change rate of 31.4% (−16.4% to 119.8%) and 25.6% (−16.3% to 108.7%) (Figure 1 and Data S1). At the national level, Guinea-Bissau consistently recorded the highest ASMR of 2.0 (0.2–12.5) in 2023. Notably, 71 countries and territories exceeded the global average (Data S1). Furthermore, all countries and territories within central, southern, and western Sub-Saharan Africa ranked in the top 100 for mortality (Data S1). Subnationally, Ekiti, a subnational region of Nigeria, recorded the highest subnational ASMR, with the rate of 1.1 (0.0–8.7) (Data S1). All subnational regions in Indonesia and Ethiopia surpassed the global mortality average. Upward trend among women aged 20–54 years in the Caribbean

Notably, the Caribbean region exhibited an upward trend in the burden of thalassemias from 1990 to 2023, marked by significant increases in both ASMR and age-standardized DALY rate (Figures 3A and 3B). This trajectory displayed pronounced age and sex heterogeneity, driven primarily by the adult population aged 20–54 years, which experienced a rise of 43.5% (−5.5% to 122.1%) in death rate and an increase of 47.9% (−3.6% to 129.2%) in DALY rate (Data S1). Stratified analysis indicated that this escalation was almost entirely attributable to females, who recorded a substantial increase of 69.8% (3.2%–172.8%) in DALY rate, whereas the burden among males remained largely stable over the same period (Table 1 and Data S1). At the national level, Haiti bore the heaviest burden among 19 countries and territories in the Caribbean region (Appendix S2 and S3). Upward trend among women aged >55 years in sub-Saharan Africa

In sharp contrast to the burden concentrated among women of childbearing age in the Caribbean, the evolving epidemiological profile of thalassemias in sub-Saharan Africa is characterized by a distinct shift toward elderly females.

While central, eastern, and western sub-Saharan Africa displayed overall declining trends in ASMR and age-standardized DALY rates, driven by reductions in the <20 years and 20–54 years age groups (Figures 1 and 2). However, a concerning increase emerged among individuals aged >55 years (Figures 1 and 2). Within these three GBD regions, western sub-Saharan Africa recorded the most substantial rises in both deaths and DALY rates for the >55 years population, increasing by 16.4% (−39.9% to 91.9%) and 13.8% (−40.2% to 86.6%), respectively (Figures 1 and 2 and Data S1). Furthermore, southern sub-Saharan Africa exhibited an overall upward trend in its age-standardized burden, a trajectory driven primarily by the >55 years age group, which experienced significant surges in death rates among females (54.7%, −3.5% to 168.6%) and DALY rate of 51.2% (−5.9% to 161.7%) (Table 1, Figures 1 and 2, and Data S1).

Sex-specific analysis across these four sub-Saharan Africa regions, including central, eastern, western, and southern sub-Saharan Africa, revealed profound disparities among females aged >55 years (Figure 4). Within this demographic, central sub-Saharan Africa registered the highest mortality and DALY rates for women aged >55 years, at 1.3 (0.0–11.5) and 35.9 (1.1–310.6), respectively (Data S1). At the national level, 35 of the 46 countries and territories across sub-Saharan Africa reported mortality and DALY rates for women >55 years that exceeded the global average (Data S1). Guinea-Bissau bore the heaviest national burden for this cohort, recording rates of 2.5 (0.0–23.3) for mortality and 69.2 (1.3–663.7) for DALYs (Data S1). Moreover, over two-thirds of countries and territories in sub-Saharan Africa experienced increasing trends in both mortality and DALY rates for female >55 years from 1990 to 2023 (Appendix S2 and Data S1).Figure 4 Global and regional sex differences in thalassemias

Subnational analyses provided further granularity regarding burden distribution. Among the 106 subnational regions in sub-Saharan Africa, Ekiti, a subnational region in Nigeria, recorded the highest mortality rate for women aged >55 years in 2023, at 6.5 (0.0–62.7) (Appendix S2 and Data S1). Meanwhile, Ondo, another subnational region in Nigeria, registered the highest DALY rate for the same group, reaching 151.9 (0.6–1538.7) (Appendix S2 and Data S1). Notably, more than 85% of these subnational regions exhibited increasing trends in both mortality and DALY rates for women aged >55 years between 1990 and 2023, underscoring a widespread regional challenge (Appendix S2).

Reduced burden of thalassemias with advanced treatment in China

China demonstrated a remarkable reduction in thalassemias burden from 1990 to 2023. In 1990, the disease burden in China was substantially higher than the global average. The ASMR in China was 0.6 (0.5–0.9) versus 0.3 (0.3–0.4) globally, and the age-standardized DALY rate stood at 49.3 (36.8–66.7) compared to the global level of 26.0 (20.2–32.7) (Figure 3B). However, by 2023, the epidemiological profile had shifted favoring China, with an ASMR of 0.1 (0.1–0.2) and a DALY rate of 10.8 (8.3–13.7), both significantly lower than the global averages of 0.2 (0.1–0.3) and 14.4 (9.5–21.3), respectively (Figure 3 and Data S1). Detailed data for 2023 reveal that among females in China, the death rate was 0.09 (0.07–0.14) and the DALY rate was 5.2 (3.7–7.2) in the 20–54 years age group, while the corresponding rates for those aged >55 years were 0.08 (0.05–0.12) and 2.3 (1.5–3.5), respectively.

Trend analysis further highlights that the pace of improvement in China significantly outpaced the global average. From 1990 to 2023, China achieved an ASMR reduction of 78.7% (−86.0% to −68.2%) and a DALY rate reduction of 78.2% (−85.1% to −68.1%), markedly exceeding the change rate at the global level (Data S1). For women aged 20–54 years, the mortality rate decreased by 72.0% (95% uncertainty intervals [UI]: −84.5% to −52.3%), and the DALY rate by 73.2% (95% UI: −83.9% to −55.7%). A similar reduction was observed in females aged 55 years and older, with a 70.3% (95% UI: −84.2% to −48.5%) decline in the death rate and a 70.7% (95% UI: −84.2% to −49.1%) decline in the DALY rate.

Frontiers analysis among women aged >55 years

The frontier analysis for mortality and DALY rates among women aged >55 years reveals a concerning global trajectory from 1990 to 2023. Regarding mortality, over 99.8% of countries and territories failed to reach the effective difference (EF) in 2023 (Figure 5A). Guinea-Bissau exhibited the greatest distance from the frontier of death rate among women aged >55 years (Figure 5A). Dynamic trend analysis highlights a widening disparity, with 90 nations experiencing an increase in their EF gap between 1990 and 2023, signaling a growing divergence from achievable optimal levels (Figure 5A). Guinea-Bissau recorded the largest expansion in this gap from 1990 to 2023 (Figure 5A). A secondary subnational analysis of 389 subnational regions revealed that 257 (66.1%) regions did not achieve the frontier level of mortality (Appendix S2). Discussion

Our study reveals two distinct and concerning upward trends in specific female populations. In sub-Saharan Africa, we identified a significant epidemiological shift, with the burden increasingly impacting women aged over 55 years. Meanwhile, the rising trend in the Caribbean was driven primarily by women of childbearing age. Furthermore, China demonstrated a remarkable reduction in its thalassemia burden, significantly outpacing the global rate of decline.

A key finding of our study is the significant and escalating burden among women aged >55 years in sub-Saharan Africa. Nowadays, advanced therapies targeting the underlying genetic defect or addressing ineffective erythropoiesis have emerged, offering the potential for a cure or a reduced transfusion burden.11 Collectively, these developments, often combined with early diagnosis via high-throughput sequencing, have successfully converted thalassemias from a fatal pediatric illness into a manageable chronic condition.4,12 Paradoxically, these advances created new patterns of disease burden even as they extend patient survival.

While gene therapies and novel agents offer curative potential elsewhere, patients in resource-limited regions like sub-Saharan Africa rely almost entirely on conventional supportive care.11,13 Notably, therapeutic regimen, including regular blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy, is pivotal for alleviating severe anemia, supporting normal growth and development, and improving overall survival by mitigating acute complications.14 Although this approach successfully extends lifespan, the long-term consequences of chronic iron overload, including cardiomyopathy, liver fibrosis, and various endocrinopathies, escalate dramatically with advancing age.13 This situation is further compounded by a widespread lack of efficient diagnostic technologies and genetic screening systems, which is acutely amplified in resource-limited regions like sub-Saharan Africa.12

High poverty rates, coupled with insufficient public investment in healthcare, have resulted in a critical deficiency in basic chronic disease management and preventive services in sub-Saharan Africa.15 This systemic issue disproportionately affects women. Households headed by females, for instance, experience greater deprivation within the health dimension of the multidimensional poverty index, a clear indication that their economic disadvantages directly translate into elevated risks of morbidity and mortality.16 The recent COVID-19 pandemic has cast a harsher light on these pre-existing disparities, as public health control measures inadvertently exacerbated inequities in healthcare access and magnified the health vulnerabilities of elderly women.17

Furthermore, a substantial number of women in sub-Saharan Africa are engaged in prolonged, high-intensity physical work, such as agriculture.14,18 This strenuous labor can worsen the physiological symptoms of anemia. Concurrently, an anemic state leads to chronic fatigue, diminished cognitive function, and a marked reduction in labor productivity.19 This creates a vicious cycle where poor health and poverty become mutually reinforcing, trapping individuals and communities in a state of compounded vulnerability and exacerbating the disease burden of thalassemias.

Concurrently, our analysis reveals a distinct pattern in the Caribbean, where the burden is concentrated among women aged 20–54 years. A primary challenge is the pronounced regional imbalance in prenatal screening; while systematic programs exist in select territories such as Jamaica and Guadeloupe, most of the region lacks comprehensive screening mechanisms, preventing timely detection and intervention.20 Furthermore, the effectiveness of prevention is undermined by a scarcity of medical resources, creating significant barriers for low-income and rural communities.21 The strain on healthcare infrastructure is exacerbated by migration, as low screening uptake among pregnant migrants indirectly hinders thalassemia management.22 Moreover, long-standing health inequalities disproportionately impact Afro-descendant populations, whose high prevalence of baseline anemia due to socioeconomic factors complicates the thalassemia-specific disease burden.23 The complex history of interethnic admixture may facilitate the propagation and interaction of high-risk alleles. The high genetic heterogeneity of the Caribbean, stemming from African, European, Indigenous, and Latino ancestries, creates a diverse landscape of hemoglobinopathies.24 Studies in Jamaica and Colombia demonstrate a strong correlation between ancestral components and the prevalence of specific mutations, such as the high frequency of HbS on the African-ancestry-rich Pacific coast of Colombia.25,26 Therefore, addressing this multifaceted issue necessitates a coordinated regional effort to establish a unified screening network and to develop comprehensive maternal health strategies for these vulnerable populations.20

With global therapeutic breakthroughs reducing the burden of thalassemias, the progress achieved in China provides a powerful blueprint for a successful strategy. The high cost-effectiveness and feasibility of its population-wide prevention are fundamentally underpinned by a relatively homogeneous genetic spectrum. Epidemiological studies in endemic southern provinces, such as Guangdong and Jiangxi, demonstrate that a limited panel of common mutations accounts for the vast majority of thalassemia alleles.27,28 This restricted mutation pool enables the widespread deployment of highly standardized and cost-effective molecular diagnostic tools.29 Consequently, government-led initiatives such as Guangxi’s “zero birth defects” plan, leveraging next-generation sequencing (NGS), have dramatically reduced the incidence of new births with severe thalassemia.30,31 The core of its curative strategy is the widespread application and innovation of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), the definitive cure for severe, transfusion-dependent patients.32 By pioneering the use of alternative donors, including haploidentical relatives and combined haplo-cord transplants, China has effectively overcome donor shortages, achieving impressive 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rates exceeding 90% for unrelated donor transplants.33,34 Continuous optimization of preconditioning regimens has significantly enhanced safety by lowering the incidence of complications like hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD) and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD).34 This exemplifies a complete ecosystem of care, spanning from population-wide prevention to state-of-the-art, accessible curative options.

Beyond these clinical breakthroughs, the widespread success of the Chinese model is deeply anchored in robust financial protection mechanisms. The continuous expansion of national basic medical insurance and catastrophic medical insurance programs has significantly alleviated the out-of-pocket financial burden for rare disease management.35 By reimbursing a substantial proportion of inpatient medical expenses, this financial safety net effectively transforms previously prohibitive treatments into universally accessible care.36 This exemplifies a complete ecosystem of care, spanning from population-wide prevention to state-of-the-art, accessible curative options.

However, a critical examination of this national success reveals remaining systemic vulnerabilities that must be urgently addressed. Firstly, a stark urban-rural and regional disparity persists in resource allocation and chronic disease management.29 While cutting-edge HSCT and comprehensive care are highly accessible in urban areas, clinical services in peripheral and rural centers remain suboptimal. Patients relying on these grassroot facilities frequently face profound challenges in accessing consistent, safe blood transfusions and standardized follow-up care. This gap is largely driven by significant hospital heterogeneity and regional disparities in blood utilization efficiency and clinical management capacities.37,38 Consequently, the lack of equitable access to basic chelation and transfusion therapies in these areas undermines the overall survival benefits achieved by top-tier hospitals. Furthermore, China’s massive internal population migration poses a novel public health challenge, driving a phenomenon of “south-to-north” epidemiological shift. Large-scale labor migration and inter-provincial marriages are continuously disseminating thalassemia alleles from traditional southern epicenters to northern and eastern, historically non-endemic provinces.39 As non-endemic regions lacking screening infrastructure increasingly encounter migrant carriers, the risks of misdiagnosis and new severe births rise, threatening targeted prevention networks. Optimizing the Chinese model requires homogenizing tiered care and establishing a national genetic registry to manage cross-provincial mobile populations.

While the Chinese blueprint serves as an aspirational goal, profound contextual differences preclude its direct application in resource-limited regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and the Caribbean. Extreme genetic heterogeneity in these regions complicates the standardization of low-cost diagnostic panels, while severe financial and infrastructural deficits hinder advanced genomic implementation.40,41,42,43 Therefore, addressing this burden requires a tailored, phased strategy prioritizing ultra-low-cost primary prevention and capacity building.

To circumvent infrastructure deficits, genetic counseling and public health education should be systematically integrated into existing, functional maternal and child health networks, such as those established for malaria prevention.43,44,45 Concurrently, for population-level carrier screening, health systems should initially bypass costly molecular assays and instead mandate the use of basic erythrocyte indices as the frontline diagnostic triage. Extensive evidence confirms that mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) are accessible and remarkably cost-effective predictors for detecting thalassemia.46,47,48,49 Leveraging these inexpensive hematological parameters allows for the rapid identification of at-risk couples, making mass screening economically viable even in the absence of advanced molecular laboratories.

Building upon primary prevention, subsequent strategies must transition toward standardized supportive care, prioritizing the establishment of regional blood centers to address systemic deficiencies in blood safety across sub-Saharan Africa.50 Given the regional incidence of transfusion-transmitted infections, implementing pathogen reduction technologies and centralized blood collection policies is urgently required.51,52,53 Concurrently, broadening access to basic, affordable oral iron chelators is vital for mitigating transfusional iron overload and reducing pediatric mortality.50,54 As healthcare economies mature, long-term objectives should advance toward precision diagnostics, utilizing localized, low-cost molecular tools like loop-mediated isothermal amplification as highly efficient alternatives to expensive conventional polymerase chain reaction.55,56 Ultimately, while HSCT remains cost-prohibitive, establishing small-scale regional transplant centers through international collaboration represents a feasible pathway to clinical cures in emerging economies.57

Our analysis reveals divergent thalassemia burden trajectories, with escalating loads in aging sub-Saharan African and reproductive-age Caribbean women highlighting critical healthcare gaps. Conversely, China’s success with an integrated care system offers a proven blueprint. This evidence mandates a paradigm shift from monolithic control toward targeted, equitable strategies tailored to distinct demographic challenges like aging and reproductive health.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. The analysis is constrained by the heterogeneous quality of primary data, unmeasured resource disparities, COVID-19-related reporting delays, and inherent GBD analytical model constraints. Specifically, regarding data quality, the lack of ubiquitous access to precise molecular diagnostics in resource-limited settings may compromise the diagnostic granularity of primary mortality registries, introducing inherent uncertainties into these regional estimates. Additionally, aggregating individuals over 55 into a single cohort to capture macro-level trends limits demographic granularity, which may mask distinct comorbidity profiles and chronic care needs between the 55–74 and ≥75 years age groups.

Method details

Data availability and ethical statement

We leveraged the GBD 2023 framework to estimate thalassemias burden across 204 countries, sourcing age sex, and Socio-demographic Index (SDI)-disaggregated data for prevalence, mortality, and DALYs from the IHME Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx). Specifically, data were extracted utilizing the precise GBD Cause ID for thalassemias (Cause ID 614), ensuring that sickle cell disorders (Cause ID 615) and other distinct hemoglobin variants were strictly excluded from the analysis to ensure clinical validity of the regional comparisons. All rates were standardized per 100,000 population. The analysis drew from 2328 mortality and 129 non-fatal data sources (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2023/sources), employing spatiotemporal smoothing and Bayesian hierarchical models to address data sparsity. This study adheres to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER), with detailed methodology in Appendix S1, with the full dataset accessible at https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/.58 A Statistical modeling was performed using R (v4.2.0), and geospatial visualizations were generated with Python (v3.9.19), with ethics approval waived for anonymized population-level data.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical computations and data visualizations were executed using R Project for Statistical Computing (v4.2.0) and Python (v3.9.19). For an exhaustive description of all statistical modeling and testing protocols applied in this study, please refer to Appendix S1.

Frontier analysis

Frontier analysis determined the minimum potentially achievable burden by relating SDI to key epidemiological indicators.59,60 The frontier was constructed using the non-parametric Free Disposal Hull (FDH) method with bootstrap iterations for uncertainty. We generated 1,000 bootstrapped samples. In each iteration, analytical locations were sampled with replacement while preserving repeated observations over time, thereby accounting for within-location autocorrelation. For each location-year, the disease-burden estimate was drawn from its uncertainty distribution. An FDH frontier was then fitted to each bootstrapped dataset. Potential outlying frontier units were identified using the super-efficiency criterion and were excluded if their super-efficiency score was greater than 1. The bootstrapped frontier values were then averaged at each SDI level rounded to the nearest 0.001. Finally, LOESS regression was applied to these averaged frontier values to obtain a smooth frontier. This procedure allowed the SDI–burden relationship to vary flexibly across the SDI range, rather than imposing a linear, log-linear, Cobb-Douglas, or translog functional form.60
