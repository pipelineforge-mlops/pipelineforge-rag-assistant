Background

The West Haven–Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory (MPI) assesses multidimensional aspects of chronic pain across five domains: pain severity, pain-related life interference, affective distress, social support, and life control. In axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), pain frequently extends beyond inflammation and involves nociplastic and central sensitization mechanisms.

Objectives

To assess the construct validity of the psychosocial section of the MPI (part 1) by examining its correlations with measures included in the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS) core domain set.

Design

Observational post hoc analysis of a multicenter randomized controlled trial.

Methods

Patients with axSpA and a pain score ≥4 (0–10) were prospectively enrolled. In addition to the five MPI domains, measures of disease activity, patient global assessment (PtGA), physical function (BASFI), health-related quality of life, fatigue, total back pain, morning stiffness, and spinal mobility (BASMI) were assessed.

Results

A total of 136 patients were included. Pain-related life interference correlated strongly with disease activity, BASFI, health-related quality of life, fatigue, and total back pain, and moderately with PtGA, mental component score (MCS), morning stiffness, and BASMI. Pain severity correlated strongly with disease activity and total back pain. Affective distress correlated strongly with MCS. Social support showed only weak associations, while life control correlated strongly with MCS but weakly with other domains.

Conclusion

The MPI-part 1 demonstrated construct validity in axSpA. Its domains capture psychosocial pain aspects like social support not reflected in current ASAS core domain set, supporting its potential value for personalized pain assessment and research in axSpA.

Registration

German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00037509).

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the axial skeleton, including the sacroiliac joints and spine, often leading to significant pain, stiffness, and disability. 1 While pharmacologic advancements such as biologic (b-) and targeted synthetic (ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) have significantly improved disease outcomes, many patients continue to experience persistent pain despite effective suppression of inflammation. 2 Pain in axSpA is a multifaceted symptom with contributions from nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic mechanisms. 3 Nociceptive pain arises from inflammatory processes in the axial skeleton, whereas neuropathic pain results from nerve damage or dysfunction. 4 Nociplastic pain, on the other hand, is characterized by altered central pain processing, leading to pain hypersensitivity even in the absence of active inflammation. 5 In axSpA, it is increasingly recognized that pain extends beyond purely nociceptive mechanisms driven by inflammatory processes.6,7 Emerging evidence highlights the prevalence of neuropathic pain in axSpA, which primarily results from damage or dysfunction within the nervous system.8–12 Additionally, nociplastic pain, characterized by altered central pain processing, is commonly reported in patients with axSpA, where fibromyalgia frequently occurs as a comorbid condition.12–14 Traditional disease activity measures such as the Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) and the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) predominantly assess inflammatory activity and patient-reported symptoms but do not fully capture the complexity of pain in axSpA.15,16 Furthermore, the presence of neuropathic and nociplastic pain components in patients with axSpA may alter patient-reported outcomes and thereby influence the interpretation of various disease specific assessments including disease activity measures such as ASDAS and BASDAI.6,14,17,18 To address this gap, various pain assessment tools have been developed. The PainDETECT questionnaire is commonly used to evaluate neuropathic pain characteristics, such as burning sensations, electric shocks, and numbness.7,19 So far, the Widespread Pain Index (WPI) has been the primary tool applied to investigate nociplastic pain in patients with axSpA. 12 However, most questionnaires for nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain do not account for psychosocial dimensions of pain. The psychosocial section of the West Haven-Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory (MPI-part 1) was designed to assess multiple aspects of pain experience, including pain-related life interference, affective distress, social support, and perceived life control. The MPI has also been applied in rheumatoid arthritis, demonstrating that cognitive and psychosocial factors are closely linked to pain intensity and disability and that pain extends beyond physical impairment and is only partially explained by clinical parameters.20,21 By capturing these broader dimensions, the MPI offers a more holistic evaluation of pain that extends beyond traditional assessments of pain in axSpA. Originally developed in the US, the MPI has been successfully adapted and validated in German-speaking populations. 22

In this study, we aimed to assess the construct validity of the MPI (MPI-part 1) in axSpA by examining its correlations with established scores within the ASAS-OMERACT core domain set.23,24 Additionally, we evaluated the sensitivity to change of the MPI, particularly in response to a digital behavioral intervention aimed at improving pain management.25–27

Methods

Study design and participants

This post-hoc analysis is based on data derived from a previously conducted, multicenter, randomized controlled trial in patients with axial spondyloarthritis. The study was conducted between September 2023 and June 2024 across four rheumatology centers in Germany and the study design, methodology, and primary outcomes have been described in detail elsewhere.28,29 In brief, adult patients (≥18 years) with a clinical diagnosis of axSpA were eligible if they had a symptom duration of at least six months, stable disease control under pharmacological therapy, and a baseline pain intensity of ≥4 on a numerical rating scale (NRS, 0–10). Stable disease control was defined by the treating rheumatologist’s judgement that no change in immunomodulatory or analgesic therapy was indicated. Exclusion criteria included language barriers, inability to provide informed consent, and planned changes in biologic (b), targeted synthetic (ts) or conventional (cs) DMARD therapy. Patients were randomized 1:1 to either an intervention group, which received access to a digital health application (ACT-app), or to standard of care (SOC) and were followed over 12 weeks. The primary aim of the trial was to evaluate the effect of a digital behavioral intervention (ACT-app) on pain, assessed using various patient-reported outcome measures. 28

Assessments

Demographic and clinical data (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], symptom duration, disease duration, comorbidities, and treatment history) were obtained from medical records. Laboratory values included C-reactive protein (CRP). The Charlson Comorbidity Index was calculated, and current nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) intake was quantified using the NSAID index.30,31 In addition, the use of b-, ts- and csDMARDs was recorded. Disease activity was assessed with the ASDAS and the BASDAI, including subitems for fatigue (BASDAI item 1), total back pain (BASDAI item 2), and morning stiffness (mean of BASDAI items 5 and 6). 32 Patient global assessment (PtGA) of disease activity was also collected. Physical function was evaluated with the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI), and spinal mobility was measured using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI). 32 Health-related quality of life was assessed with the Short Form-36 (SF-36), providing the physical (PCS) and mental component scores (MCS), as well as the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society Health Index (ASAS-HI).33,34

The MPI contains of three main sections. The first section, Pain Experience and Psychosocial Impact, evaluates pain severity, pain-related life interference, affective distress, social support, and perceived life control. The second section, responses from significant Others, focuses on the way close relatives or caregivers react to the patient’s pain, categorizing responses as solicitous, distracting, or punishing behaviors. The third section, Daily Activities and Functional Status, assesses how pain affects routine tasks, such as household chores, social activities, and recreation. For the present study, we focus specifically on the psychometric performance of the psychosocial section of the MPI (MPI-part I) in axSpA. All subsequent analyses refer to this psychosocial section (MPI part I).

The first section was designed to assess multidimensional assessment of chronic pain experiences and to evaluate the impact of pain across five key dimensions:1. Pain severity – evaluates the intensity of pain

2. Pain-Related Life Interference – Assesses the extent to which pain disrupts daily activities, work, family, and social interactions.

3. Affective Distress – Measures emotional responses such as anxiety, depression, and frustration associated with chronic pain.

4. Social Support – Examines the level of perceived support from family, friends, and caregivers.

5. Perceived Life Control – Assesses a patient’s sense of control over pain and its impact on life

The MPI-part1 has demonstrated strong internal consistency and validity, ensuring its reliability for measuring pain-related outcome. 35 The MPI has demonstrated sensitivity to change in several longitudinal intervention studies. In interdisciplinary treatment programs for fibromyalgia, MPI subscales and cluster profiles improved significantly following therapy.36,37 Similar responsiveness was confirmed in chronic back pain rehabilitation, where pre–post assessments showed clinically relevant changes across MPI domains. 38 To date, no universally accepted minimal clinically important difference (MCID) or standardized cut-off values have been established for its subscale. The MPI has also been applied in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), where it demonstrated that cognitive factors were closely linked to pain intensity and disability. 20

The 22 items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater impairment, with subscale scores calculated as the mean of the respective items. 35

All assessments, including MPI-part 1 and clinical outcome measures, were collected at baseline (BL) and repeated after 12 weeks (follow-up: FU).

The study was conducted across four rheumatology centers in Germany, approved by the Ethical Committee of the Ruhr-University Bochum (reference number 23-7831), and registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00037509). Informed written consent was obtained from all patients. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 39

Statistical analysis

Construct validity of the MPI-part 1 was evaluated using baseline data (BL) by calculating Pearson’s correlations with established clinical and patient-reported outcome measures (ASDAS, BASDAI, BASFI, BASMI, ASAS HI, SF-36, PCS/MCS). In addition, correlations between MPI domains and demographic as well as clinical variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. This cross-sectional approach was chosen to assess the relationship between MPI domains and established constructs without potential influence of treatment effects. According to Cohen’s guidelines, correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak for r ≥ 0.1, moderate for r ≥ 0.3, and strong for r ≥ 0.5. 40 To evaluate whether MPI domains provide information beyond pain intensity alone, squared semi-partial correlations were calculated. For each outcome measure, the unique proportion of explained variance attributable to the respective MPI domain was determined after adjustment for NRS pain. In addition, exploratory subgroup analyses were performed in patients aged <45 years and in patients with symptom duration <10 years. Responsiveness of the MPI was assessed in patients randomized to the digital behavioral intervention using both baseline (BL) and 12-week follow-up (FU) data. Changes over time were analyzed using paired t-tests, and sensitivity to change was quantified using standardized response means (SRM), calculated as the mean change divided by the standard deviation of the change. An SRM <0.4 was considered to represent a low effect, 0.4–0.79 a moderate effect and ≥0.8 a large effect. 41 A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were based on available-case data. Missing observations are reported for each variable, and no imputation procedures were applied. All analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.2.

Of the 136 participants, 110 completed the MPI. The five MPI domains at baseline showed mean scores of 3.6 (1.2) for pain severity, 3.5 (1.1) for pain-related life interference, 3.2 (1.2) for affective distress, 3.6 (1.7) for social support, and 3.6 (1.1) for life control (Table 2).

Using baseline data (BL), construct validity was demonstrated by correlations ranging from moderate to strong between MPI-part 1 domains and established axSpA outcome measures. The domain “pain-related life interference” showed strong correlations with BASDAI (r = 0.70) and ASDAS (r = 0.55), and moderate inverse correlations with SF-36 physical health (PCS; r = −0.52), indicating good convergent validity.

There were almost no significant correlations between the 5 domains of the MPI and demographic or clinical data (Table 3), but the correlation analyses with disease specific and generic assessments revealed distinct association patterns (Table 4). Pain severity was strongly correlated with BASDAI (r = 0.55), ASDAS (r = 0.50), and total back pain (r = 0.52), and moderately with PtGA (r = 0.40), BASFI (r = 0.40), and fatigue (r = 0.40). Pain-related life interference correlated strongly with BASDAI (r = 0.70), ASDAS (r = 0.55), BASFI (r = 0.59), total back pain (r = 0.59), fatigue (r = 0.55), and ASAS HI (r = 0.54), while negative associations were found with SF-36 PCS (r = −0.52) and MCS (r = −0.44). Affective distress correlated strongly with SF-36 MCS (r = −0.64) and moderately with BASDAI (r = 0.40), ASDAS (r = 0.32), and total back pain (r = 0.38). Social support showed only weak associations, including negative correlations with SF-36 PCS (r = −0.25) and positive correlations with BASMI (r = 0.27). Life control correlated positively with SF-36 MCS (r = 0.50) and negatively with PtGA (r = −0.31) and total back pain (r = −0.30), with weaker negative associations with ASDAS, BASDAI, and BASFI (Table 4). Exploratory subgroup analyses in patients aged <45 years (n=45) and in patients with symptom duration <10 years (n=41) demonstrated correlation patterns that were largely consistent with those observed in the overall cohort (suppl Tables 6 and 7). In both subgroups, pain severity and pain-related life interference remained strongly associated with disease activity, function, and health status measures, while social support showed only weak associations with most clinical outcomes. To assess the incremental value of MPI domains beyond pain intensity alone, additional analyses controlling for NRS pain were performed (suppl. Table 8). Pain-related life interference explained an additional 18% of the variance in BASDAI and SF-36 MCS scores, 15% in fatigue and ASAS Health Index, and 13% in SF-36 PCS. Responsiveness analyses were based on changes between baseline (BL) and 12-week follow-up (FU). Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the psychosocial section of the MPI (MPI-part 1) is capable to capture psychosocial dimensions of pain in patients with axSpA that are not represented in other outcome measures of the ASAS/OMERCAT core domain set. By addressing domains such as pain severity, pain-related life interference, affective distress, social support, and life control, the MPI expands the perspective on pain assessment beyond inflammation and physical function. Importantly, these findings are derived from a multicenter randomized controlled trial, providing a robust methodological framework for the evaluation of construct validity and responsiveness of the MPI in axSpA.

Our analyses confirmed that pain severity and pain-related life interference were strongly associated with disease activity (ASDAS, BASDAI), functional limitations (BASFI), and fatigue, consistent with the expectation that these MPI domains overlap with core measures of axSpA. By contrast, affective distress correlated most strongly with mental health-related quality of life (SF-36 MCS), while life control was associated with both mental health and patient global disease perception. Social support, in turn, showed only weak associations with disease-specific indices, reflecting a domain of patient experience that is not captured by current axSpA assessment instruments. This domain-specific correlation pattern underlines the construct validity of the MPI in axSpA, while also demonstrating its ability to add new information beyond standard assessments.

The MPI may provide additional multidimensional insights into the pain experience in patients with axial spondyloarthritis, complementing established single-item measures such as NRS pain or back pain. In particular, domains such as pain-related interference and affective distress reflect aspects of disease burden that are not captured by unidimensional measures. This was further supported by analyses controlling for NRS pain, indicating that several MPI domains provide information beyond pain intensity alone, particularly pain-related life interference and affective distress.

The importance of psychosocial influences on chronic pain is well established, and the MPI has been specifically developed to assess multidimensional aspects of the pain experience. 35 Since then, the MPI has been validated in various chronic pain conditions, including fibromyalgia chronic low back pain and RA.20,36–38 To our knowledge, this study is among the first to apply the MPI in patients with axial spondyloarthritis, confirming that psychosocial dimensions of pain contribute substantially to the overall disease burden in this patient group. Interestingly, affective distress emerged as a domain with particularly strong correlations to mental health, underscoring that psychological comorbidity plays an important role in axSpA. This aligns with findings from other chronic pain cohorts, where depressive symptoms and distress have been shown to amplify pain perception and disability. 42 In axSpA, studies on mental health outcomes are fewer, but elevated rates of depression and anxiety have been consistently shown. 43 The MPI may therefore provide a structured framework to link these psychosocial factors with pain experience in axSpA.

The dimension of life control was also highly relevant, showing moderate negative associations with disease activity and pain. This suggests that perceived control over pain may buffer the impact of symptoms on overall functioning, echoing prior findings from pain psychology that highlight self-efficacy as a key determinant of coping. 44 Since patient empowerment is increasingly recognized as an essential treatment goal in rheumatology and has also been incorporated into the recent updated ASAS recommendations for the management of axSpA, the MPI could serve as a valuable tool to assess the burden in this domain. 45

Social support, by contrast, showed only weak associations with axSpA-specific measures, consistent with its independence from disease activity. This finding is in line with other studies applying the MPI, where perceived social support influenced coping style and psychological adjustment, but not biological markers.46,47 Importantly, this highlights a gap in conventional axSpA assessments: domains related to social functioning and support networks, which may nonetheless impact treatment adherence, coping, and quality of life.

Our longitudinal data further demonstrated the sensitivity to change of the MPI, as several domains improved significantly in patients using a digital behavioral therapy intervention, 28 however, it has not yet been evaluated in pharmacological trials, and its responsiveness to anti-inflammatory therapy or interventions focusing on physical activity remains unknown.

These findings are consistent with prior longitudinal studies in fibromyalgia and chronic back pain, where MPI subscales were responsive to interdisciplinary rehabilitation and pain therapy.48,49 Thus, the MPI may also serve as a sensitive tool to evaluate non-pharmacological interventions in axSpA.

The present findings suggest that the MPI captures relevant dimensions of the pain experience not fully reflected by conventional disease activity measures and may provide complementary information in the assessment of patients with axSpA. However, at present, its routine use in clinical practice may be limited by time constraints during standard visits. The MPI may therefore be particularly useful in research settings to further explore the multidimensional impact of pain on patients’ lives. However, a better understanding of these aspects may positively influence treatment strategies. However, further studies are needed to evaluate its clinical utility and feasibility in routine care.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our sample was restricted to patients with moderate to high pain levels despite stable pharmacological therapy, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, no universally accepted minimal clinically important difference (MCID) has been defined for MPI subscales, which complicates interpretation of longitudinal change. While the MPI captures psychosocial domains not addressed by standard axSpA indices, it does not include all relevant aspects, such as work productivity, sleep, or fatigue-specific multidimensional measures as outlined in the ASAS/WHO International Classification of Functioning (ICF) Core Set for ankylosing spondylitis. 50 Future research should integrate the MPI with complementary tools to build a more comprehensive assessment of pain and its consequences in axSpA. Although the MPI captures additional multidimensional aspects of pain, it remains to be determined whether this added information impacts clinical decision-making beyond standard unidimensional measures. Additionally, information on baseline mental health comorbidities and the use of medications targeting neuropathic or nociplastic pain was not available.

Conclusion

The MPI-part 1 extends the assessment of pain in axSpA beyond nociceptive and inflammatory dimensions by capturing psychosocial factors such as distress, control, and social support. Our findings support its construct validity and responsiveness in this patient population, and suggest that the MPI may be a valuable adjunct outcome measure in both clinical practice and trials particularly targeting biopsychosocial dimension of pain.
