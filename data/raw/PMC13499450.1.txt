We have carefully reviewed the letter submitted by Hueth and colleagues regarding our Article1 published in 2022. In this letter, we respond to the authors' concerns.

Regarding the use of 0.5 continuity correction, for our analysis, we used the mada R package,2 which implements the Reitsma bivariate random-effects model for diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis. This model jointly synthesizes logit-transformed sensitivity and specificity while accounting for their correlation across studies. The mada R package applies a continuity correction when required to enable estimation in the presence of zero cells (even when this results in either n1 = TP + FN = 0 or n2 = TN + FP = 0) by applying a 0.5 continuity correction to each cell of the corresponding 2 × 2 table. This is a common approach in meta-analysis, given that logit-transformed sensitivity or specificity and the within-study variance are undefined when there are zero cells.3

The cited papers by Tsujimoto et al.4 and Sweeting et al.5 both refer to intervention studies, not diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) studies; their general observations regarding the influence of continuity corrections in sparse data settings are relevant. However, their conclusions cannot be directly transferred to DTA meta-analysis because the underlying statistical structure differs substantially (e.g., the bivariate nature of the data, the two effect sizes – sensitivity and specificity – should be simultaneously considered rather than a single effect size) and induced correlation across studies due to the threshold effect, among others. The mada R package is a widely used, well-established package designed explicitly for conducting DTA meta-analyses. It applies established bivariate methods and is not equivalent to either naïve pooling of individuals across studies or separate pooling of sensitivity and specificity using univariate meta-analytic techniques.6,7

Simple aggregation of 2 × 2 tables across studies is strongly discouraged under all circumstances, as it ignores between-study heterogeneity, the diseased/non-diseased likelihood, imbalances within studies, and ultimately treats the data as if from one ‘mega-study’, ignoring the hierarchical structure of the evidence and thereby introducing bias.

Furthermore, Hueth and colleagues state in their letter that “Using the extracted data, we applied GLMMs via the R (version 4.5) “meta” package to determine fixed-effect and random-effect estimates of sensitivity and specificity”. This approach corresponds to separate pooling of sensitivity and specificity estimates across studies (univariate meta-analytic techniques) and is also inappropriate for two main reasons. First, as outlined in the Cochrane Handbook, heterogeneity is expected in meta-analyses of DTA studies, and therefore, random-effects models are recommended.6 The appropriateness of a statistical model depends on whether it reflects the structure of DTA data. A GLMMs that separately pools sensitivity and specificity estimates treat these as independent outcomes and does not model their within-study correlation. Meta-analysis of DTA studies requires statistical methods that account for the (joint) bivariate structure of the data, where sensitivity and specificity must be modeled jointly rather than as separate, independent outcomes (single proportions). As already commented above, ignoring this joint structure fails to capture the correlation between sensitivity and specificity across studies, often driven by threshold effects, and may lead to biased or misleading results.6

Finally, in our review we used GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) to assess the certainty of evidence in the primary analyses, explicitly considering imprecision due to high uncertainty in effect estimates, which can occur because of small sample sizes, zeros in the 2 × 2 tables, low power, etc., and considering other potential sources of bias in the body of evidence (e.g., study risk of bias).8 This framework ensures that limitations such as those raised are factored into the interpretation of findings.

Lastly, given that our 2022 review may be considered outdated, we plan to update it with newly available evidence from the literature. Following the newest methodological guidance outlined in the 2023 Cochrane Handbook for Diagnostic Test Accuracy Reviews,6 we will also apply a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) approach using the glmer function from the R package lme4.9 This choice is further supported by evidence from a recent large-scale empirical evaluation comparing LMMs and GLMMs.10 Importantly, this differs from separately fitting univariate GLMMs for sensitivity and specificity. The proposed approach using glmer will explicitly model the paired binary outcomes from the 2 × 2 tables within a single hierarchical framework, preserving the dependence structure inherent to DTA data.

Regarding the disagreements with included studies, we agree that a potential selective inclusion may influence DTA estimates in DTA meta-analyses. Fortunately, this is not the case in our meta-analysis. We had very strict inclusion criteria, as our objective was to evaluate DTA rather than mere concordance. This criterion was defined a priori and stated in our PROSPERO registration (CRD42020139285) and detailed in our Methods section: “studies had to simultaneously apply the reference standard (e.g., CSF/blood culture for bacteria, specific PCR, or laboratory-developed tests for viruses) and the index test (i.e., FA/ME) in patients with suspected CNS infections”. Articles that were excluded either did not meet these criteria or lacked sufficient and explicit methodological details. For instance, studies in which the Fa/ME was performed only to a specific subset of patients of the total population, e.g., those that had a particular result in any other test, were excluded because they did not represent the population of interest, i.e., the patients with suspected CNS infection. As specified also in the text: “We excluded studies with unclear information and those whose authors did not respond to our email inquiries; studies that included only immunocompromised patients were also excluded.”

Appendix 3 of the supplementary material of the Article provides detailed explanations for the exclusion of 45 articles that had initially passed screening. Moreover, based on the same principles of eligibility mentioned above, it is worth clarifying that not all the included articles were used for the analysis of every microorganism. In this letter, we aimed to clarify the methodological standards for DTA meta-analysis and to explain why the approaches we applied are statistically appropriate and preferable to naïve data aggregation. Therefore, we consider the results of the previous meta-analysis valid and reliable.
