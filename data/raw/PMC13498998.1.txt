Key Points

Question

Among critically ill pediatric intensive care unit (ICU) patients with prolonged sedative infusion exposure, is greater adherence to identified sedative weaning opportunities associated with shorter ICU and hospital length of stay?

Findings

In this multicenter cohort study of 15 747 ICU encounters from 12 737 children with at least 48 hours of continuous sedative infusion exposure, median weaning adherence was 28%, with 72% of opportunities missed. Each 10% increase in weaning adherence was associated with a 2.3-day shorter ICU length of stay and a 3.6-day shorter hospital length of stay, as well as shorter sedative infusion duration and lower cumulative sedative exposure; all associations were statistically significant.

Meaning

This study suggests that improving adherence to sedative weaning opportunities may represent a modifiable target to reduce sedative exposure and shorten ICU and hospital lengths of stay for critically ill children.

Importance

Prolonged sedative exposure in pediatric intensive care units (ICUs) is associated with adverse outcomes including delirium, withdrawal, and neurodevelopmental impairment. Clinical guidelines recommend timely weaning, but adherence to weaning opportunities and its association with clinical outcomes is unknown.

Objectives

To evaluate adherence to sedative weaning opportunities and examine the association between weaning adherence and clinical outcomes among pediatric ICU patients.

Design, Setting, and Participants

This multicenter cohort study included ICU patients at 4 pediatric academic medical centers admitted between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2024, with greater than 48 hours of cumulative sedative infusion exposure. Data extraction and analyses were performed between February and November 2025. Neonatal ICU stays were excluded.

Exposure

Weaning adherence, defined as the percentage of identified opportunities to wean from sedation that resulted in weaning actions (dose decreases or discontinuations), calculated at the ICU encounter level. An opportunity was defined as 0 or 1 as-needed doses of any sedative in a 12-hour period.

Main Outcomes and Measures

The primary outcomes were ICU and hospital length of stay. Secondary outcomes included infusion duration and cumulative sedative dose of each drug class. Multivariable linear regression models adjusted for center, age, and sex examined associations between weaning adherence and outcomes.

Results

The cohort included 15 747 ICU encounters from 12 737 patients (median age, 1.1 years [IQR, 0.2-6.5 years]; 56% of encounters among male patients). Median ICU length of stay was 13 days (IQR, 7-25 days) and median hospital length of stay was 26 days (IQR, 14-55 days). Among a median of 30 (IQR, 13-68) opportunities to wean per ICU encounter, overall adherence to weaning opportunities was 28% (IQR, 14%-49%), while missed weaning opportunities were common (median, 72%; IQR 51%-86%). Higher weaning adherence was associated with shorter median ICU length of stay (–2.3 days [IQR, –2.4 to –2.2 days] per 10% increase; P < .001) and shorter median total hospital length of stay (–3.6 days [IQR, –4.0 to –3.4 days] per 10% increase; P < .001). Higher weaning adherence was associated with shorter median sedative infusion duration for all sedative classes combined (–1.1 days [IQR, –1.2 to –1.0 days] per 10% increase in adherence; P < .001) and lower median cumulative exposure to opioids (–3.5 mg/kg [IQR, –3.8 to –3.2 mg/kg]; P < .001), benzodiazepines (–3.4 mg/kg [IQR, –3.8 to –3.0 mg/kg]; P < .001), and α2 agonists (–27.7 µg/kg [IQR, –30.2 to –25.3 µg/kg]; P < .001).

Conclusions and Relevance

In this multicenter cohort, adherence to sedative weaning opportunities was low. Higher weaning adherence was independently associated with shorter ICU and hospital lengths of stay and reduced sedative exposure. Improving adherence to sedative weaning opportunities may represent a modifiable target for reducing length of stay and sedative exposure.

This cohort study of more than 12 000 pediatric intensive care unit patients evaluates adherence to sedative weaning opportunities and examines the association between weaning adherence and clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Critically ill children frequently require prolonged sedation to facilitate mechanical ventilation, manage pain, and ensure patient safety during intensive care. However, prolonged sedative exposure is associated with adverse outcomes including iatrogenic withdrawal syndrome, delirium, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and increased length of stay (LOS).1,2 Furthermore, early-life sedative exposure has been increasingly implicated in long-term neurodevelopmental harm. Postnatal opioid exposure in infants undergoing congenital cardiac surgery has been associated with worse cognitive, language, and motor scores at 2 years of age.3 Similarly, midazolam exposure in preterm infants has been linked with hippocampal dysmaturation and impaired working memory.4 These observations align with experimental evidence that early administration of gamma-aminobutyric acid agonist and N-methyl-d-aspartate antagonist sedatives can lead to lasting changes in the developing brain.5 Therefore, clinical practice guidelines and protocols consistently call for the active titration of sedatives toward the minimum effective dose, an approach associated with shorter weaning duration, reduced withdrawal symptoms, and decreased hospital LOS.1,2,6,7,8,9,10,11 However, implementation of titration practices remains variable across institutions.12 Barriers include concerns about patient safety, lack of standardized protocols, and challenges in identifying appropriate weaning opportunities.1,13 Adherence to protocols may also decrease with time after implementation14 and may be hindered by staff turnover, nursing workload, and limited monitoring and feedback infrastructure.15

The minimum effective dose is conceptually clear but is dynamic and challenging to identify in practice. As-needed dose frequency and infusion patterns can serve as key indicators of sedation adequacy, and are therefore frequently identified as triggers for the upward or downward titration of sedative infusions. Despite frequent guidance to this end, it has recently been shown at a single center that adherence to both dose increase and decrease opportunities based on as-needed frequency is extremely poor; 92.5% of opportunities to increase and 95.5% of opportunities to decrease doses were missed.16

To better characterize the association of missed weaning opportunities with clinical outcomes, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study using electronic health record (EHR) data from 4 pediatric academic medical centers. We developed 2 algorithms to identify opportunities to wean, one based on as-needed use of sedation and another based on recent infusion activity. We examined the association between weaning adherence and LOS, infusion duration, and cumulative sedative exposure. We hypothesized that higher weaning adherence would be associated with shorter LOS and reduced sedative exposure.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We conducted a retrospective, multicenter cohort study using EHR data from 4 pediatric academic medical centers: Boston Children’s Hospital, Helen DeVos Children’s Hospital at Corewell Health, Monroe Carell Jr Children’s Hospital at Vanderbilt, and Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. This report follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies. The study was centrally approved by the Boston Children’s Hospital institutional review board with reliance at each participating center with waiver of informed consent because this retrospective study was considered low risk to the patients. Sedation protocols for each center, when they existed, are included in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1. We extracted 3 focused data domains from each institution’s data warehouse: (1) continuous sedative and analgesia infusion administrations, (2) intermittent bolus administrations, and (3) patient location transitions with corresponding LOS metrics. All data were stored in standardized parquet format to facilitate cross-institutional harmonization.

Cohort Selection

We included pediatric and cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) encounters with sedative medication administrations occurring between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2024, as available at each center. Encounters (or fractions thereof) in neonatal ICUs were excluded, given fundamental differences in pharmacokinetics, sedation assessment tools, drug selection, and weaning frameworks. To focus on patients with substantial sedative exposure, we retained only ICU encounters with at least 48 hours of continuous sedative infusion exposure.

Data Harmonization and Quality Control

After dose unit standardization, we implemented quality control filters to exclude physiologically implausible or clinically irrelevant records. Specifically, we excluded records where (1) infusion start times occurred after end times, (2) administration routes were nonstandard (eg, patient-controlled analgesia, intrathecal), (3) dose units were incompatible with administration (eg, milligrams per kilogram without temporal component for infusion dosing), (4) dose values were missing, and (5) for bolus administrations, only records with routes of intravenous, enteral (oral, gastric tube, and similar), transdermal, or nasal were retained.

Medication Processing

To distinguish temporary interruptions in entering information in the medical record from true medication discontinuations, we identified distinct medication periods for each (patient, encounter, drug) stream using a deterministic, per-site rule. After sorting administrations by time, a new medication period was opened whenever the gap between consecutive records exceeded a center-specific threshold, reflecting differences in documentation cadence across the 4 EHR configurations. Thresholds were 12 hours at center 1 (Cerner continuous-infusion module; requires nursing verification every 1-2 hours), 24 hours at centers 2 and 4 (Epic Medical Administration Record, captures discrete barcode scans and rate changes), and 72 hours at center 3 (Epic configured to document at the order or refill level rather than during ongoing administration). Within each medication period, the first record was classified as start; the final record (when there were ≥2 records) as discontinuation; and intermediate records as increase (dose strictly greater than the immediately preceding record), decrease (dose strictly less than the immediately preceding record), or continuation (dose unchanged from the immediately preceding record). The decision rule is mutually exclusive and exhaustive within each period and is illustrated in eFigure 2 in Supplement 1. The distribution of dose-change magnitudes among classified increase and decrease events (eTable 1 in Supplement 1) confirms that very small dose changes do not dominate the classification at any site. For bolus administrations, scheduled and as-needed doses were distinguished using the order-frequency indicators in each EHR.

Cumulative Dose Calculations

To enable cross-drug comparisons and aggregate exposure metrics, we converted all sedative doses to equivalent units within 3 drug classes: opioids (morphine equivalents), benzodiazepines (midazolam equivalents), and α2 agonists (dexmedetomidine equivalents). Conversion factors were derived from established pharmacologic equivalency tables and applied based on specific drug and administration route (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Each medication administration record contained both a weight-normalized dose (milligrams per kilogram or micrograms per kilogram) and an order-specific dosing weight, which were used to calculate the total dose for each administration.

For continuous infusions, we calculated cumulative ICU exposure as the sum of all drug- and weight-normalized administrations over time when patients were located in the ICU. For bolus administrations, we calculated 3 metrics: (1) total cumulative bolus dose across the entire ICU stay, (2) cumulative bolus dose administered during periods of active infusion (to assess rescue medication use), and (3) cumulative bolus dose administered outside of infusion periods.

Weaning Adherence Metrics

Opportunity Identification

Criteria for weaning sedative infusions varied based on each institution’s protocol during the study period; some protocols suggest a decrement of sedative infusions of 10% to 20% when 3 or fewer as-needed doses were given per shift, others suggest a decrement when no increases were made within a similar time frame, and others were more vague. In our primary analysis, we defined an opportunity to wean as any 12-hour infusion period (assessed every 4 hours) in which either 0 or 1 as-needed dose was administered. Counts of as-needed doses were aggregated across drug classes rather than tracked per agent, an intentionally conservative definition in which any breakthrough dose, regardless of class, counted toward the threshold. We also performed a secondary analysis in which we examined a pragmatic, infusion-based definition of an opportunity to wean, in which (1) no new sedative infusions were started, (2) no dose increases occurred, (3) an active infusion was ongoing for at least 24 hours, and (4) no decreases or discontinuations occurred in the preceding 24-hour period. This secondary definition was constructed to evaluate whether a simpler, infusion-only algorithm (one that does not require enumeration of as-needed administrations) could identify weaning opportunities yielding comparable inferences. Definitions of as-needed–based dosing opportunities require time-stamped documentation that is variably available across institutions and complex to deploy in real-time clinical decision support tools, whereas an infusion-only algorithm can be implemented with substantially lower data infrastructure requirements.

Weaning Action Classification

For each identified weaning opportunity, we assigned the actions as belonging to 1 of the following groups: group 1, exactly 1 decrease or discontinuation occurred within the window; group 2, multiple decreases or discontinuations occurred (potentially indicating overly aggressive weaning); group 3, weaning occurred in the absence of a clear opportunity; or group 4, no weaning action occurred despite opportunity (Figure 1). To describe weaning in the broader sedation context, we computed weaning adherence as the percentage of weaning actions that took place (groups 1-3) among all opportunities (groups 1-4). Group 3 events represent weaning actions taken outside our operational threshold, typically in response to narrower stability windows or other clinically valid signals. Including these events allows the adherence metric to reflect the full range of clinically motivated weaning behavior observed in this cohort. This metric was calculated at the ICU encounter level, providing a continuous measure of weaning practice adherence ranging from 0% (no weaning performed) to 100% (all opportunities acted upon).

Statistical Analysis

Data extraction and analyses were performed between February and November 2025. Descriptive statistics are presented as medians with IQRs for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Between-center comparisons were performed using Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate (gtsummary package in R, version 4.2.3 [R Project for Statistical Computing]).

For regression analyses, we fit separate multivariable linear regression models for each outcome, with weaning adherence as the primary variable, adjusted for center, age, and sex. Outcomes examined were: (1) ICU and total hospital LOS (primary outcome), (2) infusion duration (total days of sedative infusion for all classes combined, and separately for opioids, benzodiazepines, and α2 agonists), and (3) cumulative sedative dose. Ordinary least-squares regression was chosen because the analytical sample (n = 15 747 ICU encounters) provides asymptotic normality of coefficient estimators by the central limit theorem, and because coefficients on the original scale (days, milligrams per kilogram) are clinically interpretable. Outliers beyond the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were excluded from regression, while all data were retained for descriptive analyses. Model diagnostics were satisfactory (all variance inflation factors ≤3; no Cook distance >0.007 after trimming). Results are presented as regression coefficients with 95% CIs and P values, adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (preferred over Bonferroni given correlation among outcomes). A 2-sided adjusted P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R, version 4.2.3 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The final analytical cohort included 15 747 ICU encounters from 12 737 patients across 4 centers (Table 1). The median patient age was 1.1 years (IQR, 0.2-6.5 years), 56% of encounters involved male patients, and 44% of encounters involved female patients. Median ICU LOS was 13 days (IQR, 7-25 days) and median hospital LOS was 26 days (IQR, 14-55 days). Significant differences were observed across centers in patient demographics, encounter characteristics, ICU type, and sedation practices. Among 4.2 million rows of bolus administrations, data were excluded due to (nonexclusively) a missing dose (3%) or missing unit (0.1%). Among 4.0 million rows of infusion administrations, data were excluded due to end time prior to start time (0.1%), missing dose (0.6%), or missing unit (0.3%).

Overall median adherence to weaning opportunities was 28% (IQR, 14%-49%), with marked center-level variation (P < .001). Strict proper weaning (ie, exactly 1 weaning action if opportunity present) occurred in a minority of cases (median, 8%; IQR 0%-15%), while missed weaning opportunities were common (median, 72%; IQR, 51%-86%). The median number of weaning opportunities per ICU encounter was 30 (IQR, 13-68), which varied significantly across centers (P < .001). These findings were similar when the opportunity to wean was defined based on infusion patterns, with a predominance of missed opportunities (median, 67% [IQR, 35%-85%]) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Sedative Exposure Patterns

Sedative exposure varied substantially by center and drug class (Table 2). Opioids were used in 89% of encounters, with a median opioid cumulative dose of 18 mg/kg morphine equivalents (IQR, 8-45 mg/kg morphine equivalents). Benzodiazepines were used in 64% of encounters, with a median dose of 11 mg/kg midazolam equivalents (IQR, 4-32 mg/kg midazolam equivalents). α2 Agonists were used in 76% of encounters, with a median cumulative dose of 75 µg/kg dexmedetomidine equivalents (IQR, 27-235 µg/kg dexmedetomidine equivalents). All exposure metrics differed significantly by center (P < .001).

Sedative Context of Weaning Events

Evaluation of sedative administration patterns in the 12-hour window preceding weaning events revealed frequent exposure to recent dose escalation and as-needed sedative use (Table 3). Overall, 10% of weaning events occurred after a new sedative infusion start, and 24% followed a dose increase in the preceding 12 hours. As-needed sedative bolus exposure prior to weaning was common: 44% of weaning events occurred with no as-needed sedative administration, 12% with 1 as-needed administration, 11% with 2 as-needed administrations, and 33% with 3 or more as-needed administrations in the preceding 12 hours.

Association Between Weaning Adherence and Clinical Outcomes

In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for center, age, and sex, higher weaning adherence was associated with improved clinical outcomes (Figure 2A). Higher weaning adherence was associated with shorter median ICU LOS (–2.3 days [IQR, –2.4 to –2.2 days] per 10% increase; P < .001) and shorter median total hospital LOS (–3.6 days [IQR, –4.0 to –3.4 days] per 10% increase; P < .001), independent of center, age, and sex. The clinical association of weaning adherence was similar when opportunities to wean were defined based on infusion patterns rather than number of as-needed sedative administrations. In this secondary analysis, higher weaning adherence was associated with shorter median ICU LOS (−2.1 days [IQR, –2.2 to −2.0 days] per 10% increase; P < .001) and shorter hospital LOS (−3.2 days [IQR, –3.5 to −3.0 days] per 10% increase; P < .001), independent of center, age, and sex (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort study of 15 747 ICU encounters among 12 737 patients across 4 centers, we found that adherence to sedative weaning opportunities was low and was associated with LOS, total sedation exposure, and sedative duration. Although there were significant center differences in patient factors and sedation practice, weaning adherence was consistently low across centers. These associations persisted after adjustment for center, age, and sex, suggesting that weaning adherence represents a modifiable factor that may be associated with patient outcomes, and may provide a target for improvement efforts.

The low overall weaning adherence observed in this cohort suggests that opportunities to reduce sedative exposure are routinely missed. Prior studies have primarily described adherence in the context of new protocol implementation, when rates are likely inflated by concurrent educational efforts. Even in that setting, the RESTORE (Randomized Evaluation of Sedation Titration for Respiratory Failure) study described an 80% adherence to infusion increases but only a 71% adherence to weaning from sedatives during the weaning phase.7 The greatest unrecognized opportunity likely lies in the acute or titration phase, where many protocols recommend down-titration of sedative infusions by 10% to 20% when a threshold number of as-needed sedative administrations is not met. When sedatives are escalated early in a patient’s course but not weaned until the dedicated weaning phase, cumulative exposure builds over many days, likely contributing to the associations we observed between weaning adherence and both sedative exposure and LOS.17,18 Most cumulative dosing was from infusions rather than intermittent doses. Perhaps shifting administration more toward as-needed sedative doses could reduce total dosing.

Defining opportunities to wean was one of the more challenging aspects of this work. Most guidelines call for the active titration of sedatives to achieve a sedation target documented by an age-appropriate scoring system, such as the State Behavioral Scale (SBS) or Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS). In our data, we found that target and current scores were documented infrequently, often once per day or not at all, rendering them unsuitable as dynamic markers of an opportunity to wean. Clinicians often disagree on sedation goals, reflecting diverse beliefs about appropriate sedation.19 Instead, we defaulted to the use of bedside clinician behaviors as markers of opportunities to wean, assuming that sedation changes were reflective of clinician-perceived differences in current and target sedation level. There are clinical situations in which clinician concern for a patient, and the clinician’s willingness to wean sedation, are not congruent with recent as-needed sedation density. Anticipated procedures or a concerning trajectory may justify maintaining sedation even when as-needed sedation density is low, situations our metric would classify as missed opportunities.

Severity of illness is a critical determinant of both ICU LOS and sedative exposure, and we considered adjustment for it carefully. No unifying severity measure was suitable for this diverse cohort: Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) and Pediatric Index of Mortality (PIM) scores are calibrated for time of pediatric ICU admission, Pediatric Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (pSOFA) requires time-stamped physiological and laboratory inputs that were not consistently available across centers, and Risk Adjustment for Congenital Heart Surgery (RACHS) scores apply only to cardiac surgical patients. Furthermore, ideal adjustment would occur at the moment of each weaning opportunity rather than only at admission, and no such metric currently exists. Instead, we viewed our definition of a weaning opportunity as an implicit measure of clinical stability and tolerance of the current regimen. Less-stable patients typically require more frequent as-needed sedative doses and infusion titrations, and therefore intrinsically generate fewer weaning opportunities. This denominator captures both bedside clinician concern and perception of stability, which are difficult to operationalize in conventional severity scores.

No matter the definition, adherence to weaning opportunities is never expected to be complete, particularly when measured across institutions, units, protocols, and eras. However, these data suggest marked room for improvement. Understanding barriers to weaning is paramount and must account for the competing demands of the ICU environment, including clinician inexperience, frequent handovers, variable patient responses, and evolution of illness. Shifting the initiation infusion dose downward and favoring a greater fraction of sedative administration from as-needed doses may achieve tighter acclimation of sedation to the patient’s state. Information technology innovation may further support this effort. Future tools could continuously quantify sedative state, use real-time prediction models to augment bedside dosing decisions,20 or display opportunity to wean alerts that obviate counting of as-needed sedative doses.21 Our secondary analysis takes this a step further by demonstrating that the underlying opportunity flag need not rely on as-needed dosing data at all: an infusion-only definition yielded comparable associations with clinical outcomes, and may be more readily deployable at centers where as-needed dosing data are not consistently available in structured form.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective observational study, and we cannot establish causality. Although we adjusted for center, age, and sex, unmeasured confounding, including underlying disease and severity, prior sedative exposure, cognitive impairments, chromosomal abnormalities, and the use of neuromuscular blockade, may have influenced our results. The diversity and large size of this cohort diminish but do not eliminate the association of this limitation. Second, our algorithm identified opportunities based solely on sedative administration behavior as a surrogate for clinician-perceived suitability to wean. Clinicians may appropriately choose not to wean from sedation in situations that appear to meet our criteria, although the breadth of the population studied suggests our findings are representative. Balancing measures, including unplanned extubation and inadvertent catheter removal, were not consistently captured in structured form across the participating data warehouses, limiting our ability to characterize the safety implications of weaning behavior. Any quality improvement should capture these measures alongside weaning adherence. Sedative infusion duration and cumulative sedative exposure are computed from the same medication period algorithm used to define weaning opportunities, so they are not independent confirmations of the LOS findings; rather, they are mechanistic outcomes consistent with the LOS results, which are derived from a separate data stream. Duration of invasive mechanical ventilation was not included owing to the difficulty of adjudicating intubation and extubation timing consistently across 4 EHR systems, and its expected collinearity with LOS outcomes.22 Finally, our cohort excluded neonatal ICU encounters and patients with sedative exposure duration less than 2 days, limiting generalizability to these populations.

Conclusions

In this multicenter cohort study of pediatric ICU patients, we found that adherence to sedative weaning opportunities was consistently low. Higher weaning adherence was consistently associated with reduced sedative exposure across all drug classes and shorter LOS. These findings suggest that improving adherence to weaning opportunities may represent a modifiable target for quality improvement initiatives aimed at reducing sedative exposure and improving outcomes among critically ill children.
