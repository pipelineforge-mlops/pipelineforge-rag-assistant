Introduction

Abortion has been decriminalised in New South Wales, Australia since 2019. Post-decriminalisation, non-government organisation Family Planning Australia commenced abortion services. This study used Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) to examine the implementation of abortion services at five clinic sites.

Methods

A convergent mixed methods study was conducted with >50% of staff and 43 clients during the implementation period. Quantitative data was described using descriptive statistics. Qualitative data were analysed deductively using NPT constructs, with data within each domain examined to identify patterns, similarities and differences across participants. Data were integrated and synthesised narratively to align with NPT.

Results

Abortion care was conceptualised by staff as an important service addition. More than a third of staff surveyed increased in that belief post-implementation, some staff also reported a diminished scope of practice. 92% of clients rated their care as very good or excellent. Staff reported challenges implementing the service, which occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. Time and logistical constraints negatively impacted the implementation. Staff debriefing opportunities and client access to social workers had a positive influence.

Conclusions

Key drivers of implementation were identified as shared staff values regarding abortion, and high client satisfaction. We make recommendations for new implementation or ongoing improvement of abortion services delivered in primary care contexts.

Background

The prevention of unsafe abortion and provision of safe abortion options for pregnant people is an internationally recognised component of optimal reproductive and sexual health care. 1 While abortion in Australia has been decriminalised nationally (Western Australia was the last state to do this in 2023), accessing affordable and high-quality abortion in a timely manner is still difficult for many abortion seekers, 2 and was identified as an important area of redress in a 2023 Australian Senate enquiry. 3

There is limited availability of detailed information of the termination of pregnancy services in public health service settings in the state of New South Wales (NSW) from the perspective of both abortion seekers,4,5 and healthcare professionals.6,7 Maintaining equitable access to abortion services is essential for ensuring safe abortion and providing reproductive choice. 2 Complicating this is the pervasive attitude reported amongst some primary health care clinicians, that abortion is not routinely considered as being within the scope of primary health care services.8,9 Currently, very few public hospitals in NSW openly provide formalised abortion services, 10 pushing many abortion seekers into costly private care.

In NSW, surgical abortion is available up to 22 weeks of gestation and can only be obtained once informed consent has been provided. 11 Surgical abortions after 22 weeks can be provided in public healthcare facilities Australia-wide, there is one registered Therapeutic Goods Association option for medical abortion for gestations less than 63 days, which involves the medications Mifepristone and Misoprostol. 12 Family Planning Australia, a non-government organisation, commenced early abortion services in 2020, following the commencement of the Abortion Law Reform Act 2019 13 that decriminalised abortion in NSW. This study assessed the experiences of Family Planning Australia staff and clients during the implementation of abortion services as part of an extended primary care reproductive and sexual health service delivery across NSW. This study was carried out using the Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) framework 14 to understand the implementation of abortion services from the range of actors in the system, including staff of varying roles/seniority, and clients. NPT is ideal for understanding the implementation of healthcare services, to ensure ongoing maintenance and optimisation of such services. 15 NPT is used as a framework due to its focus on individual and collective behaviour in the implementation process. 16 The four NPT implementation constructs are used in this study; these relate to the work involved in an implementation as carried out by individual ‘actors’ within a system. The constructs are coherence (meaning and sense-making by participants), cognitive participation (staff engagement and commitment including barriers and facilitators), collective action (the work participants undertake to make the intervention function including factors that inhibit/promote use), and reflexive monitoring (how participants reflect and appraise the intervention and its impact on practice). 14 This study may inform both the implementation and ongoing improvement of primary care abortion services in an outpatient Australian context.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

All research was conducted within Family Planning Australia, with staff participating in the research providing healthcare services, and clients receiving abortion care. Family Planning Australia has five fixed clinic sites across regional and metropolitan NSW. The research employed mixed methods to examine the implementation of abortion services at Family Planning Australia. Mixed methods were used to capture meaningful insights across a complex implementation, to understand the underlying drivers of how the implementation progressed.

Frameworks and Approach

This research complied with the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guidelines 17 (see supplementary 1). Implementation of abortion began in a staggered fashion in 2020, as services were introduced gradually across sites, including the construction of a day surgery for surgical abortion. Due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on service delivery, the implementation study was extended to 24 months (the study began in January 2020, ending in January 2022).

Data collection tools developed for the overall implementation project were chosen to align to four dimensions of abortion care: availability, affordability, accessibility and quality. This was adapted from and based on the recommendation of WHO safe abortion technical and policy guidance for health systems 18 ; Benson’s conceptual framework for evaluating the outcomes of a safe abortion program 19 ; and Dawson’s framework for examining access to early abortion services. 20

The study used a convergent mixed methods design, and integrated findings via connecting and building; whereby mixed methods data are collected and analysed over a similar timeframe, and a subset of the participants are from the same sampling frame, while others are independently added and analysed. 21 The research draws on perspectives of multiple stakeholders within the implementation, including service users to understand the complexity in the context that an implementation occurs, and the way it is enacted and understood by those involved. The outcomes of the study were synthesised and integrated via narrative alignment to NPT’s implementation constructs. 15

Client Survey

A standardised recruitment script was developed and used to recruit participants during their termination of pregnancy (TOP) assessment appointment. Only clients who consented to participate in the study were sent a survey link via text message (Supplementary 2), and were therefore de-identified to researchers. The participant information sheet was shown at the beginning of the online survey. Informed consent for participation in the study was given by submitting the survey. The survey was sent 3 weeks after their abortion medication/procedure appointment, once their clinical care and follow-up was completed. The 15-item survey was hosted via SurveyMonkey and took up to 5 minutes to complete, and the questions included client’s perception of the abortion service and care they received. Professional referral services were included in the information statement in case participants wanted to debrief or discuss feelings or topics that arose during survey participation. The survey was open throughout the 2021 calendar year.

Staff Surveys

Family Planning Australia doctors, nurses, social workers and clinic reception were invited to participate in two short, anonymous surveys sent approximately 10 months post-initiation (survey 1; supplementary 3), and 12 months following survey 1 (survey 2; supplementary 4). Survey 1 was initially planned for 6-months post initiation of abortion services, but was delayed to capture more participating staff in the staggered implementation. Informed consent for participation in the study was given by submitting the surveys. Respondents were asked a mandatory question about their involvement in abortion services (with those not involved in abortion services excluded), use and rating of clinical tools and supports, challenges in abortion service provision, and views and feedback on abortion services. All other question items were not mandatory.

Staff Interviews

At the end of the second staff survey, an invitation for a 10–15-minute phone interview was included. Staff provided written, informed consent to be contacted for interview participation. Interview contact details were collected in a separate, unlinked form so interview participants could not be identified in their survey responses. Interview participants received information and consent forms, and verbal consent was obtained at the beginning of the interview.

Interviews were conducted between November 2020 and December 2021, an interview guide was provided (supplementary 5), but interviews were not restricted to those items only. Staff interview participants were all involved in abortion care and discussed their views and experiences with the medical and surgical abortion services. All interviews were conducted on Zoom and audio-recorded with participants’ permission for later transcription.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise findings of the client surveys, and where relevant, is aggregated by type of termination (medical vs surgical). To compare decision-making by abortion type, a Pearson’s Chi-square test was used, with an alpha level of 0.05 for statistical significance.

For staff surveys, descriptive statistics were used to summarise survey findings using counts and percentages. Responses were compared between the two time-points for question items that matched. Due to the anonymous nature of the surveys, the data could not be paired, but due to the possibility that staff could participate in both, the samples are dependent. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio software (version 4.3.1). For non-mandatory survey questions, missing data was excluded from analyses. Denominators are reported where relevant and reflect the number of respondents that provided a response to a given question item.

Qualitative Data Analysis

De-identified transcripts and survey comments from staff and client surveys were imported into NVivo and coded deductively using the core constructs of NPT; coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring. 22 A coding framework based on these domains was developed a priori and applied systematically across all data. Coding and analysis were conducted by a single researcher (GR). Coding decisions were reviewed iteratively to ensure consistency in the application of the framework. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the analysis process through ongoing reflection on the researcher’s positionality, including their professional background in implementation research and potential influence on data interpretation. Following coding, data within each domain were examined to identify patterns, similarities and differences across participants, which informed the interpretation of the findings.

Results

A total of 45 staff participated (55% response rate). Survey participants included healthcare professionals and administrative staff. Ten participants recruited from S2 participated in follow-up interviews to gain further insights. Coherence

There were overwhelming positive and affirming attitudes by staff in the implementation of abortion services at Family Planning Australia’s clinics. In survey open text fields, many staff chose to talk about the importance of abortion as a sexual health service, and the satisfaction with being able to provide (and contribute to the normalisation) of this healthcare. “Being able to support someone feel ok about making a really difficult decision makes our job feel worthwhile to me. We are providing services to clients who otherwise would not be able to afford or access abortion. We are normalising abortion as part of people’s healthcare needs and rights.”

Staff in S2 were asked whether their impressions of abortion provision had changed since implementation, 38% (11/29) agreed that it had, while 62% (18/29) said it remained the same (remainder did not respond). This high level of internalisation of the value of the implementation was a supportive factor. Comments from 13 participants provided insight into their conceptualisation of providing abortion care services. Among those who remained unchanged in their beliefs, there was already a strongly held belief in abortion service provision in the community. “I have always felt it was important to provide access to affordable, safe abortion services”

Staff whose impressions of abortion service provision had changed were divided into two primary views, that they now felt abortion was even more essential, or that they now believe it has come at the expense of other sexual health service delivery in their work. Those that felt a deeper resonance for abortion provision post-implementation usually described encounters with clients for affirming that change. With staff remarking: “I feel that abortion provision is even more essential. The complexity of client lives and the issues affecting those with unplanned pregnancy are vast.”

“Seeing more women access the service I can see there is a need in the general community for this”

Those who believed abortion services came at the expense of other sexual health services discussed these beliefs in terms of the reputation of the organisation, or themselves as professionals. The implementation of abortion care services was perceived to overwhelm the organisation’s capacity for the range of other health service offerings done in the past, with staff making comments such as: “Yes I know it is an important service but it does feel that Family Planning Australia has become mainly a TOP [termination of pregnancy] and IUD [intra-uterine device] service, different to what it has been known for”

“It is an important part of our service, but unfortunately it now dominates our work at the expense of our overall ability and reputation to provide diverse expert sexual and reproductive health services”

Across the surveys, there were 10 similar statements made by staff. One staff member reflected on their awareness of the concept of diminished scope among their colleagues, which provides an insight into service implementation: “I hear it from [staff] that practicing to the breadth of their scope has diminished. I suspect some of this is perception rather than being truly accurate… I think addressing this issue is… about addressing the dialogue and the negative connotation that the introduction of a new service has been limiting - (as opposed to seeing that it's terrific that another service is being offered to clients in need)”

Even when beliefs conflicted with actions, staff conveyed their underlying support of abortion access, also reflecting the NPT construct of cognitive participation, as it relates to their implementation work.

Cognitive Participation

Users of an abortion service must decide on how they engage with it, thereby signifying their cognitive participation in the service. The survey of clients highlights some decision-making factors that abortion service users had regarding type of termination, and cost. Choice between medical and surgical abortion (Figure 1) was reportedly based on personal preferences (46.15%), convenience (33.33%), recommended by the doctor for medical reasons (15.38%), this was the only option the client had because of the timing (12.82%), location (5.13%), followed by cost (7.69%) and others (7.69%). A greater number of surgical termination of pregnancy (STOP) clients than medical termination of pregnancy (MTOP) clients reported personal preference, and conversely more MTOP clients reporting convenience than STOP clients (Figure 1). However, the difference in proportion by type of termination showed no significant differences (X2=9.17; p= .16). It should be noted that due to the low sample size, the data is underpowered and unable to confidently detect a difference between MTOP and STOP (beta level of 0.7 when alpha is set to 0.05).Figure 1. The beliefs of clinical staff (doctors and nurses) were surveyed regarding the optimal time during abortion care for providing contraceptive advice. Most that responded, selected all the available options (assessment, medication/procedure appointment and follow-up). There were no pronounced differences between S1 and S2 (Figure 2). Notably, in both surveys there was an option “none of the above” that was unused, indicating that staff agree that contraception is an important inclusion in abortion care. This promoted service implementation through cognitive participation in decision-making about the timing of contraceptive counselling, as individuals continue to support this component of the service but enact it deliberately and autonomously.Figure 2. In interviews, many clinic staff noted that educating clients on contraception during their TOP assessment is challenged by the highly stressful situation some clients find themselves in, and the limited time that is available during the appointment. Consequently, contraception consultations are often delayed until post-abortion follow-up, when some clients are uncontactable or are at risk of another pregnancy. The delay in initiating these conversations due to extenuating circumstances lead to inconsistencies in the implementation. “I think the other thing is to expect to do a medical termination and also then talk about contraception afterwards, in all of that appointment, I think that’s quite a big ask, to be honest.”

“When you’re talking about medical termination – at the time when we provide it, they’re still thinking about the pills and when they have to take the pills and what’s going to happen. And so, often, we delay that discussion, and we just breeze across it and delay it until the follow-up appointment. But sometimes… people have already been sexually active, and [had] unprotected sex between… the provision and the follow-up. And so they’re, again, at risk of pregnancy.”

“The other problem has been loss to follow up… sometimes we just lose them.”

Finally, most participants mentioned that having a multi-disciplinary, like-minded, and passionate team at the healthcare organisation was crucial for successful implementation and engagement in abortion services. Clinicians relied on one another to be able to learn and adapt while abortion services were gradually integrated and expanded across clinics. Many also stated that they were thankful for having a social work team to support difficult cases such as those facing domestic violence or who were living with financial hardships. “The main thing has been that we’ve got a very like-minded team. And I work with are all passionate about providing the best care for our clients.”

“Having a social worker available is really, really – helps as well.”

“So I think there’s a strong teamwork ethic at our clinic. So we’re all trying to improve our practice, and when things have gone wrong, something has been missing, we’ve all been able to have a talk about how we can do better. Just to support each other.”

This collegiality supports both staff engagement in the implementation process and enactment of the work, consistent with the collective action construct of NPT.

Collective Action

Positively, for administration and reception staff, confidence handling and booking enquiries related to abortion was high (rated >8 on a scale of 1 to 10). The average confidence rating showed a negligible change between S1 and S2 for both handling (8.57 in S1 vs 8.5 in S2) and booking (8.86 vs 8.75). Some of the challenges identified in interviews centered on the emotional toll of managing a busy service that requires timely care and often distressed clients. “Assessment and provision appointments are scarce as service requirements seem to have increased in demand, especially since [COVID-19].”

“It’s hard when they start to cry on the phone and they are very confused. You offer to put them through to Talkline [nurse phone service] and you need to calm them down before we can transfer the call. I feel at most times we are Counsellors/Receptionist when we take these calls. It can be very overwhelming sometimes.”

Unlike reception staff, clinicians involved in abortion care initially felt less confident in their skills and sought advice from other more experienced colleagues. This was particularly the case when dealing with more complex cases such as clients with mental health concerns or those who experienced post-abortion complications. The lack of confidence reported by some staff may have caused areas of individual resistance to the implementation, which was mitigated by some through referrals made to senior staff or the social work service.“In the beginning, I was totally not confident… but I feel fairly confident now, in terms of what’s required. I guess the issues that I feel, still, that I need to develop are complications after terminations of pregnancy and how to deal with them”.

“We do get the occasional person who’s suffering quite significant grief. And sometimes I don’t know what to say and I don’t want to say something that makes it worse.”

“And we don’t have a lot of background in the counselling side of [abortion care] - so we can refer to social work.”

‘Time’ was a frequently mentioned word across all open text responses in surveys, whether it related to explaining variability, appointment length, follow-ups, or pressure. However, as the implementation progressed, the prevalence of time constraints as the theme of open text responses reduced from 40% (66/165 open text responses) in S1 to 28% (32/114 responses) in S2. In the interviews, many participants discussed how limited appointment availability and limited time during assessment appointments caused further stress to clinic staff and those seeking abortion care. “Assessment often takes an hour [15 minutes overtime] and if woman needs LARC or sedation assessment too, this only adds to the time needed”

“I think, the biggest challenge that we’re facing, particularly at the moment is lack of appointment availability. So, we’ll get a phone call through reception or through to Talkline [phone advice service] from someone who’s already seven to eight weeks. And it’s very rare that we can fit them in because we just don’t have availability.”

The COVID-19 pandemic during the implementation of abortion services also caused challenges, as telehealth services, e-referrals and other processes needed to be promptly implemented in addition to abortion care services. COVID-related restrictions in movements and vaccination mandates also meant that clients had limited access to services for pharmacy, ultrasound and pathology services. “It limits people’s capacity to move and go places and access services as readily as they would have before. It’s impacted radiology places to be able to provide ultrasounds. People have to arrive earlier, there’s restrictions on vaccination status and things like that.”

“We had a… woman who had no transport who was in a hotspot. She had her assessment done via telehealth. She had provision done by telehealth, and then there was this awful thing about well, how are we going to get her to pick up her supply?”

“The whole COVID – having to change to telehealth, being able to get clients access to prescriptions, being able to get clients access to ultrasound referrals or pathology referrals. That’s been really, really challenging.”

The use of telehealth was also explored in the client survey, telehealth was reported by 55% MTOP clients – mostly in follow up calls, while 40% of STOP clients had their pre-abortion assessment via telehealth (for self-reported use of telehealth by appointment type, see supplementary 6). COVID and telehealth was mentioned as a facilitator of abortion care: “Generally I had a good experience. It was a positive experience. It was convenient not to have to travel, especially during the COVID restrictions. I do not feel the effectiveness was compromised by having phone consults.”

Reflexive Monitoring

The staff interviews and S2 survey were conducted almost two years following the implementation of abortion services. Staff had experience of working during the implementation period and were asked to reflect on their experiences. Clients also reflected on aspects of their care, three weeks after receiving care, with a majority of participants (33/36) rating the quality of care they received at the clinics as either very good or excellent. The 3 remaining clients that answered this question rated their care as satisfactory.

Clients could rate the level of received information on a scale of not enough (1) to too much (5) for a variety of topics. A breakdown of this information by proportion is shown in Figure 3. Most reported the information they received about their abortion care was the right amount. Clients more often said the information was a lot or too much compared to a little/not enough.Figure 3. In their feedback, clients commented that the information on side effects, potential complications and expectation in medical terminations lead to them anticipating a worse experience than what eventuated. “From the information given was expecting a worse experience. Had a good experience with MTOP. This amount of information was helpful to know what to expect.”

“Too much information around pain I may experience. This caused some anxiety. I had less pain than what I had prepared for.”

Through the interviews and staff surveys, staff took the opportunity to reflect on the wellbeing of staff and clients throughout the implementation, and how it could be improved. Many participants expressed the desire for regular debrief meetings among colleagues to enable clinic staff to discuss more complicated or stressful cases to be able to process it and find solutions in a collaborative process. Those who had these discussions consistently said it was valuable, and those that did not wished that they had the opportunity. In both interviews and survey, staff participants identified a need to expand the social work service as well as offering some basic counselling training to staff providing abortion services to better support women in complex or difficult situations. “I would like to see some additional training in – not so much counselling but just the basics… often we’ll get women who have come in for an assessment and they’re quite distressed. But just even some of those basic skills from a nurse’s perspective, so that we can create a safe environment.”

“I suppose developing the social work service as well. I think there is a big workload and there is a capacity for that to expand… we do get a lot of really complex clients.”

“Having some sort of debrief capability for difficult or distressing cases. We do hear a lot of sad and traumatic stories in the scenario of TOP, and there isn’t really scheduled time for us to debrief together about that, so yeah, that might be really helpful as well.”

Additionally, many staff noted in their interviews and survey comments, a need to further streamline abortion services. The need for several appointments before an abortion was reportedly a substantial barrier to accessing services, especially for those in rural or remote areas. Improving appointment availability was seen as another priority, as well as introducing on-site prerequisites such as an ultrasound and a blood test (unavailable on-site at the time of implementation) to ensure a more efficient continuum of care. “We do find a lot of women are very distressed when we say you need to have an ultrasound, we need to have blood. So, that would be the first thing I’d say, is to remove some of those barriers if it can be done safely.”

“I know a lot of women will say - well, I just want to get it done, I just want to get it over and done with… And then we go, oh, well, now you need to wait 10 days for an assessment and then once we’ve assessed you – because you’ve missed the cut off for paperwork being in by Friday, now you’ve got to wait another week. I’m just wondering if there could be a more streamlined way to do that, a more patient-centred care rather than an organisational-centred care.”

Throughout the client and staff reflection, there was strong communal appraisal for the implementation continuing, but there was additional reconfiguration around expectations and wellbeing that were listed as areas for continuous improvement.

Discussion

This study presented an examination of abortion services implementation in a primary care sexual health organisation. Through the use of NPT as a framework, we gained an understanding of the mechanisms of an implementation by the work of those involved, summarised in Figure 4. A majority of staff and clients valued the introduction of abortion services and were satisfied with the outcomes. However, staff felt overwhelmed at times, regarding the practical completion of their duties, the perception of remaining barriers for clients, and the emotional side of providing abortion care.Figure 4. Summary of NPT alignment and data integration. Normalisation Process Theory constructs are presented as wedges of a circle. Key findings from the study are connected to the construct wedges they are aligned with. The mixed methods data sources are identified alongside each point to demonstrate how these were integrated to benefit the findings

Our findings that staff support for abortion is consistent with previous research among healthcare providers in sexual health settings.23-25 Despite support for abortion in principle, some staff perceived abortion services to exceed the scope of practice or dilute the range of sexual and reproductive services offered at the organisation. It has been reported internationally that the roles and responsibilities of staff can be negatively impacted by large organisational change, even when the implementation methods have been sound, 26 potentially playing a role in this study as COVID-19 restrictions were occurring during the implementation. Another possible interpretation of these remarks comes from research on scope of practice beliefs of individuals and healthcare as a profession. 27 The Solar model of scope of practice describes one’s personal and professional scope as influenced by four factors; regulation and policy, practice environment, education and training, and professional relationships and boundaries. 27 The barriers and inhibitors raised by staff in this study related to a change in the practice environment from the addition of a new service, which could have moved an individual’s sense of their individual or professional scope. It is therefore recommended that in the planning of new service implementation, that ways to understand perspectives of scope of practice are considered and addressed to provide support to staff.

A striking outcome of the implementation was the emotional impact on staff regardless of their role. The implementation elicited frustration and stress in relation to operational barriers, as well as empathy or distress in relation to client care or circumstances. Staff working in specialised abortion and reproductive healthcare in Australia have previously reported the emotional load they experience from their patient experiences, the difficulties in the healthcare system and the experience of stigma.28,29 An important countermeasure reported by some staff in this study was the ability to debrief and discuss challenging cases among colleagues. Debriefing has been shown to support staff in dealing with traumatic events, 30 and in training or professional development situations31,32 which have some alignment with the study setting. For implementation of new services, and in ongoing abortion provision services, we recommend that appropriate debriefing opportunities are provided, such as that outlined by the Association for Medical Education in Europe. 33 It is also recommended that clinicians have access to basic counselling training to support them when dealing with clients experiencing psychological distress. Further studies could investigate the usefulness of such recommendations in practice.

This study was conducted when COVID-19 restrictions affected parts of the state (and therefore study sites) in varying ways. COVID-19 related restrictions may have impacted study participants’ views and experiences, even beyond what was directly reported by them. Other research on Australian abortion access during COVID-19 identified that the restrictions exacerbated pre-existing barriers for abortion seekers, such as choice of abortion type, language, reproductive coercion, cost, and location. 34 This is further evidenced by data from our organisation’s advice hotline, that demonstrated a significant increase in abortion-related enquiries during the COVID-19 pandemic, financial difficulties, and diverse callers such as those with disability and those from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds. 35 The relationship between healthcare staff and COVID-19 restrictions has been demonstrated within Australia, with significant shifts in service delivery logistics affecting the type and quantity of services provided, 36 and relationships between clients and colleagues became stressors when tension and uncertainty increased. 37 Ultimately, the coherence of staff supporting abortion access was an important driver of the implementation. Abortion provision continued in the study setting across NSW despite the tumult of a post-decriminalisation landscape and COVID-19 restrictions.

A strength of this study was the use of multiple sources of data to gain insight into abortion implementation from staff and clients, which is advantageous in addressing the complexity of the factors affected by healthcare implementations 38 (Figure 4). This study assessed training, service delivery, personal beliefs and expectations and identified factors that may promote and inhibit the implementation of abortion services. Reflexive monitoring from staff, and feedback from clients on these elements adds to the ongoing quality improvement of abortion services within the organisation.

There is evidence that expectations of abortion care are low in abortion seekers,39,40 and therefore abortion seekers may highly rate their satisfaction due to anticipated poor treatment. 41 By not interviewing clients on their experiences, we acknowledge a limitation in this study in exploring client-centred indicators of quality and satisfying abortion care. Another limitation of the study is the duration of the study period, evaluating the implementation for two years, through COVID-19 uncertainties. Promisingly, medical and surgical abortion services have continued in the study setting, but an ongoing monitoring of NPT constructs beyond the study period until it was completely ‘normalised’ may have provided nuanced insights into factors affecting the longevity of implementation in primary care contexts. Nevertheless, the findings from this study are valuable for interpreting how the work of different multidisciplinary team members in the system is instrumental in the effective operationalisation of abortion service implementation. The use of NPT for synthesising the results was challenging due to the dynamic relationship between constructs, which has been observed in NPT studies (reviewed elsewhere 42 ). There was overall benefit of using NPT for this study, there was a limitation in presenting evidence as belonging to one construct over another.

Conclusion

The findings from this study may be utilised as learnings by those seeking to implement new services in Australian primary care contexts using NPT as a framework for collecting and interpreting data. Evidence from this study can provide an understanding of how abortion services in NSW may be implemented to increase access to these vital services, while promoting positive staff and client outcomes. In abortion service delivery, we recommend promotion of reproductive rights-based values among staff, structured opportunities for debrief among colleagues, and acknowledgement of the distinction between individual and professional scope of practice to enhance staff satisfaction.
