Cloud-based electronic health records (EHRs) are vulnerable to modification during transmission and use, compromising security and privacy. To reduce this risk, we implemented a semi-fragile watermarking method using Slant Transform and chaotic sequences. This method creates a clinically sensitive tamper dataset. Transfer learning classifies images as original or tampered. Further, a black-box semi-fragile neural network watermarking is proposed, which can determine the model availability by distinguishing accidental modifications and malicious tampering using the generated fingerprints with the pretrained models such as ResNet, EfficientNet, and Vision Transformer architectures. If there is a tampering on the image, the semi-fragile watermarking supports tamper localization and self-recovery. Our approach resists clinically sensitive attacks and can self-recover tampered images, achieving high perceptual quality of ( PSNR of 44.91 dB and SSIM of 0.962), tamper detection (94.9%), and tamper localization (97.9%). The trained networks are watermarked to verify model integrity, and the model detects tampered images with 96.8% accuracy.

Introduction

A recent revolution in science and technology is the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) with cloud-based applications1,2. This technology has made significant contributions to agriculture, including soil nutrient analysis and estimation3, crop disease prediction, and biomass prediction4. In medicine, AI-based tools help physicians with diagnostics and treatment planning5. The applications in AI based deep learning models have a lot of commercial potential. The introduction of 4G connectivity6 has allowed widespread use of data-driven services such as multimedia streaming, digital content exchange on social platforms, and online healthcare and consultation7. This trend accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic8. Hospitals now offer online consultations and share patient health reports9 and medical images10 through the cloud11. However, this rapid growth poses a major challenge to the security and privacy of patient medical data. Unauthorized modification of medical images can lead to incorrect diagnoses12, endangering the lives of patients, and to false medical claims in the insurance sector13. Therefore, this paper focuses on safeguarding the security14 of medical images.

Recent advances in watermarking15 and encryption have drawn significant attention in the Cyber security community. Encryption16,17 is widely used to protect confidential information during data transmission, but it can signal to hackers that communication is happening. In contrast, watermarking18 conceals information by embedding digital content, such as text, images, video, or audio, within multimedia files, making hidden data less detectable. The literature identifies three types of watermarking scheme: fragile, semi-fragile, and robust19. Robust digital watermarking20,21 embeds external watermarks, such as hospital logos, into cover images using spatial or transform-domain techniques. This approach resists both intentional and unintentional attacks and is primarily used for ownership verification and copyright protection. In the Fragile Watermarking scheme22, the watermark is removed by even minor changes to the watermarked image, making it suitable for image forensics and verification of medical image integrity. Semi-fragile watermarking23 offers a balance between robust and fragile methods. When an image is subjected to clinically relevant attacks, the semi-fragile method can detect tampered areas and attempt to recover them. It tolerates attacks such as compression and intensity adjustments, making it well-suited for secure transmission24 of medical images in the cloud. The general approach of a semi-fragile watermarking process is given in Fig 1. In this process if an image is attacked during the transmission, then at the receiving end, the image needs to be cross checked for probable tampering. If the algorithm detects that the image is tampered with, then it goes to the further process of tamper localization and self-recovery.Fig. 1 An overview of a semi-fragile watermarking.

In a medical distributed system, a large number of images are communicated over the covert communication channels. It may be a computationally difficult task to check the integrity of each and every medical image for potential tampering. Hence, at this point if one can use the deep neural network (DNN)33 approach, it will be an added advantage to the existing semi-fragile watermarking. Jagadeesh et al.34 proposed an approach where a set of medical images are watermarked then 5 different attacks applied on each watermarked image hence a dataset was formed with 6 different classes. This dataset is trained (transfer-learned) on a an existing GoogleNet and AlexNet. Whenever an image is requested by the end-user, initially is passes through transfer-learned network for potential tamper-detection, if network classifies the requested image as tampered with, then the tampered localization and self-recovery will be done based to repair the tampered portions. Hence this methodology is available to deal with the huge numbers of images uploaded to the cloud with help of semi-fragile watermarking and deep transfer learning for tamper detection, localization and self-recovery respectively in a pipeline. Now the interesting part is what happens if the transfer learned network tampered with ? . In the literature various techniques proposed to watermark a DNN Yuan et al.35 and introduced technical jargon such as black box, white box, fragile and semi-fragile. This paper cleverly combines the semi-fragile watermarking at image level and at a DNN level to securely transmit a medical image from end-to-end in a smart healthcare environment. The main contribution of the proposed work is:We employed our existing semi-fragile watermarking scheme26 for watermarking different image modalities and the watermarked images of these are attacked with 5 attacks and later preprocessed and augmented to create a database34 and for classification of six labels (One is untampered and the rest five are tampered attacks) we employed pre-trained models such as RESNET, EfficientNet and Vision transformer(ViT).

A black-box semi-fragile neural network watermarking is employed that determines the model availability that is able to identify non-intentional attacks and malicious tampering using the generated RESNET, EfficientNet and Vision transformer model fingerprints.

A dual watermarking approach at image level and network (DNN) level for medical image content authentication as well as verified usage of the DNN.

The performance of the proposed tamper detection, localization and self-recovery is analyzed in terms of FPR and FRR.

The statistical analysis of the proposed workflow is analyzed with ANOVA.

Related works

This section is planned to explore the recent works on semi-fragile watermarking at image level in “Image level semi-fragile watermarking schemes” section and the works on Deep learning is covered in “Deep learning based watermarking schemes” section.

Image level semi-fragile watermarking schemes

Amrullah et al.25 proposed a two-level tamper detection method to obtain a high tampered localization accuracy. They employed IDWT transform and the authentication bits produced from LL band are concealed in the LH and HL bands. The first phase will analyze four segments to find the tampered bit or not. The second phase is carried only if the first phase has no tampered bit. The second phase checked each segment to determine whether it was tampered with or not. The authors employed Normalized Hamming Similarity (NHS) to classify malicious and incidental attacks. At the receiving end, tamper detection is achieved by secret key matching and the tampered areas are recovered through the scrambled recovery data. Pascal et al.28 intelligently used the error location codes proposed in Wolf & Elspas36 for computationally efficient tamper detection and localization method. Jagadeesh et al.26 proposed a method where initially the cover image is divided into 2 × 2 non-overlapping block, from each block an 8-bit watermark is generated using Slant Transform (ST) and DNA logistics, embedding will be based on a block mapping governed by a chaotic sequence. The tamper detection and self-recovery is shown under clinically sensitive attack types. Maazen et al.27 proposed a watermark generation with HL and LH bands of Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) of a cover image with control parameters α and β, the embedding is based on the chaotic substitution box with an edge awareness based on Laplacian operator. The comparative analysis of these start-of-art methodologies are given in Table 1. Rani et al.15 presented a review on recent works on watermarking. A composite watermarking approach was proposed by Lakhani et al.37.

Deep learning based watermarking schemes

From the literature it is found that, the DNN watermarking can be classified into white-box and black-box approaches. In white-box approach, the model internals are accessed for the integrity verification, whereas in black-box approach the model integrity is verified with a pre-defined input-output relation, here the specific input is called as trigger. In this section a review on recent works on black-box DNN is given. Liu et al.38 proposed the flaws in black-box watermarking schemes where the trigger samples by adding a random pattern to the clean samples which may not some times produced accurate results for the watermarking. Therefore, the authors applied Fourier perturbation impact of different frequency components of the input sample on the task functionality of the DNN by applying random perturbation. Then, by K-means clustering, they improved the watermarking parameters such as imperceptibility and robustness. Mouke et at.32 proposed a black box watermarking which can uniquely identify a user in a multi-user scenario also prevent the likelihood of poisoned image counterfeiting. The white-box approach of the DNN watermarking is out of scope of this paper but the interested readers may refer the work proposed by Minoru Kuribayashi et al.In this paper the authors proposed an image level semi-fragile watermarking inspired from the previous works of Jagadeesh et al.26 and model level black-box DNN watermarking, where the DNN is trained with a transfer learning approach for the tampered type classification in a cloud based smart healthcare system39. Ngo et al.29 proposed a three stage DNN watermarking approach which address embedding, persistence evaluation and restoration of the watermark. Proposed methodology

This section describes the proposed intelligent tamper-management framework that ensures medical image integrity and model authenticity. The architecture integrates three modules, namely, one is Semi-fragile watermarking for image integrity verification and recovery. The second one is deep learning–based tamper classification using transfer learning, and the third is Black-box DNN watermarking for model fingerprinting and ownership verification. The overall methodology is shown in Fig. 2, where semi-fragile watermarking ensures image-level security, transfer learning enables intelligent tamper detection, and DNN watermarking preserves model integrity against adversarial modification or unauthorized reuse.

Tampered (attacked) dataset creation

A specialized dataset40 of tampered images was developed using watermarked medical images to replicate real-world tampering situations and support tamper classification. We identified five prevalent types of clinically sensitive attacks: content removal, copy-move, copy-paste, multi-region, and text addition tampering. Using a MATLAB-based batch processing script, five tampered versions were created for each of the 2500 watermarked images. In the content removal method, a section of the image was replaced with a neutral gray patch. The copy-move attack involved duplicating a segment of the image and pasting it elsewhere within the same image. The copy-paste attack meant inserting a patch from another image. The text addition attack included adding artificial labels or annotations to the image. Multi-region tampering incorporated two or more of these manipulations. Verifying neural network integrity using semi-fragile adversarial watermarking

To enhance security beyond just detecting image-level tampering, we integrate a new semi-fragile watermarking mechanism directly into the trained deep learning model. Building upon the approach described in Yuan et al.35, we employ adversarial examples as fingerprints to confirm the integrity of the neural network employed for classifying tamper types. This method functions like a black box and does not need access to internal model weights, making it ideal for deployment in real-world scenarios41. Specifically, it applies small perturbations to a collection of benign inputs using the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), creating a set of adversarial examples (fingerprints). The original model accurately classifies these adversarial inputs yet remains sensitive to any changes in its structure (for instance, due to pruning, tampering, or the introduction of Trojans). The expected class labels for these fingerprints are safely stored as a watermark signature. Nonetheless, this approach faces challenges regarding fingerprint diversity, interpretability, and dynamic adaptability.

Fingerprint generation

Tamper detection using verified CNN

In the cloud-based Smart Health Care system (SHS) environment, the watermarked CNN model is used for distributed applications. Before accessing the model, users must verify its integrity via fingerprint verification. Once the model is confirmed to be intact, users can request a watermarked medical image from the cloud, which stores both the dataset and the model itself. The requested image is then processed by the model. If the image is identified as original and untampered, it is sent directly to the user. If tampering is detected, the image undergoes further processing to localize tampering and enable self-recovery, after which the restored image is provided to the user. A set of medical images with different modalities, such as MRI, X-RAY, Ultrasonic, PET, and Skin melanoma, are considered for experimentation. Tamper localization and self-recovery ability under copy-paste attack

When pixels are copied from a visually similar image and pasted onto another, this is referred to as a copy–paste attack, also known as a collage attack (Type ii) in the literature. Such manipulations can mislead medical diagnostics by either concealing or introducing clinically significant regions.

To evaluate the reliability of the proposed fingerprint verification mechanism, both genuine and tampered model variants were generated. Genuine models included the original fingerprinted network and benign modifications such as fine-tuning, pruning, and quantization, whereas tampered models included noise injection, scaling, combined attacks, backdoor insertion, and data poisoning. Each model was subjected to fingerprint verification and classified using a threshold-based decision rule. After carefully viewing the fingerprint accuracy obtained from benign and malicious tampering results summarized in Fig. 26, a decision threshold of τ = 0.63 with a decision margin of 0.44 is considered. The resulting TP, TN, FP, and FN outcomes were subsequently used to compute the FAR and FRR, thereby quantifying the probability of accepting tampered models and rejecting genuine models, respectively. Conclusion and future scope

This work introduces a unified semi-fragile watermarking and adversarial fingerprinting framework for medical imaging systems. This work combines transform-domain watermark embedding (using Slant transform), chaotic sequence mapping for watermarking, and tamper classification using deep learning models. Quantitative results show the method delivers excellent image quality (PSNR = 44.8 dB, SSIM = 0.93), precise tamper localization (99.9 %), and robust self-recovery. At the model level, the semi-fragile adversarial DNN watermark verifies authenticity with 96.8 % accuracy, remaining robust against benign modifications but resilient to adversarial fine-tuning and poisoning. Compared to recent semi-fragile and neural watermarking methods, our work offers notable advantages in scalability, adaptability, and verification reliability, effectively bridging the gap between image and model integrity protection. This dual-layer approach improves trustworthiness in medical images and provides a flexible blueprint for secure AI integration in healthcare. Future work will explore quantization index modulation and adaptive watermarking, AI-driven integrity monitoring, and real-time edge deployment to enable autonomous, self-healing trust in medical and biomedical imaging systems. For the proposed framework, a structural limitation arises from the sequential dependency between the DNN model and the semi-fragile watermarking-based tamper localization and self-recovery process. Sometimes the DNN may misclassify a tampered image as genuine. This false-negative would bypass the tamper localization and self-recovery pipeline. But the probability of this false-negative is very low. To address this limitation. Future work may integrate blockchain or a blind zero-watermark pre-check, thereby eliminating the single-point dependency on the DNN classifier.
