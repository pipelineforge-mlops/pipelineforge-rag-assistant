Background

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic syndrome characterised by widespread musculoskeletal pain, accompanied by fatigue, functional alterations, and changes in body composition.

Objective

The main objective was to analyse muscle architecture variables using ultrasound in patients with FM.

Design

A cross-sectional observational study conducted in accordance with the STROBE guideline.

Methods

80 patients with FM (54.06 ± 9.66 years) were assessed. Sociodemographic and anthropometric variables, functional capacity (5-sit-to-stand-test (STST), Handgrip (HGS), Time Up Go (TUG) and 4- metres-walk-test (4MWT) and muscle architecture variables (cross-sectional-area of vastus lateralis (VL-CSA) and cross-sectional-area of rectus femoralis (RF-CSA), thickness of vastus lateralis and pennation angle of vastus lateralis (AP-VL)) were collected. Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed, and Pearson or Spearman correlations were applied as appropriate. Multiple linear regression was performed to evaluate the association between VL-CSA and clinical, functional, and anthropometric variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants reported high pain intensity (VAS: 7.03 ± 1.94). Mean VL-CSA was 11.73 ± 3.76 cm2. Ultrasound-derived muscle parameters showed significant positive associations with lean mass and anthropometric variables, with a stronger association between VL-CSA and trochanteric circumference than with body mass index (BMI). The regression model explained 46.6% of VL-CSA variance (p < 0.001), with BMI, trochanteric circumference, 5-STST, performance and handgrip strength emerged as independent predictors (p < 0.05), while gait speed was not significant.

Conclusions

Correlations were found between anthropometric variables and muscle architecture. VL-CSA was significantly and independently related to BMI, 4-meter test, 5 sit-stand test, handgrip strength, and trochanter circumference.

Plain language summary

Fibromyalgia is a long-term condition that causes widespread pain, fatigue, and difficulties in daily activities. Many women with fibromyalgia also experience reduced muscle strength and physical function, but the role of muscle structure in these limitations is not well understood. This study included 80 women with fibromyalgia. We assessed body measurements, physical function using simple strength and mobility tests, and thigh muscle size using ultrasound, a safe and painless imaging technique. The results showed that participants reported high levels of pain and had reduced physical performance. Women with larger thigh muscles tended to have more muscle mass and better results in strength and mobility tests. In addition, body weight, hip-related measurements, and physical performance were important factors associated with muscle size. These findings suggest that muscle structure is closely related to physical function in women with fibromyalgia and that ultrasound may be a useful tool to better understand functional limitations in this population.

Introduction

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic syndrome characterised by widespread musculoskeletal pain that is usually accompanied by fatigue, functional alterations, sleep disorders and changes in body composition. 1

FM is a major cause of chronic musculoskeletal pain, affecting approximately 2-4% of the general population, with a marked predominance in women. 2 It is unclear aetiology complicates both diagnosis and treatment. 3 The substantial impact of FM on daily functioning underscores its relevance as a public health concern. 4 Current management includes non-pharmacological approaches such as exercise, physiotherapy, and cognitive–behavioural therapy, often combined with pharmacological treatment. 5

Some of the symptoms of the condition, such as fatigue and muscle pain, suggest skeletal muscle involvement. Several studies have evaluated morphological and metabolic changes in skeletal muscle through biopsy, as well as peripheral mechanisms that may play an important role in chronic pain.6,7 This chronic pain leads patients with FM to significantly reduce their daily physical activity, which has direct consequences on body composition and functional capacity. 8

Assessing body composition and functional capacity is essential for understanding the progression of FM, but few studies have evaluated morphological changes in skeletal muscle using ultrasound. However, ultrasound is undoubtedly a suitable tool that allows us to detect functional and structural changes in skeletal muscle.9,10 These functional and morphological changes caused by physical inactivity led to a loss of muscle mass and strength, as well as higher pain. A higher level of body fat is known to be negatively correlated with the severity of FM. 11

Ultrasound-derived variables quadriceps thickness and cross-sectional area (CSA) are key correlates of muscle strength. 11 Reduced CSA has been associated with greater fatigue and poorer quality of life in both healthy individuals and women with FM, with additional links to increased social isolation in the latter group. 12

Various studies have shown that people with FM have lower functional capacity and physical performance than the general population.13–16 Significant correlations have also been observed between the impact of FM (FIQ) and the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) with different functional tests in women diagnosed with FM. 17

Despite growing evidence of muscle function deterioration, reduced physical capacity, and altered body composition in women with FM, the structural characteristics of skeletal muscle and their functional relevance remain insufficiently explored. Ultrasound measurements, such as VL-CSA and thickness of vastus lateralis, offer a non-invasive and reliable method for quantifying muscle morphology; however, their association with functional performance and anthropometric indicators in this population has not been clearly established to date.

Although peripheral muscle alterations have been described in some studies, FM is primarily understood as a disorder of central sensitization rather than a primary muscle disease. Therefore, any structural or morphological differences observed in skeletal muscle should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect secondary consequences of chronic pain, fatigue, and reduced physical activity rather than intrinsic muscle pathology. Clarifying whether ultrasound-derived muscle characteristics relate to functional performance may help contextualize these secondary changes within the broader clinical picture of FM.

The main objective of the study was to analyse muscle architecture variables using ultrasonography in patients with FM. Secondary objectives were to correlate ultrasound-derived variables with physical capacity, assessed through functional tests and to determine whether there is a relationship between ultrasound measures and anthropometric variables assessed through bioimpedance.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study sample consisted of 80 patients diagnosed with FM according to the 2016 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria. Participants were aged between 18 and 75 years and were members of the Afinsyfacro Association in Móstoles (Madrid, Spain). All individuals who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate voluntarily were included. The research was conducted between 31 January 2025 and 15 December 2025. As this was a descriptive observational study, the criteria set out in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed. 18

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) adults with a confirmed medical diagnosis of FM; (b) aged between 18 and 75 years; (c) sufficient cognitive ability and comprehension to complete the self-administered questionnaires.

Exclusion criteria were: (i) presence of interventions or injuries that could interfere with the ultrasound image; (ii) inability to perform functional assessments; (iii) pregnancy at the time of the study; (iv) recent surgical procedures or traumatic that could impair mobility or alter physical functioning; (v) neurological conditions affecting mobility and pain perception; (vi) metabolic disorders with a possible influence on body composition; (vii) patients with implanted pacemakers.

Questionnaires

FIQ: The Spanish version of this questionnaire was used to assess the impact of FM on functional capacity and quality of life. Scores range from 0 to 100, with 0 representing the best condition and 100 the worst (Supplementary Material 1). 19

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A/HADS-D): we used these questionnaires to assess the symptoms of anxiety and depression. They consist of 14 items divided into two subscales of seven items each, with each item scored between 0 to 3. Total scores range from 0 to 7 (normal), 8 to 10 (mild case), and ≥ 11 (clinical case). 20

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI): we used this questionnaire to assess the sleep quality. This questionnaire comprises 24 items each scored from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates no difficulty and 3 indicates severe difficulty. The global score (0–21) classifies respondents as good sleepers (≤5) or poor sleepers (>5) (Supplementary Material 2). 21

Functional tests and pain

Handgrip Strength (HGS): Participants sat with their arms resting on armrests and held a digital dynamometer (Saehan DHD-1) with their elbow flexed at 90°, their wrist in 0-30° dorsiflexion, and 0-15° ulnar deviation. Three maximum efforts of 5 seconds each were performed with the dominant hand, interspersed with 30-second rest intervals. Handgrip strength values were recorded in kilograms, and the mean of the three trials was used for analysis. 22

Chair test (5-STST): Participants began seated on a standardised chair (46 cm high) with their feet flat on the floor and their arms crossed over. They were instructed to stand up and sit down completely five times consecutively as quickly as possible without using their arms. The time, in seconds, required to complete the five repetitions was recorded from the moment the investigator gave the ‘go’ signal 22

Gait speed (4-MWT): This test was used to assess walking speed (m/s). Participants were instructed to walk 4 m. as fast as possible after the ‘Ready, go’ signal. The time was recorded in seconds, and the mean of the two attempts was used for analysis. 22

Timed Up Go (TUG): Participants were instructed to stand up from a 46 cm-high chair, walk 3 m, turn around a cone, return to the chair, and sit down. Two attempts were performed, with a 3-minute rest period between trials. The test began following the ‘go’ signal, and the time taken to complete the task was recorded by the investigators. The time taken to complete the test was recorded in seconds, and the mean of the two attempts was calculated for analysis. 23

Algometry: to assess pain sensitivity, an analogue algometer (Fischer FPK 20) applied to specific tender points (bilateral epicondyle, inner knee, and trochanter). Three measurements were taken at ≥30-s intervals, and the mean value was calculated. 24 Pain intensity was evaluated using a VAS, a 10 cm line on which participants indicated their perceived pain intensity using 0-10 scale. 25

Anthropometric and ultrasound measurements

Body composition analysis: Weight and body composition were assessed using direct segmental multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody® 770, Bilbao, Spain), applying six frequencies (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, and 1000 kHz). This technique provides accurate estimates of fat mass, lean mass, and body water content and distribution. 26

Muscle architecture measurements: Participants were positioned in the supine position with the limbs relaxed and aligned in the anatomical position before ultrasound assessment. The site used to evaluate vastus lateralis (VL) muscle architecture was determined at the midpoint (50%) between the greater trochanter and the knee joint line. The ultrasound probe was positioned with a neutral orientation, perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the limb. A layer of conductive gel was applied to the skin before the probe made contact to facilitate image acquisition. For each variable, three ultrasound images were obtained, and the average of these measurements was used for subsequent analysis. The pennation angle (AP) was calculated as the angle between the muscle fascicle and the deep aponeurosis. The CSA was assessed in panoramic images by freehand planimetry of the VL belly within the aponeuroses (Figure 1).11,27 Muscle thickness was defined as the linear distance between the superficial and deep aponeuroses of each muscle evaluated. Measurements were obtained by drawing a perpendicular line between these two structures at the point of greatest thickness observed in the ultrasound image 30 Three independent images were acquired at each anatomical site, and the mean value of these three measurements was used for subsequent statistical analysis. All ultrasound scans were performed by a single investigator (E.C-F-P) with more than 10 years of prior experience in musculoskeletal ultrasound. Image acquisition and data extraction were performed by another independent investigator. Neither had access at any time to the participants’ clinical or functional data. Although reliability was not directly assessed in the present study, all measurements were conducted under strictly standardised conditions by experienced operators, following previously described protocols that have demonstrated high reliability.28–30Figure 1. Ethical issues

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Clínico San Carlos (approval number 24/745- EC_X) and was registered on https://clinicaltrials.gov/ under the identifier NCT06253273.

All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the 2014 Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association concerning medical research involving human subjects. Participants’ privacy and confidentiality were rigorously protected, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants; documentation is securely held by the corresponding author.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS software, version 29.0. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated prior to performing parametric tests. Associations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rho for non-parametric data and Pearson’s correlation coefficient for parametric data. Where appropriate, multiple comparisons were adjusted to control for Type I error. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Outliers were identified and examined for plausibility; extreme values were retained only if they reflected valid measurements.

All analyses were conducted according to assumptions of the selected statistical tests, ensuring robustness and reproducibility of the results. A multivariate lineal regression model was used to analyze the relationship between the variable of interest, VL-CSA and the other variables in the study, including all participants, regardless of their sex. A multicollinearity analysis was performed using the VIF. The VIF values below 5 for the model indicate the absence of significant multicollinearity. The multivariate lineal model was calculated using the “stepwise forward” strategy.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to analyse muscle architecture variables (specifically VL-CSA, RF-CSA and VL thickness) in women with FM, and to examine their associations with anthropometric characteristics and functional capacity. Overall, the findings show moderate associations between muscle architecture variables and functional tests (5-STST and HGS), suggesting that these measures are related to physical performance in women with FM.

The functional profile observed, characterised by impaired lower-limb performance alongside relatively preserved handgrip strength, suggests a differential impact of FM on weight-bearing versus non-weight-bearing tasks. Specifically, performance in the 5-STST and TUG indicated reduced physical capacity,31,32 whereas handgrip strength values were approximately at the 50th percentile for age- and sex-matched populations. 33 Gait speed was also below normative values for women aged 50–59 years. 34 In addition, the sample presented an overweight profile with high fat percentage and relatively low skeletal muscle mass (SMM), consistent with previous reports in Spanish cohorts of women with FM. 35

Although reference values for muscle architecture variables in populations with FM remain scarce, parameters such as VL-CSA, muscle thickness, and AP have been characterised in other cohorts. In healthy women aged 50–60 years, VL thickness of 1.9 ± 0.5 cm and 1.89 ± 0.27 cm have been described, while AP of 17.3 ± 4.9° and 12.19 ± 1.46°, respectively, have been described.36,37 Regarding CSA, values reported in healthy women of similar age are lower than those observed in our sample, suggesting that RF-CSA in this cohort is not reduced despite functional impairment. 36 In patellofemoral pain syndrome, significantly lower VL-CSA values have been observed compared with healthy controls. 38

It has been demonstrated that VL-CSA and thickness of VL are directly related to muscle strength.39,40 Previous studies have reported associations between VL-CSA, VL thickness and muscle strength measures in different population, in women with osteoarthritis, lower knee extension strength has been associated with a greater risk of worsening knee pain. This population reports greater pain and inability to perform knee extension. 41 In our sample, relationships between the HGS and 5-STST functional tests have been observed, even though these patients do not have this condition.

In our sample, VL-CSA showed statistically significant correlations with 5-STST and HGS. The same occurs in older populations, where a correlation is observed between VL-CSA with stride length and gait speed. 42

On the other hand, and in relation to body composition parameters, we found that the VL-CSA and RF-CSA correlates significantly (p<0.001) with the anthropometric variables FFMDL WDL, SMM and trochanter circumference. A study in obese population showed that RF-CSA is a strong predictor of active cell mass, along with HGS and BMI. 43 Consistently, in our regression model, VL-CSA was significantly associated with both anthropometric and functional factors. BMI emerged as the strongest predictor, suggesting that greater VL-CSA is associated with higher body weight.44,45 Importantly, a larger CSA does not necessarily imply better muscle quality, particularly in patients with FM, because CSA reflects structural size rather than contractile function.46,47 The significant association with trochanter circumference supports the use of ultrasound as a valid tool for assessing muscle morphology and its potential role as a structural biomarker in FM. 48

The observed associations between VL-CSA and functional tests (5-STST and HGS) suggest that muscle architecture variables and functional performance may be related in women with FM. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes determining whether these morphological characteristics influence functional capacity or simply coexist with other factors associated with physical condition. 49 Previous studies have found correlations between ultrasound-derived measures and absolute strength in the upper and lower limbs. In healthy women, we found a correlation between leg extension strength and VL-CSA, but no significant correlation was found with HGS. 50 Interestingly, no significant association was observed between pain intensity and muscle architecture variables. This finding suggests that pain and physical capacity may represent partially independent dimensions in FM, supporting previous evidence that functional impairment is not solely driven by pain severity 51

Importantly, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences regarding the relationship between muscle architecture and functional performance. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence that variations in muscle morphology determine physical capacity, as both may be influenced by other external factors.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the use of ultrasound as a complementary tool for assessing muscle morphology and physical condition in women with FM. 52 Ultrasound-derived variables, such as VL-CSA, VL thickness and AP VL, provide objective information on muscle mass and architecture, which could help clinicians identify patients with alterations in muscle mass and low physical capacity. Given the observed associations between VL-CSA and functional outcomes, particularly handgrip strength and the 5-STST, ultrasound assessment may contribute to the early detection of muscle deterioration and the monitoring of adaptations to exercise-based interventions. As ultrasound is a non-invasive, low-cost and widely accessible imaging technique, it could be used as a tool to complement patient-reported outcomes and functional performance tests in clinical practice. However, muscle morphology should not be considered a primary diagnostic target; rather, the assessment of muscle architecture can contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation of physical function.

Limitations and future lines of research

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the sample was small and consisted predominantly of women and belonged to a single association. Second, the analysis did not incorporate information regarding participants’ habitual physical activity levels or medication used or duration of FM, all these factors can affect muscle morphology and functional capacity Third, the absence of a control group prevents direct comparison with healthy individuals, limiting the ability to determine whether the observed findings are specific to FM or reflect more general patterns. Additionally, ultrasound assessments were restricted to the lower extremities, preventing a broader understanding of potential architectural alterations in upper-limb musculature. In addition, intra-rater and inter-rater reliability were not formally evaluated for the ultrasound measurements. The use of a stepwise variable-selection procedure should be considered a limitation of the present study. Although this approach was useful for exploratory model building and for identifying variables independently associated with VL-CSA, stepwise methods are data-driven and may produce sample-dependent models, unstable coefficient estimates, and an increased risk of overfitting. Furthermore, clinically relevant variables may be excluded when they do not meet the statistical criteria for entry or retention. Consequently, the associations identified in the present model should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation using prespecified regression models and validation in independent samples. Although image acquisition and analysis were performed by experienced investigators following standardised protocols with previously reported high reliability, intra- and inter-rater reliability were not formally assessed in the present study. Consequently, measurement error and observer-related variability cannot be completely excluded, which may have influenced the precision of muscle architecture measurements and, therefore, the strength of the observed associations.

Furthermore, although muscle architecture values were interpreted in relation to previously reported reference values, the lack of well-established normative data for FM populations together with the absence of complementary indicators of muscle quality, such as echo intensity or neuromuscular activation, limits the interpretation of cross-sectional area and muscle thickness as surrogate markers of muscle function. Therefore, the apparent preservation of muscle morphology observed in this study should be interpreted with caution, as these structural parameters may be influenced by body composition, chronic pain-related adaptations, or other systemic factors rather than reflecting true muscle function.

Future research should incorporate control groups, longitudinal designs, and interventional approaches to clarify causal relationships and determine whether targeted exercise interventions can modify muscle architecture and improve functional outcomes. Expanding ultrasound assessment to additional muscle groups and integrating objective measures of physical activity would further enhance understanding of musculoskeletal adaptations in FM.

Conclusions

In this cohort of women with fibromyalgia, ultrasound-derived measures of the vastus lateralis and rectus femoris demonstrated moderate associations with anthropometric characteristics and functional performance. These relationships should be interpreted within the broader context of fibromyalgia as a condition primarily driven by central sensitization rather than intrinsic muscle pathology. The morphological differences observed in muscle architecture are therefore more likely to reflect secondary adaptations related to physical deconditioning, reduced physical activity, and chronic symptom burden.

Although ultrasound cannot determine the underlying mechanisms of these changes, it offers a non-invasive and accessible method to characterize physical status in this population. The associations identified between muscle morphology, handgrip strength, and lower-limb functional tests highlight the potential value of integrating ultrasound assessments into comprehensive evaluations of physical function. Such information may help clinicians identify individuals who could benefit from tailored exercise-based interventions aimed at improving strength, mobility, and overall conditioning.

However, the clinical interpretation of ultrasound findings must remain cautious. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, no causal inferences can be drawn, and muscle morphology should not be considered a primary therapeutic target in fibromyalgia. Instead, these findings underscore the importance of addressing physical deconditioning as part of a broader, multidisciplinary approach that acknowledges the central sensitization mechanisms underlying the syndrome. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how changes in muscle architecture evolve over time and whether they respond to targeted rehabilitation strategies.
