Summary

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a heterogeneous syndrome for which creatinine and urine output incompletely represent underlying biology and temporal evolution. We organise AKI pathophysiology into four interconnected dimensions: initiation and effector injury; cell fate and immune microenvironment; repair and outcome determination; and systemic integration. These dimensions encompass microvascular and metabolic failure, oxidative injury, regulated cell death, inflammation, adaptive and maladaptive repair, senescence, fibrosis, and organ crosstalk. We translate these hallmarks into measurable phenotypes using biomarkers, imaging, and functional tests, and propose an aetiological trigger-hallmarks-phase (E-H-P) framework linking cause, dominant biology, and disease phase. Practical examples illustrate how E-H-P phenotyping can complement conventional staging and support trial enrichment. We distinguish established supportive care from investigational mechanism-directed therapies and identify priorities for biomarker validation, phase-specific intervention, and prevention of AKI-to-CKD transition.

Search strategy and selection criteria

PubMed was searched for English-language articles published from January 1, 2014, to June 10, 2026, using combinations of the terms “acute kidney injury”, “AKI”, “biomarker”, “phenotype”, “microvascular”, “mitochondria”, “metabolism”, “ferroptosis”, “senescence”, “fibrosis”, “organ crosstalk”, “experimental model”, and “therapy”. Reference lists of relevant primary studies, consensus documents, and reviews were also screened. Priority was given to human studies, guidelines or consensus statements, systematic reviews, and recent mechanistic studies; older seminal work was retained where necessary. Final selection was based on relevance to the hallmark and E-H-P framework.

Outstanding questions

1. Which compact biomarker and imaging combinations can identify dominant AKI hallmarks rapidly enough to alter management, and how should the H dimension be updated as biology changes over time?

2. Can E-H-P phenotypes be reproduced across sepsis, surgery, nephrotoxicity, ischaemia, and AKI on chronic kidney disease using affordable, standardised assays?

3. Which hallmark-defined subgroups show treatment-effect heterogeneity in prospective trials, and what endpoints best capture both acute benefit and prevention of AKI-to-CKD transition?

4. How can adaptive fibroblast activation, protective cell-cycle arrest, and reparative inflammation be distinguished from persistent fibrogenic or senescent states so that late interventions are timed safely?

5. Which cross-organ measurements of congestion, inflammation, and microvascular failure provide actionable information beyond conventional organ-failure scores?

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is clinically defined as an abrupt decline in renal excretory function, typically manifesting as elevated serum creatinine and/or reduced urine output.1 It is a prevalent and serious condition, affecting over 13 million individuals globally each year and contributing to approximately 1.7 million deaths.2 The clinical burden of AKI spans all healthcare settings, from community-acquired episodes such as dehydration or drug toxicity to hospital and intensive care unit cases linked to shock, sepsis, major surgery, or multi-organ failure.3 Outcomes vary widely, ranging from full renal recovery to persistent dysfunction or death, positioning AKI as a significant risk factor for the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease in survivors.4

A hallmark of AKI is its heterogeneity.4 AKI can result from a wide array of causes, including ischaemia (such as hypotension or renal artery occlusion), nephrotoxic exposures (drugs, contrast agents, environmental toxins), sepsis and systemic inflammation, or urinary tract obstruction, each initiating distinct patterns of renal injury.5 Even within a given aetiology, patient-specific factors such as age, baseline kidney function, and comorbidities influence the severity and phenotype of AKI.6 Clinically, some cases present primarily as haemodynamic disturbances with minimal structural damage, whereas others involve extensive tubular necrosis and inflammatory infiltration. This biological and clinical diversity complicates diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic decision-making, particularly because conventional diagnostic criteria, namely elevations in serum creatinine or reductions in urine output, fail to reflect the underlying pathophysiological processes or the true extent of injury.7

Importantly, AKI does not occur in isolation but exists along a continuum that spans both acute and chronic kidney disorders.8 The concept of acute kidney disease (AKD) has emerged to describe a subacute phase of kidney dysfunction lasting from 7 to 90 days after the initial AKI event, effectively bridging AKI and CKD.9 Many patients undergo an “AKI-to-CKD transition”, in which incomplete or maladaptive repair after acute injury results in progressive nephron loss, interstitial fibrosis, and long-term functional decline.10 Traditional clinical metrics often fail to capture these evolving processes. For instance, serum creatinine may return to baseline despite ongoing cellular injury or repair, and the kidney's substantial functional reserve can mask early damage until nearly half of the nephrons are lost. As a result, some patients develop subclinical AKI, an injury that falls below current diagnostic thresholds yet still carries the risk of adverse outcomes.11 Similarly, many AKI survivors exhibit persistent renal impairment beyond the acute phase, reflecting incomplete recovery that may not be apparent from creatinine alone.12 Such subclinical injuries and post-AKI sequelae highlight blind spots in current diagnostics, representing core challenges that new biomarkers and our hallmarks framework seek to address.13 These limitations underscore the need for more pathophysiologically grounded biomarkers and nuanced classification systems to enable earlier detection, targeted intervention, and improved long-term management. However, a critical therapeutic dilemma remains: current AKI management is largely supportive, with no approved specific pharmacological agents to directly mitigate kidney injury.14 This gap underlines the urgent need for precise, mechanism-based interventions, an urgency that our pathophysiological hallmark-based subtyping aims to address.

Unlike conventional AKI classifications based on creatinine and urine output, anatomical compartment, or a single cause, our modular framework separates three questions: what triggered the episode (Aetiological trigger, E), which biological hallmarks are active and measurable (H), and when in the injury-repair trajectory the patient is assessed (Phase, P). The E-H-P framework is not intended to replace KDIGO staging. Its additional value is to provide a common vocabulary linking mechanism, measurement, model selection, trial enrichment, and phase-appropriate intervention. Hallmarks of AKI

Initiation and effector phase

The initiation and effector phase links the inciting insult to early loss of cellular energetics. Ischaemia, sepsis, and nephrotoxins converge variably on microcirculatory dysfunction, tissue hypoxia, mitochondrial ATP depletion, and redox injury, which together impair tubular transport and promote cell death. The relative contribution of each process differs by aetiology; the three core hallmarks discussed below are therefore complementary rather than sequential or universally co-dominant (Fig. 1).Fig. 1 Hallmarks of AKI. Schematic overview of the major pathobiological hallmarks of acute kidney injury across a temporal continuum. Early insults, including nephrotoxins, ischaemia, and sepsis, initiate microvascular dysfunction/endothelial injury and mitochondrial-metabolic failure, leading to ATP depletion, oxidative stress, and tubular injury. Injured tubular cells undergo apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, release damage-associated molecular patterns, and reshape the inflammatory microenvironment. Repair subsequently diverges towards adaptive regeneration or maladaptive repair characterised by senescence and fibrosis, ultimately culminating in systemic integration, organ crosstalk, and functional decline. Representative biomarkers associated with injury, inflammation, and repair are indicated.

Microvascular dysfunction and tissue hypoxia

AKI frequently involves profound renal microvascular dysfunction.15 In ischaemia-reperfusion injury, the “no-reflow” phenomenon reflects persistent capillary hypoperfusion despite restored systemic pressure, driven by endothelial swelling, activation, and microthrombosis.16 Endothelial cells lose anticoagulant and vasodilatory properties, upregulate adhesion molecules, and promote leucocyte and platelet adhesion, leading to congestion.17 The outer medulla is particularly vulnerable due to its high oxygen demand and limited supply, resulting in severe hypoxia and necrosis of S3 segment and thick ascending limb cells.18 Inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species (ROS)18 exacerbate barrier dysfunction, causing oedema that further compresses capillaries.19 During recovery, capillary rarefaction often persists, contributing to chronic hypoperfusion, fibrosis, and functional decline. Thus, microvascular collapse in AKI amplifies acute injury and predisposes to long-term renal deterioration.20

Renal microcirculation and hypoxia in AKI can be evaluated using advanced imaging modalities such as contrast-enhanced ultrasound and BOLD-MRI, which reveal perfusion deficits and tissue deoxygenation.21 Experimental tools include oxygen-sensitive probes and expression of hypoxia-responsive genes like erythropoietin. Biomarkers of endothelial injury, such as angiopoietin-2, endothelin-1, and endothelial microparticles, have been proposed but lack routine clinical application.20 Therapeutically, preserving microvascular integrity is critical. Beyond maintaining systemic perfusion, targeted strategies include renal-selective vasodilators, anticoagulants to reduce microthrombi, and endothelial-stabilising agents. Nitric oxide donors and prostacyclin analogues enhance capillary flow in models, though systemic effects limit clinical use.22 Emerging therapies such as VEGF-based angiogenic agents or Ang/Tie2 modulators show promise for promoting microvascular repair.23 This hallmark highlights the importance of correcting hypoxia and vascular dysfunction to mitigate AKI and prevent progression to CKD.

Mitochondrial-metabolic failure

Renal tubular cells, particularly in the proximal tubule, rely heavily on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for energy.24 In AKI, ischaemia, nephrotoxins, and sepsis disrupt mitochondrial function, triggering a rapid ATP decline.25 Impaired FAO leads to lipid accumulation and energy shortfall, while a compensatory shift to glycolysis is insufficient to sustain tubular transport and exacerbates acidosis.26 Mitochondrial injury also promotes excessive fission and inhibits mitophagy, resulting in ROS-leaking dysfunctional mitochondria that amplify oxidative damage.27 NAD+ depletion, due to PARP activation, further impairs metabolic resilience. This mitochondrial collapse underpins ionic imbalance, transport failure, and ultimately tubular cell death.28

When fatty-acid oxidation and glucose oxidation are constrained, ketone bodies may provide an alternative mitochondrial substrate and signalling cue. In mouse models of AKI, enhanced 3-hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2)-dependent ketogenesis increased β-hydroxybutyrate availability and preserved cellular ATP levels.29 Subsequent studies further implicated renal HMGCS2-driven ketogenesis in protection against ischaemic injury and in early reparative responses associated with changes in matrix stiffness.30,31 Consistently, exogenous β-hydroxybutyrate attenuated sepsis- and cisplatin-induced AKI by limiting inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress and ferroptotic injury.32, 33, 34 These findings remain preclinical; optimal dose, route, timing, and the safety of inducing ketosis in critically ill patients have not been established.

Multiple indicators reflect mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction in AKI. Reduced cortical ATP, elevated lactate, impaired mitochondrial respiration, and diminished activities of OXPHOS and FAO enzymes are observed in injured kidneys.35 Histologically, swollen mitochondria with cristae loss and lipid accumulation denote energy failure.36 Metabolomic profiling reveals altered succinate, acyl-carnitines, and NAD+/NADH ratios.37 Therapeutically, mitochondria-targeted antioxidants (e.g., MitoQ, SS-31) mitigate ROS and stabilise membranes, while agents promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, such as PPAR agonists or NAD+ precursors, restore energy metabolism.38 Inhibiting deleterious metabolic fluxes (e.g., succinate dehydrogenase blockade with dimethyl malonate) has also shown protective effects. Preserving mitochondrial integrity is thus central to limiting injury and promoting recovery in AKI.39

Oxidative stress and macromolecular damage

Oxidative stress, a hallmark of nearly all AKI forms, arises from an imbalance between reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) and antioxidant defences.40,41 ROS originate from dysfunctional mitochondria, activated immune cells (via NADPH oxidases), and injured renal cells (e.g., xanthine oxidase, uncoupled NOS).40,42 These species damage cellular components through lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation or nitrosylation, and DNA oxidation (e.g., 8-oxoguanine formation), triggering apoptosis or cell cycle arrest.43,44 Oxidative stress also amplifies injury by activating ferroptosis and pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB.40,45 Antioxidant systems, enzymes like superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase, and molecules like glutathione, are often depleted or impaired in AKI, especially due to NADPH loss.40,46 This establishes a vicious cycle where oxidative injury further compromises cellular defences, leading to escalating ROS accumulation and sustained tissue damage.40

Oxidative stress in AKI can be monitored via biomarkers of molecular injury. Elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) and F2-isoprostanes indicate lipid peroxidation; protein oxidation is reflected by carbonyl or nitrotyrosine levels; and altered glutathione redox status signals redox imbalance.47 Nrf2 pathway activation, marked by upregulation of genes like haem oxygenase-1, also denotes oxidative stress.48 Therapeutic targeting has proven difficult, as general antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine and ascorbic acid have shown limited efficacy in established AKI. Current strategies focus on spatiotemporal precision, with mitochondria-targeted antioxidants like MitoQ and SS-31, as well as Nrf2 activators, aiming to enhance endogenous defences. However, these interventions must be delivered during the critical window of injury and without impairing physiological ROS signalling.49 As a result, oxidative stress remains a key but complex therapeutic target in AKI.

Cell fate and immune microenvironment

This stage centres on how intrinsic renal cells and the immune system respond to the acute insults.18 In the face of energy crisis and oxidative damage, stressed renal tubular cells undergo phenotypic changes and their fate (survival, death, or senescence) is determined by the balance of regulated cell death pathways.50 At the same time, injury-associated molecules (e.g., DAMPs) released from stressed or dying cells recruit and activate immune cells, reshaping the renal immune microenvironment.51 Early on, this immune response tends to be aggressively pro-inflammatory and tissue-damaging, but it can evolve towards a more reparative profile in later phases.52 The molecular links among immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial-metabolic failure, and regulated tubular cell death are summarised in Fig. 2.Fig. 2 Molecular mechanism of AKI. Tubular stress and fate change

The renal tubular epithelium, especially the proximal tubule, is the primary target of most AKI insults.18 Injured cells exhibit acute stress responses and phenotypic shifts, including brush border loss, polarity disruption, and detachment from the basement membrane.53 These changes impair reabsorption and promote luminal debris accumulation.54 Stressed tubular cells often dedifferentiate into a progenitor-like state, suppressing specialised transporters while upregulating injury-response genes such as KIM-1 and NGAL.55 Loss of cytoskeletal integrity facilitates cast formation and may trigger maladaptive signalling.56 Notably, this reprogrammed state also enables repair, as surviving cells must proliferate and redifferentiate to restore nephron architecture.57 The outer medullary S3 segment is particularly susceptible due to its high metabolic demand,58 though other segments, including the thick ascending limb, may also be affected in severe AKI.59 At the molecular level, pathways such as Notch and Wnt/β-catenin govern tubular cell dedifferentiation and proliferation60; transient activation of these developmental programmes supports regeneration, whereas sustained Wnt/Notch signalling can become maladaptive and, if unchecked, drive fibrogenesis.61

This hallmark centres on measurable indicators of tubular injury.62 Biomarkers such as urinary KIM-1 and NGAL reflect damage to proximal and distal tubule cells, while L-FABP signals oxidative stress in the proximal tubule.63 Injured epithelia also release cytokines (e.g., CCL2, IL-6), indicating epithelial-immune crosstalk.64 Therapeutically, preserving epithelial integrity and reducing tubular stress are key goals.4 Strategies include enhancing adaptive responses, such as hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) activation65 or autophagy induction,66 and reducing transport workload, as seen with sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, which lower AKI risk by decreasing proximal tubule oxygen demand.67 Pharmacological targeting of pathways involved in cytoskeletal breakdown or cell death may further protect tubular architecture.68 Maintaining epithelial function not only limits acute injury but also facilitates effective regeneration.69

Regulated cell death

Regulated cell-death programmes overlap in AKI but vary in relative importance across aetiologies. Apoptosis is prominent in cisplatin nephrotoxicity, severe ATP depletion favours necrotic injury, and necroptosis can amplify ischaemic and inflammatory damage. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent death programme driven by lipid peroxidation, is especially relevant to haemoglobin- or haem-rich injury, rhabdomyolysis, cisplatin toxicity, and selected ischaemia-reperfusion models. Pyroptosis links inflammasome activation to gasdermin pore formation and is prominent in inflammatory contexts such as sepsis. These pathways converge through mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species and release damage-associated molecular patterns, including HMGB1 and mitochondrial DNA, that propagate innate immune activation. The dominant pathway should therefore be inferred from aetiology, timing, and molecular readouts rather than assumed from AKI stage alone.

Specific biochemical and histological markers can identify active cell death programmes in AKI.70,71 TUNEL staining detects apoptotic DNA fragmentation, while lipid peroxidation products (e.g., malondialdehyde, 4-HNE) and reduced GPX4 activity indicate ferroptosis.45,72 Elevated IL-1β and IL-18 levels in tissue or urine suggest pyroptosis and inflammasome activation, whereas LDH release and circulating cell-free DNA reflect nonspecific cell lysis.73, 74, 75 Therapeutically, targeting these pathways has shown promise in preclinical models: caspase inhibitors reduce apoptosis; ferroptosis can be blocked by lipid peroxidation inhibitors or iron chelators; necroptosis is attenuated by RIPK1/RIPK3 blockade.45,76, 77, 78, 79 Given the overlap among death pathways, combinatorial approaches, such as mitochondria-targeted interventions preserving cellular energetics, may offer broader cytoprotection. These convergent pathways make intervention timing central to limiting irreversible tubular loss and downstream inflammatory amplification.

Inflammation and immune remodelling

Innate and adaptive immune responses are central to AKI pathogenesis.80 Following injury, neutrophils rapidly infiltrate the kidney, releasing ROS and proteases that exacerbate tissue damage.81 Monocytes differentiate into pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, producing cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) and chemokines that recruit additional leukocytes.52 Resident macrophages and dendritic cells, activated by DAMPs, contribute to antigen presentation and further cytokine release.82 T lymphocytes may aggravate injury via IFN-γ and cytotoxic mechanisms, while regulatory T cells can promote resolution.83 Although inflammation aids in debris clearance and defence, unchecked responses lead to collateral injury.84 This hallmark reflects a feed-forward loop in which immune activation amplifies tissue damage, perpetuating injury unless appropriately modulated.

The immune response in AKI evolves dynamically.18 Following the acute pro-inflammatory phase, a transition towards resolution typically occurs, with macrophages adopting a reparative M2 phenotype that secretes factors such as TGF-β and VEGF, supporting tissue remodelling but potentially promoting fibrosis if unchecked.85 Inflammatory activity can be assessed using biomarkers such as NGAL, IL-8 (neutrophil activation), MCP-1/CCL2 (monocyte recruitment), and interleukins (IL-6, IL-1β, IL-18).63 Spatial profiling techniques have mapped immune cell distributions in injured kidneys, revealing neutrophil and macrophage clustering.86 While broad immunosuppression has shown limited efficacy and high risk in AKI, precision strategies are under development. These include complement inhibitors, cytokine-neutralising antibodies (e.g., IL-1, IL-6), and chemokine receptor blockers (e.g., CCR2/CCR5).56 Crucially, therapeutic success depends on timing, as interventions beneficial during early inflammation may hinder subsequent repair.87 Aligning immune modulation with the AKI phase and subtype may limit injury, support regeneration, and reduce progression to CKD.

Recent studies highlight the complex intercellular communication within the AKI immune microenvironment.88 Injured tubular epithelial cells release DAMPs that activate pattern-recognition receptors on immune cells, while immune cell-derived cytokines and chemokines feed back to influence tubular cell survival and repair.89 Crosstalk among neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells modulates the balance between destructive inflammation and reparative immunity, ultimately shaping the trajectory of injury and healing.90

Repair and outcome determination

The aftermath of the acute injury phase is a critical crossroads where the quality of repair dictates long-term kidney outcomes. Surviving cells attempt to restore normal structure and function, and the success of this adaptive repair hinges on the ability of cells to re-enter the cell cycle and redifferentiate appropriately.50 Key hallmarks of this stage include adaptive repair and regeneration, cell-cycle arrest and senescence, and maladaptive repair leading to fibrosis.18 When repair is successful, nephron architecture and function are largely restored.50 When the repair programme is derailed by persistent cell-cycle arrest, senescence, or dysregulated healing, maladaptive outcomes ensue.91 Maladaptive repair is characterised by fibrosis, capillary rarefaction, and chronic inflammation, and it ultimately drives the transition from AKI to CKD.13

Adaptive repair and regeneration

The kidney possesses a remarkable capacity for adaptive repair following AKI, contingent on preservation of the epithelial and stromal framework.50 Surviving tubular cells exit quiescence and enter a proliferative phase once injury signals subside, replenishing lost epithelium within 1–3 days in rodent models.92 These cells subsequently re-differentiate, restoring polarity, brush border integrity, and transport function.50 When successful, repair yields near-complete architectural and functional recovery, marked by normalisation of glomerular filtration, clearance of casts, and resolution of inflammatory infiltrates.18 Injury biomarkers such as KIM-1 and NGAL decline to baseline.63 Effective regeneration depends on preservation of the extracellular matrix scaffold, sufficient vascular support, and timely cessation of proliferative signalling to avoid hyperplasia.13

Adaptive repair also requires a transient stromal response. In ischaemia-reperfusion models, interstitial fibroblasts activate within hours through tubular Sonic hedgehog signalling and proliferate before peak epithelial recovery; blocking this early response worsens injury, indicating that early fibroblast activation can support matrix integrity and regeneration.93 The same response becomes maladaptive when activation persists, shifts towards myofibroblast states, and drives excess matrix deposition. Timing and duration, rather than fibroblast activation per se, therefore distinguish adaptive repair from fibrosis.

Adaptive repair in AKI can be assessed by proliferative markers (e.g., Ki-67, PCNA), declining injury biomarkers (e.g., KIM-1, NGAL), and morphological restoration of brush borders and tubule architecture.50 Enhancing regeneration is a key therapeutic goal.94 Growth factors like HGF and IGF-1 promote tubular proliferation, while transient activation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling may support recovery.61 Cell-based therapies using mesenchymal stem/stromal cells facilitate repair largely via paracrine effects.95 Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived renal progenitors represent an emerging regenerative strategy.96 Modulating inflammation, particularly through macrophage polarisation towards a reparative phenotype (e.g., IL-10/TGF-β balance), further aids regeneration.97 The concept of microenvironment optimisation emphasises maintaining adequate perfusion, minimising ongoing insults, and supporting metabolic demands. Together, these strategies aim to accelerate renal recovery and prevent progression to chronic dysfunction.

Cell-cycle arrest and senescence

Following acute injury, renal tubular epithelial cells often activate a cell-cycle arrest programme, typically at the G2/M checkpoint, to facilitate DNA and organelle repair.50 This response, marked by upregulation of p21CIP1 and p16INK4a, can be protective in the short term, limiting maladaptive mitoses and promoting survival.98 However, sustained arrest leads to cellular senescence, characterised by irreversible growth arrest and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).99 Senescent cells, identifiable by p16INK4a expression and SA-β-gal activity, secrete pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic mediators such as IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β.100 In AKI, especially under severe or unresolved injury, the accumulation of these cells contributes to chronic inflammation and fibrogenesis, bridging acute damage with long-term renal decline.101

Tubular cell senescence is increasingly recognised as a driver of the AKI-to-CKD transition.102 Both experimental models and human biopsies reveal that persistent senescent cell burden post-AKI correlates with impaired recovery and fibrosis.103 This hallmark can be assessed via p21/p16 expression, γH2AX staining for DNA damage, or detection of SASP factors in tissue and urine.104 Single-cell transcriptomics have identified injury-senescent proximal tubule populations marked by high KIM-1 and p21 levels, implicating them in fibrogenic signalling.105 Therapeutically, senolytics such as BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibitors have reduced fibrosis in experimental models when administered early, while senomorphics like JAK inhibitors aim to suppress the SASP without inducing cell death.106 Another strategy involves reactivating arrested cells to re-enter regenerative cycles.107 However, these interventions must be precisely timed, implemented early enough to prevent chronic senescence yet not so early that they disrupt protective cell-cycle arrest. Therefore, targeting this hallmark requires nuanced modulation that promotes repair while minimising maladaptive persistence.

No optimal senolytic schedule has been established in human AKI, and preclinical effects are strongly dependent on injury model and timing. In ischaemic AKI, CDK4/6 inhibition with palbociclib during or after the acute phase promoted senescence and fibrosis, whereas dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) produced senolytic and antifibrotic effects that were reversed by subsequent palbociclib.108 In glycerol-induced rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI, post-injury D+Q blocked a senescence-associated macrophage transition and was associated with prolonged nephroprotection.109 By contrast, D+Q pretreatment before folic-acid-induced AKI did not preserve kidney function and increased Lcn2, Havcr1, p21, and senescence-associated secretory phenotype-related gene expression.110 These divergent findings do not define an optimal schedule; rather, they support testing D+Q only in aetiology-specific trials, with the recovery or acute kidney disease phase after peak cell death and evidence of persistent senescence as a plausible window. D+Q remains investigational and should not be used as routine acute-phase therapy.

Maladaptive repair

A substantial subset of AKI survivors experience maladaptive repair, in which healing is incomplete and biased towards fibrosis rather than regeneration.13 Persistent low-grade inflammation, unresolved cell-cycle arrest, hypoxia, and vascular rarefaction contribute to chronic pathological remodelling.111 Interstitial myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition lead to fibrosis; lost capillaries are not restored, perpetuating hypoxia; and some tubules remain atrophic or functionally impaired.60 Clinically, this manifests as sustained GFR decline, proteinuria, or impaired urine concentration, which are hallmarks of CKD.112 Notably, maladaptive changes may be focal and subclinical, with patients appearing to recover yet progressing silently towards CKD.113 This underscores the continuum between AKI and CKD, highlighting AKI as a common initiating event in CKD.

Detecting maladaptive repair involves identifying signs of persistent injury beyond the acute phase.114 Biomarkers such as sustained urinary KIM-1 or albuminuria weeks after AKI predict progression to CKD, indicating ongoing tubular or glomerular stress.115 Imaging techniques like MRI or elastography can reveal fibrotic remodelling, while research biopsies may show interstitial collagen deposition, capillary rarefaction (such as CD34+ endothelial loss), and inflammatory infiltrates.116 Therapeutically, the “repair fork” refers to a transitional period following AKI that offers a critical window for intervention.117 Anti-fibrotic strategies under investigation include inhibition of the TGF-β pathway, early administration of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) to reduce fibrosis and glomerular hypertension, and modulation of extracellular matrix turnover via matrix metalloproteinase activators.60 Promising approaches also include targeting fibrogenic cells, such as blocking the pericyte-to-myofibroblast transition with PDGFR inhibitors, and eliminating senescent cells to reduce SASP-driven fibrosis.60 Recognising and treating patients undergoing maladaptive repair may shift AKI from a self-limited insult to a modifiable entry point into CKD.

Systemic integration

In this perspective, local kidney injury is viewed in the context of whole-organ function and multi-system interactions. The cumulative cellular and molecular disturbances in AKI ultimately converge to produce global loss of renal function.4 At the same time, the injury disrupts the coordination among components of the nephron and triggers far-reaching effects on other organ systems.118 Key hallmarks at this integrative level include overall functional failure of the kidney, nephron network dysfunction with interstitial responses, and organ crosstalk (remote organ interactions).4 Together, these processes make AKI not only a renal problem but a driver and victim of multi-organ dysfunction.

Functional collapse

Functional collapse refers to the rapid loss of the kidney's filtration and regulatory function that defines clinical AKI. As nephrons lose effectiveness, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) drops sharply, leading to the accumulation of waste products.119 Patients frequently develop oliguria or anuria, along with azotaemia and disturbances in fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base homoeostasis, such as hyperkalaemia and metabolic acidosis.120 Haemodynamically, functional collapse may result from acute reductions in renal perfusion pressure or severe intrarenal vasoconstriction, as seen in prerenal AKI, or from intrinsic parenchymal injury that compromises the filtration capacity of multiple nephrons simultaneously.121 Importantly, the severity of functional decline does not always correlate with the degree of structural cell injury.122 For instance, dehydration or shock can acutely lower GFR via vasoconstriction and tubular feedback, producing a reversible prerenal AKI without overt tubular damage.123 Conversely, in early intrinsic AKI, substantial tubular injury may be present even with minimal creatinine elevation, as surviving nephrons transiently compensate.124 Serum creatinine often lags behind actual injury, with diagnostic changes sometimes becoming apparent only 24–48 h after kidney injury.125

Clinical measurements of functional collapse include rising serum creatinine, reduced urine output, and changes in urine composition, such as elevated fractional excretion of sodium in intrinsic acute tubular injury.126 However, these conventional metrics have notable limitations.62 Serum creatinine is influenced by non-renal factors such as muscle mass and volume status, and it underestimates acute injury due to renal reserve and delayed kinetics.127 Urine output can be affected by diuretics and fluid administration, and not all AKI cases present with oliguria.128 Consequently, current research emphasises the early detection of injury before overt functional decline, using stress biomarkers and dynamic functional tests.129 Biomarkers such as NGAL and KIM-1 may rise in urine or plasma even when GFR is still preserved, indicating subclinical AKI.130 The furosemide stress test also provides dynamic insight into renal reserve, where a diminished diuretic response predicts progression to more severe AKI.131 From a translational perspective, functional collapse should trigger timely risk stratification. Monitoring kinetic creatinine changes, integrating injury biomarkers, and evaluating renal reserve can help predict impending decompensation and enable earlier intervention. Preventing or shortening episodes of functional decline is essential for avoiding complications such as volume overload and uraemia.

Nephron network dysfunction and interstitial responses

AKI disrupts the coordinated function of the nephron and its microenvironment, leading to tubulo-glomerular imbalance.11 Normally, tubuloglomerular feedback matches filtration to reabsorptive capacity, but when proximal tubules are injured and fail to reclaim sodium, the macula densa triggers afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction and reduces GFR.132 This response is protective in intent but ultimately deleterious to overall kidney function.133 During early recovery, glomerular filtration may transiently overshoot tubular reabsorptive capacity, resulting in polyuria.134 Intratubular obstruction from necrotic debris and Tamm-Horsfall protein casts raises upstream hydrostatic pressure, lowering net filtration pressure.134 Concurrently, epithelial damage permits filtrate backleak into the interstitium, contributing to local oedema.133 Interstitial oedema, compounded by capillary leak and impaired lymphatic drainage, increases tissue pressure, compressing microvessels and worsening hypoxia.18 These network-level disruptions underlie clinical features such as oliguria in acute tubular necrosis and distinguish intrinsic AKI patterns from prerenal azotaemia.11

Nephron-level dysfunction in AKI can be assessed through urinary findings and functional indices.135 Pigmented granular casts and renal tubular epithelial cell casts in urine sediment suggest intrinsic tubular injury, whereas bland sediment favours prerenal causes.135 Fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) helps differentiate prerenal (<1%) from intrinsic AKI (>2%), reflecting tubular reabsorptive integrity.136 Imaging, particularly ultrasound, aids in ruling out obstruction and identifying parenchymal changes.137 This hallmark emphasises system-level pathophysiology: interventions include relieving post-renal obstruction, optimising fluid balance to reduce interstitial oedema or venous congestion, and adjusting medications based on tubular function. Computational models are emerging to simulate nephron responses to localised injury, enabling more personalised AKI management.138 Incorporating these integrative dynamics into clinical phenotyping can refine decision-making around diuretics, renal replacement therapy, and nephrotoxin use.

Organ crosstalk

Kidney-lung crosstalk is mediated by systemic inflammation, endothelial leak, impaired alveolar fluid clearance, and the hydrostatic effects of fluid overload; conversely, positive-pressure ventilation and hypoxaemia can reduce renal perfusion and increase venous pressure. Besides, kidney–heart interactions include reduced forward flow, venous congestion, neurohormonal activation, and electrolyte or uraemic effects on myocardial function. Recent clinical cohorts support the relevance of these pathways: among 220 patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, higher cystatin C and interleukin-6 were associated with subsequent AKI139; in cardiac surgery, severe intrarenal venous-flow abnormalities were associated with postoperative AKI and longer ventilation140; and in acute heart failure, higher Venous Excess Ultrasound Score grades were associated with greater AKI risk.141 These studies demonstrate the association rather than causality, but they position congestion, inflammation, and microvascular dysfunction as measurable cross-organ phenotypes.

Clinical management should focus on established organ-protective measures: source control and antibiotics for sepsis, haemodynamic optimisation, avoidance of fluid overload and venous congestion, lung-protective ventilation, correction of electrolyte and acid-base disturbances, and kidney replacement therapy for conventional indications. Cytokine adsorption, cytokine blockade, and extracorporeal strategies intended specifically to modulate organ crosstalk remain investigational; current evidence is insufficient for routine AKI treatment. Multicentre sepsis phenotyping further suggests that microvascular and metabolic subgroups with AKI cluster with persistent multiple-organ dysfunction and higher mortality, supporting integrated rather than kidney-isolated risk assessment.142,143 The 2023 Chinese multidisciplinary guideline for cardiorenal syndrome likewise supports phenotype-specific congestion management and coordinated heart-kidney care. In stable chronic cardiorenal phenotypes, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists should follow established heart-failure or chronic-kidney-disease indications; neither class is a treatment for established intrinsic AKI, and use during acute illness requires individual assessment of haemodynamic stability, kidney function, and potassium.144 Kidney-gut crosstalk is clinically less mature: a Mendelian-randomisation and case–control study associated selected microbial profiles with AKI risk and outcome, but causal mechanisms and microbiome-directed interventions require prospective validation.145

Measurable hallmarks: biomarkers, imaging, and phenotyping

Accurate detection of kidney injury processes is essential for early AKI diagnosis and intervention.62 Traditional markers like serum creatinine and urine output are delayed and nonspecific.146 Novel biomarkers now enable earlier and more precise assessment.63 In this section, we emphasise translating the above hallmarks into clinical tools.

Screening and early warning

A key clinical question is whether a patient has already sustained, or is about to sustain, kidney parenchymal injury before overt functional loss.11 To address this, clinicians are moving “beyond creatinine” to deploy early warning tools.147 Stress and injury biomarkers can reveal subclinical AKI or impending injury that traditional metrics miss.148 Tubular injury markers, including NGAL, KIM-1, IL-18, and L-FABP, rise hours before creatinine, signalling that epithelial stress or necrosis is underway even when GFR is still preserved.149 KIM-1 is specific to proximal tubule injury, and its persistence may indicate maladaptive repair.115 Cell-cycle arrest biomarkers TIMP-2 and IGFBP7, combined in the NephroCheck® test, reflect G1 arrest and predict imminent AKI.150 In practice, a positive NephroCheck® result alerts clinicians that a patient is at high risk for developing AKI within the next 12–24 h.151 Integrating these stress biomarkers with functional stress tests enhances early detection.152 For example, the furosemide stress test, which measures urine output after a standardised diuretic dose, can reveal diminished renal reserve; a poor diuretic response serves as an early warning of impending functional collapse.153 By identifying “pre-AKI” states, such tools enable intervention during a window when injury is still preventable or reversible, corresponding to the hallmarks of early functional decline and tubular stress.154 Ongoing biomarker-guided trials aim to personalise nephroprotective therapies based on such hallmark-specific profiles.155

Aetiological profiling and differential diagnosis

Another critical question is determining the driving cause and subtype of AKI in a given patient, for example, whether the injury is predominantly inflammatory, nephrotoxic, or haemodynamic.156 To this end, a biomarker signature approach can help profile the aetiological subtype of AKI.62 Different injury mechanisms release different constellations of biomarkers, and these patterns can be used to infer dominant hallmarks.62 For instance, an inflammation-dominant AKI might be signalled by high cytokine levels (elevated IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1), whereas a tubular injury-dominant AKI would feature high proximal tubule injury markers (KIM-1, NGAL) indicating severe epithelial stress and regulated cell death.157 An endothelial/microvascular dysfunction-dominant AKI could present with markers of endothelial injury (e.g., angiopoietin-2, circulating endothelial microparticles), reflecting profound ischaemia.158 A patient with a metabolic/senescence-heavy AKI (high risk of fibrosis) might show persistent elevation of injury markers like KIM-1 beyond the acute phase or the presence of senescence-associated factors (e.g., β2-microglobulin, SASP cytokines).13

Beyond biomarkers, point-of-care imaging augments aetiological profiling.159 Bedside renal ultrasound with Doppler can assist in distinguishing prerenal from intrinsic AKI and can identify postrenal obstruction.159 For example, in prerenal (functional) AKI, renal blood flow is preserved or shows a high resistive index due to vasoconstriction, whereas intrinsic microvascular AKI may show abnormal parenchymal perfusion patterns or reduced cortical flow.160 The presence of hydronephrosis indicates obstruction (postrenal AKI). Newer techniques like contrast-enhanced ultrasound can identify cortical no-reflow zones (microvascular failure) and distinguish these from purely functional prerenal states.21 Thus, by integrating biomarker panels with imaging findings, clinicians can infer whether AKI is primarily driven by haemodynamic insufficiency, toxic tubular injury, inflammation, or outflow obstruction, enabling more targeted therapy, such as immunomodulation for immune-driven AKI versus volume optimisation for prerenal AKI.

Prognosis and risk stratification

To improve patient outcomes, clinicians need tools to stratify AKI severity and predict whether a patient will recover fully or progress to CKD. Traditional staging (based on creatinine/urine output) correlates incompletely with true injury depth or regenerative potential.161 Emerging biomarkers promise to refine risk stratification by reflecting both the acute injury magnitude and the quality of the repair process.62 Markers of severe tissue injury, such as circulating cell-free DNA released during extensive necrosis or high plasma neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) levels, indicate a large nephron injury burden and have been associated with worse short-term outcomes.162 Profound metabolic derangements (e.g., lactic acidosis, severe hyperkalaemia) can likewise signal an especially severe AKI that requires aggressive intervention.163

Equally important are biomarkers of repair and fibrogenesis that predict which patients are at risk for incomplete recovery and AKI-to-CKD progression. Currently, this is a major knowledge gap and focus of research.13 One promising finding is that the persistence of injury biomarkers beyond the acute phase portends poor outcomes.129 For example, urinary KIM-1 measured after AKI has been associated with subsequent CKD incidence or progression, although its prognostic value depends on urine concentration adjustment.164 Similarly, the emergence of fibrosis- or senescence-related signals in convalescence may identify patients on a trajectory towards CKD.165 Detection of SASP factors (such as IL-6 or matrix metalloproteinases) or profibrotic mediators (like TGF-β) in urine suggests that a senescence/fibrosis programme has been triggered.101 An incomplete return to normal function can also be picked up by subtle clinical markers, for instance, persistent low-level proteinuria (especially tubular proteinuria) or impaired concentrating ability after AKI, which indicate residual nephron damage.166 Incorporating these injury and repair biomarkers into prognostic scores could allow early initiation of secondary prevention (e.g., ACE inhibitors or novel anti-fibrotics) in patients likely to progress, while reassuring those with truly reversible AKI.

Clinical hierarchy and limitations of current biomarkers

For early warning, urinary [TIMP-2] x [IGFBP7] is most useful as a short-horizon stress marker in selected critically ill or perioperative patients; a meta-analysis reported pooled sensitivity of 0.79 (95% CI 0.72–0.84), specificity of 0.70 (95% CI 0.62–0.76), and an AUROC of 0.81 (95% CI 0.78–0.84), with performance varying by timing, severity, and setting.151 NGAL can rise within hours and has reported sensitivity of 0.65–0.89 and specificity of 0.60–0.85 (AUC 0.70–0.91) in ICU studies, but the systematic review did not provide a single pooled confidence interval, and inflammation, chronic kidney disease, age, and assay matrix reduce aetiological specificity.146 Urinary KIM-1 is more oriented towards proximal-tubule injury but has moderate diagnostic performance; pooled adult sensitivity was 0.73 (95% credible interval 0.67–0.78), specificity was 0.75 (0.70–0.80), and AUC was 0.81 (0.77–0.84).167 These diagnostic-accuracy meta-analyses report interval estimates rather than a single hypothesis-test P value, and none of the biomarkers should be interpreted as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Current biomarkers cannot independently assign AKI aetiology because they reflect cellular stress, injury compartment, or inflammatory context rather than a unique cause. Context-specific profiles can nevertheless refine a probabilistic classification. In sepsis, NGAL may rise early but is also released during systemic inflammation, limiting renal and causal specificity.146 After cardiac surgery, [TIMP-2] x [IGFBP7] provides useful short-horizon risk prediction but does not by itself establish an ischaemic mechanism.151 In contrast- or cisplatin-exposed patients, KIM-1 can strengthen evidence for proximal-tubule injury, but exposure history is still required to attribute the injury to a nephrotoxin.167 Biomarker panels should therefore support early warning, risk stratification, and hallmark endotyping, while aetiological assignment continues to integrate exposure history, haemodynamics, urine sediment, imaging, microbiology, and the clinical trajectory.

A practical hierarchy is therefore: risk prediction and stress ([TIMP-2] x [IGFBP7]); early structural injury (NGAL, KIM-1, and L-FABP); dynamic functional reserve (furosemide stress test); and prognosis or recovery (serial CCL14, KIM-1, proteinuria, and kidney-function trajectory). Sensitivity and specificity depend on sampling time and aetiology, and single thresholds perform inconsistently across sepsis, cardiac surgery, nephrotoxicity, and chronic kidney disease. Availability is also uneven: automated NGAL and NephroCheck assays may not be accessible around the clock, cost can impede serial testing, and pre-analytical and platform differences limit comparability. Biomarkers should therefore complement, not replace, clinical context, creatinine and urine output, urine sediment, and imaging.

Towards integrative classification

Looking ahead, the ultimate goal is to move beyond simplistic AKI definitions and develop an integrative classification that captures the multifaceted nature of the syndrome. Current clinical staging remains predominantly based on changes in serum creatinine and urine output, criteria that, while convenient, are blunt and insensitive to the underlying biology. We propose that future AKI classification should incorporate multiple dimensions: Aetiology (E), Hallmarks (H) and Phase of injury (P). This “E-H-P” framework would create a multi-dimensional AKI phenotype for each patient, replacing the one-size-fits-all approach.168 In practice, this means considering the precipitating cause (e.g., sepsis vs. ischaemia), identifying active hallmarks (e.g., dominant inflammation, cell death, etc.), measuring specific biomarker signatures or imaging findings to refine the subtype, and accounting for the time-phase (early injury vs. recovery phase).156 With advances in data science, clinicians could input a patient's clinical context, biomarker panel results, and imaging data into algorithms (potentially AI-driven) that output a refined classification and risk assessment. Embracing such integrative phenotyping will enable precision diagnostics where therapy can be tailored to the patient's AKI endotype. It also provides a platform for designing clinical trials that target specific biological processes, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting effective treatments in subpopulations. Disease-specific models and hallmarks mapping

A diverse range of animal models has been developed to study AKI, each recapitulating specific injury aetiologies and corresponding hallmarks of the syndrome.170 The ischaemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) model, which involves transient renal pedicle clamping, mimics clinical hypotension or transplant-related ischaemia and features acute tubular necrosis, microvascular dysfunction, and robust inflammation.171 Cisplatin-induced AKI models DNA damage and tubular apoptosis; at higher cumulative doses, it induces persistent cell-cycle arrest and senescence and can progress to fibrosis.172 Mechanistically, cisplatin exposure can accelerate tubular senescence programmes that couple maladaptive repair to progressive tissue remodelling.173 Sepsis-induced AKI, induced by caecal ligation and puncture or endotoxin challenge, emphasises systemic inflammation and organ crosstalk and typically shows less overt tubular necrosis.174 Additional aetiology-specific models include glycerol-induced rhabdomyolysis, characterised by oxidative stress, haem pigment toxicity, and cast nephropathy.175 Contrast-induced AKI highlights medullary hypoxia and direct tubular toxicity alongside oxidative stress and vasoregulatory disturbances.176 Large-animal models, including sheep cardiopulmonary bypass-associated AKI platforms, capture perioperative haemodynamic and inflammatory contributors with high translational relevance.177 Each model has distinct advantages and limitations: IRI is highly controlled but lacks the comorbid complexity of human disease, whereas sepsis models exhibit greater variability in severity yet better reflect clinical ICU scenarios.178

Using multiple models in tandem can better cover the spectrum of AKI hallmarks and strengthen confidence that a therapeutic target is broadly relevant across aetiologies.18 For instance, a cytoprotective strategy can be evaluated in both ischaemic and nephrotoxic settings to test whether benefit generalises beyond a single trigger.4 It is also instructive to note where models diverge: sepsis models often cause AKI dominated by inflammation and haemodynamic alterations with comparatively limited frank necrosis, whereas toxin models can produce prominent tubular cell death and injury signatures.179 Awareness of these differences guides appropriate model selection and supports development of complementary platforms (including large-animal systems) to bridge mechanistic insight to clinical translation. Ultimately, mapping hallmark expression across models enhances mechanistic clarity and improves the likelihood that preclinical findings will translate to human AKI.

In vitro and organoid platforms

In vitro systems and organoids provide highly controlled platforms for dissecting specific AKI mechanisms.180 Cultured primary renal tubular cells and immortalised cell lines enable interrogation of cell-autonomous responses to stressors such as hypoxia, nephrotoxins, and inflammatory cytokines, capturing hallmarks including tubular stress, regulated cell death, and, in some settings, senescence.50 These reductionist models are particularly valuable for tightly manipulating experimental conditions and attributing effects to defined perturbations, for example, inducing ferroptosis through glutathione depletion or testing whether antioxidants mitigate hypoxia-induced tubular injury.181 However, conventional two-dimensional cultures lack the vascular, stromal, and immune components that shape AKI pathophysiology in vivo.182

Human pluripotent stem cell-derived kidney organoids, which contain rudimentary nephron-like structures including glomerular and tubular compartments, provide a more physiologically relevant context than conventional monolayer culture.183 Organoids can reproduce injury responses, for example, upregulating KIM-1 after cisplatin exposure, and have been used to interrogate intrinsic repair programmes, with the added advantages of human genetics and suitability for scalable drug screening.184 Microfluidic kidney-on-a-chip platforms extend this concept by introducing fluid flow and shear stress, thereby recreating dynamic microenvironments relevant to filtration and reabsorption.185 These systems enable interrogation of inter-compartmental signalling, such as tubule-endothelial crosstalk or drug-induced barrier dysfunction, in a small-scale and tightly controlled setting.186 By enabling manipulation of individual variables, organoids and chips excel at isolating specific hallmarks under defined conditions, for example, testing how a single genetic perturbation shapes tubular cell death or stress responses, and they complement in vivo models by supporting rapid mechanistic dissection and therapy screening.187 Together, these platforms bridge the gap between reductionist cell culture and whole-animal studies, offering insights into human-relevant AKI pathways.

Advanced tools for spatiotemporal analysis and manipulation

AKI research increasingly leverages cutting-edge tools to visualise, quantify, and manipulate hallmark processes in vivo at high resolution.188 Spatial and temporal approaches enable investigators to track injury as it evolves and to map molecular changes in situ.188 Intravital two-photon microscopy, for example, allows real-time imaging of living kidneys in animal models, capturing peritubular capillary blood flow to reveal microvascular dysfunction and monitoring glomerular filtration dynamics to assess nephron function.189 Such live imaging can directly demonstrate a hallmark, such as reduced red blood cell flux in capillaries during ischaemia, and document recovery following intervention.190 In parallel, functional imaging probes and targeted contrast agents have been developed to report hallmark activities, including caspase-sensitive probes for apoptosis, ROS-responsive fluorescent markers for oxidative stress, and collagen-targeted tracers for fibrogenesis.191 Spatial transcriptomics and multiplex tissue immunostaining further enable localisation of injury programmes within the renal microenvironment, for instance, identifying immune-cell niches enriched for neutrophils and macrophages or delineating focal regions of fibrosis and capillary rarefaction adjacent to surviving tubules.192 Together, these technologies generate a spatiotemporal atlas of AKI, revealing how hallmarks emerge, co-localise, and interact across kidney compartments over time.

In parallel, sophisticated genetic and computational approaches are enabling researchers to establish causality and uncover therapeutic targets.193 Lineage-tracing studies in transgenic mice, in which defined cell populations are indelibly labelled, have shown that tubular regeneration after injury largely arises from the proliferation of surviving tubular epithelial cells rather than from recruitment of exogenous stem cells, helping resolve a long-standing debate about the cellular source of adaptive repair.194 Conditional gene knockout or overexpression models further permit cell type-specific perturbation of hallmark pathways; for example, selectively deleting a ferroptosis-regulatory gene in proximal tubules can directly test whether suppressing ferroptosis attenuates AKI severity.195 Such interventions distinguish hallmarks that are merely associated with injury from those that are mechanistic drivers.

On the computational side, single-cell RNA sequencing and other multi-omics platforms have revealed previously unrecognised injury-associated cell states, including proinflammatory proximal tubule programmes and distinct infiltrating macrophage subsets, thereby refining the cellular taxonomy of AKI and suggesting additional hallmarks or sub-hallmarks.196 Machine-learning analyses of large-scale omics datasets or electronic health records can likewise identify composite biomarker patterns that predict outcomes or infer hallmark activation.197 By integrating high-dimensional data, these approaches can generate predictive models, for example, algorithms that forecast maladaptive fibrosis based on early molecular signatures, thereby linking experimental mechanisms to clinically actionable risk stratification.198 Collectively, these tools provide unprecedented precision for observing and manipulating AKI biology, accelerating translation from mechanistic insight to therapeutic hypothesis.

Limitations and future directions

Despite extensive modelling efforts, current experimental systems have important limitations that must be addressed to better approximate clinical reality.199 Most animal models impose an acute, relatively homogeneous insult in young, otherwise healthy subjects, and therefore incompletely capture the heterogeneity of human AKI, particularly chronic sequelae and the modifying effects of comorbidities such as ageing, diabetes, and heart failure.200 For example, rodents often recover after a single ischaemic episode, whereas older patients may progress to CKD.13 In addition, models that reflect subclinical AKI or the cumulative impact of repetitive, low-grade injuries remain scarce, despite the clinical relevance of “multiple-hit” exposures in hospitalised patients.169 Addressing these gaps will likely require broader adoption of aged and comorbidity-bearing animals, two-hit or multi-hit paradigms spaced over weeks to model AKI-on-CKD or recurrent insults, and longer-term follow-up after acute injury to capture AKI-to-CKD trajectories.201,202 On the in vitro side, increasingly complex co-culture and hybrid organoid systems, such as integrating renal organoids with vascular organoids and/or immune cells, are being developed to incorporate multicellular interactions and model organ–organ crosstalk, including pathways relevant to the gut–kidney axis and cardiorenal syndromes, within a controlled experimental setting.203, 204, 205

Moving forward, researchers should explicitly define which core hallmarks each model is best suited to interrogate to improve conceptual clarity and translational relevance. Because no single system captures the full spectrum of human AKI, a multi-model strategy is often the most informative, with key hypotheses validated across complementary platforms, for example, confirming an anti-fibrotic intervention in both ischaemic and nephrotoxic settings and, where feasible, in aged or comorbidity-bearing animals.169 Adopting more complex models will inevitably increase cost and experimental variability, but these trade-offs are essential for better approximating clinical heterogeneity.206

Future progress will also be accelerated by tighter integration with computational approaches, including in silico nephron-network simulations and “virtual trial” frameworks, to forecast how mechanistic interventions might perform in humans and to guide prioritisation of candidates for translation.207,208 Through iterative refinement of experimental models and incorporation of human-relevant conditions, the field can strengthen the translational pipeline and increase the likelihood that therapies emerging from preclinical work will succeed in the diverse patient populations in which AKI occurs.209

Therapeutic roadmap

Current AKI management remains largely supportive, focussing on early recognition and reversal of precipitating factors, such as treating infection, relieving obstruction, and discontinuing or removing nephrotoxins, alongside optimisation of haemodynamics and volume status.210 Preventing hypotension and correcting outflow obstruction are central to limiting ongoing ischaemic or pressure-mediated injury.211 Renal replacement therapy can be life-saving in severe AKI with urgent complications, including refractory hyperkalaemia or pulmonary oedema, but it functions primarily as a bridge and does not directly modify the underlying renal pathophysiology.212 A hallmark-guided framework underscores opportunities for mechanism-specific intervention.213 Although no pharmacological agent is currently approved for treating established AKI, multiple targeted strategies, spanning anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic therapies as well as metabolic modulators, are progressing through preclinical and early clinical evaluation and may enable more precise, subtype- and phase-aligned treatment paradigms in the foreseeable future.214

Therapeutic evidence should be read in tiers. Established care includes cause reversal, haemodynamic and fluid optimisation, avoidance or dose adjustment of nephrotoxins, treatment of complications, and kidney replacement therapy when indicated. Biomarker-guided KDIGO care bundles have clinical evidence in selected high-risk settings but do not constitute a molecularly targeted drug. Cytokine or complement blockade, ferroptosis and necroptosis inhibitors, mitochondria-targeted compounds, NAD+ augmentation, senolytics, and antifibrotics are investigational for AKI; most efficacy data derive from cells or animal models, and none is standard care for established AKI. Recent P2X7 and SIRT5 inhibitors improved septic AKI in mouse models,215, 216, 217 illustrating mechanistic promise rather than clinical efficacy. Lactate metabolism and lactylation are likewise emerging targets but currently remain conceptual or preclinical.26

Successful AKI therapy will ultimately depend on delivering the right intervention at the right time against the right target.7 This principle acknowledges that different pathobiological processes dominate at different stages of AKI, and therapeutic windows for intervention are finite.4 Intervening too late or at the wrong process may yield no benefit or even harm.218,219 Therefore, a hallmark-guided, phase-specific therapeutic roadmap is shown in Fig. 4:1. Early phase (0–24 h after recognised injury) - Prevention window. Established management is rapid identification and reversal of the cause: treat infection, restore appropriate perfusion, relieve obstruction, stop or adjust nephrotoxins, avoid fluid overload, and apply a KDIGO-based supportive bundle in high-risk patients. Biomarkers may identify patients who warrant closer monitoring, but they do not by themselves justify corticosteroids, cytokine-neutralising antibodies, endothelial agents, SS-31, or NAD+-augmenting therapy. These mechanism-directed interventions remain investigational and require aetiology- and biomarker-enriched trials.

2. Established injury phase (1–7 days) - Damage-control window. Established care remains management of haemodynamics, fluid balance, electrolytes, acid-base status, drug dosing, nutrition, and conventional indications for kidney replacement therapy. The rationale for ferroptosis inhibition is strongest in preclinical ischaemia-reperfusion, cisplatin, rhabdomyolysis, and other haemoglobin- or haem-rich models. Septic AKI is more heterogeneous, and inflammation, microvascular dysfunction, pyroptosis, apoptosis, or metabolic failure may dominate; a ferroptosis inhibitor cannot therefore be assumed to have equal efficacy across aetiologies. Ferroptosis, necroptosis, cytokine, and antioxidant strategies have no established clinical efficacy for AKI and should be tested only in mechanism-matched trials.

3. Recovery phase (>7 days) - Outcome-determination window. Established practice is structured reassessment of kidney function, proteinuria, blood pressure, medication safety, volume status, and cardiovascular risk, with nephrology follow-up for high-risk or incompletely recovered patients. Persistent injury, senescence, or fibrotic signals may identify candidates for trials, but dasatinib plus quercetin, other senolytics, pro-angiogenic agents, pirfenidone, halofuginone, and cytokine-directed therapies are not standard post-AKI care. If senolytics are studied, the biologically defensible window is after acute cell death has subsided and persistent senescence has been demonstrated, not during the early protective arrest response. The optimal timing remains unknown in humans.

Fig. 4 Therapeutic roadmap of AKI. Phase-adapted therapeutic roadmap for AKI across early prevention (0–24 h), established injury or damage control (1–7 d), and recovery or outcome determination (>7 d). Established clinical management comprises cause reversal, haemodynamic and fluid optimisation, avoidance of nephrotoxins, management of complications, and kidney replacement therapy when indicated. Mechanism-directed interventions against inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, regulated cell death, senescence, or fibrosis are investigational and should be matched to aetiology, hallmark profile, and phase in clinical trials.

Advancing AKI therapy will also require reimagining clinical trial design and addressing implementation challenges.220 Many prior AKI trials have likely failed, at least in part, because patient heterogeneity was not accounted for, with interventions tested in broad, unstratified populations that diluted subtype-specific effects.220 To overcome this barrier, future studies should incorporate integrative subtyping and adaptive trial methodologies:1. Enrichment strategies: Enrol patients based on biomarker-defined risk or subtype. For example, a trial of an IL-11 inhibitor could preferentially recruit patients with a high-inflammatory signature rather than an unselected, all-comer population.221 Such enrichment increases the likelihood of detecting benefit when a therapy truly targets a subgroup's dominant pathology.220

2. Stratified and adaptive trial design: Patients can be stratified by baseline hallmark profile, for instance, by the presence or absence of strong cell-death markers, to ensure balanced randomisation and enable prespecified subgroup analyses. Adaptive designs can incorporate interim biomarker readouts to modify trial conduct, such as discontinuing therapy in participants who show no early biomarker response or reallocating enrolment towards molecularly defined responders.220 As biology evolves over time, protocols could also allow treatment adaptation, for example, de-escalating immunomodulation when inflammation resolves while introducing anti-fibrotic strategies as remodelling signatures rise.

3. Composite endpoints: Conventional endpoints, such as short-term creatinine changes or dialysis requirement, may not capture meaningful biological effects. Trials should incorporate composite endpoints aligned with hallmarks, including early reductions in injury biomarkers (e.g., NGAL or KIM-1 at 24–48 h), days alive and dialysis-free, and longer-term kidney function trajectories over 6–12 months to reflect AKI-to-CKD transition risk.220

4. Pragmatic and implementation considerations: Real-time stratification requires rapid, widely accessible point-of-care assays for key biomarkers.222 Successful execution will also depend on multidisciplinary trial teams spanning nephrology, critical care, pharmacology, and data science. In parallel, regulatory frameworks may need to evolve to accept validated biomarker responses as evidence of target engagement and clinically meaningful benefit, which will require consensus-building and prospective validation.

Despite these challenges, hallmark-driven, patient-tailored trial strategies are likely essential to move beyond repeated neutral results. Collectively, an AKI therapeutic roadmap should match interventions to the dominant biology and disease phase in each patient.11 Ultimately, a hallmark-based approach seeks to deliver the right therapy at the right time to the right patient, improving acute outcomes while reducing the longer-term risk of progression from AKI to CKD.

Conclusion and perspective

AKI is a heterogeneous syndrome in which similar creatinine and urine-output changes can arise from different biological processes. The E-H-P framework adds a mechanistic layer to, rather than replaces, conventional staging by recording the aetiological trigger, the dominant measurable hallmarks, and the phase of injury or repair. Its immediate clinical value is to structure assessment, communicate uncertainty, and align supportive priorities; its future value depends on prospective validation for prognostic enrichment and mechanism-matched trials. The framework also makes evidence boundaries explicit: supportive and cause-directed management are established, whereas most hallmark-directed drugs remain investigational.

A practical implementation pathway would begin with a rapid phenotyping bundle at diagnosis, integrating clinical context, creatinine and urine output, urine sediment, selected stress or injury biomarkers, and bedside imaging to assign provisional E and H dimensions. Serial reassessment over 24–72 h would update the profile as haemodynamics, inflammation, injury markers, and organ support change. This classification should guide supportive priorities and eligibility for mechanism-matched clinical trials, not routine off-label use of senolytics, ferroptosis inhibitors, cytokine blockade, or antifibrotics. During the AKD and post-AKI periods, kidney function, albuminuria, blood pressure, medication safety, and cardiovascular risk should be followed to detect incomplete recovery and support evidence-based secondary prevention.

The significance of the proposed framework extends beyond AKI. It reflects nephrology's broader transition from defining disease primarily by organ-level function to a paradigm grounded in molecular and systems medicine. An integrated E-H-P approach could provide a generalisable blueprint for precision phenotyping across other renal disorders, including heterogeneous forms of CKD and glomerular diseases, where grouping patients by creatinine metrics or histopathology alone often fails to capture actionable biological differences.

In the foreseeable future, AKI need not remain a vague, monolithic complication but can be reframed as a set of definable and potentially manageable sub-diseases. As biomarkers and classification strategies mature, AKI may be detected earlier, including at preclinical stages, treated with interventions matched to its dominant drivers, and monitored longitudinally to ensure true recovery rather than incomplete resolution. The hallmarks framework and the E-H-P integrative classification outlined here represent key building blocks for this emerging map of AKI. With continued mechanistic discovery and clinical innovation, AKI can evolve from a largely supportive-care syndrome into a model for phase- and mechanism-guided management, improving acute outcomes while reducing the long-term burden of CKD that often follows.
