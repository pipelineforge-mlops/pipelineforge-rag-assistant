Background

Medication errors are a leading preventable cause of harm in hospitalized children. The pediatric population is particularly vulnerable due to weight-based dosing requirements, developmental pharmacokinetic variability, and limited capacity for self-advocacy.

Objectives

To map and synthesize the available evidence on factors associated with the occurrence of medication administration errors in hospitalized pediatric patients.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies addressing administration phase medication errors (per the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention, NCC MERP) in hospitalized children aged 0 to 18 years, published in English, Spanish, or Portuguese, using quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods designs.

Sources of Evidence

Nine electronic databases were searched from inception to July 2026 using English, Spanish, and Portuguese (DeCS) terms, supplemented by hand searching of reference lists.

Charting Methods

The review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology; data were charted with the JBI standardized instrument and synthesized narratively with thematic analysis.

Results

Of 1,012 records, 25 studies met the inclusion criteria. Incorrect dosing was the most prevalent error type (18 studies, 72%). Neonates and infants were the most vulnerable group, and PICU and NICU the highest-risk settings, although a direct observation study reported that 37% of administrations on general wards involved an error. Human factors, particularly knowledge deficits, were the most frequently reported contributors (13 studies, 52%). Five complementary mitigation strategies were identified: professional education, dual verification, unified non-punitive reporting, technology, and interprofessional communication.

Conclusions

Medication administration errors in pediatric inpatients are driven by a multilayered interplay of human, environmental, communicative, and medication related factors. Effective prevention demands integrated, systems-based approaches combining education, technology, standardized protocols, and a non-punitive safety culture.

Plain language summary

Medication errors are among the most common causes of preventable harm in hospitalized children. Children are especially at risk because their medication doses must be individually calculated based on body weight, and young children cannot speak up if something seems wrong with their treatment.

In this review, we examined 25 published studies to understand what types of medication errors happen most often when healthcare professionals administer medications to hospitalized children, what factors contribute to these errors, and what strategies can help prevent them.

We found that giving the wrong dose is the most common error, reported in nearly three quarters of all studies. Newborns, infants, and young children are most affected, and intensive care units have the highest error rates. The main factors that contribute to errors include gaps in knowledge about pediatric medications, interruptions during medication preparation, poor communication between healthcare team members, and problems with drug labeling or equipment.

To reduce these errors, studies recommend a combination of approaches: ongoing education for healthcare providers about pediatric medications, having two professionals independently verify high risk medications before administration, creating non-punitive error reporting systems, using technology such as barcode scanning and computerized ordering, and improving communication within healthcare teams.

These findings highlight that preventing medication errors in children requires a system wide approach rather than focusing on individual mistakes. Hospitals and policymakers can use these results to design targeted safety programs that protect hospitalized children from preventable medication harm.

Introduction

Medication errors constitute one of the most significant threats to patient safety worldwide. The World Health Organization launched its Third Global Patient Safety Challenge, “Medication Without Harm,” aiming to reduce severe avoidable medication related harm by 50% over 5 years. 1 In the United States, medication errors have long been identified as one of the leading causes of preventable harm in healthcare systems. 2

Within the broader landscape of medication safety, the pediatric population occupies a uniquely vulnerable position. Unlike adults, children require individualized weight-based dosing calculations, exhibit age-dependent pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic variability, and possess limited physiological reserves to compensate for drug related adverse events. 3 Furthermore, young children cannot self-advocate or identify potential errors in their own care. In this regard, the characterization of the pharmacotherapeutic profile in pediatric populations constitutes an essential tool for anticipating and preventing medication related risks. 4

The medication use process encompasses multiple sequential stages: prescribing, transcribing, dispensing, preparing, administering, and monitoring. Each stage carries inherent risks for error, with administration representing one of the most critical phases because it constitutes the final opportunity to intercept an error before it reaches the patient.5,6 A medication administration error (MAE) is defined, according to the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCC MERP), as any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm while the medication is in the control of the healthcare professional during the administration phase. 7 This operational definition, encompassing errors of omission, wrong dose, wrong route, wrong time, wrong drug, and unauthorized drug administration was adopted as the inclusion criterion for this review.

Several systematic reviews have examined medication errors in pediatric and neonatal intensive care settings,8,9 general pediatric populations,10,11 and specific error types such as dosing errors. 12 Recent meta-analytic evidence has confirmed that the pooled prevalence of medication errors in hospitalized pediatric patients remains alarmingly high across diverse healthcare settings, 13 while reviews have proposed integrated quality improvement frameworks encompassing technological, educational, and organizational interventions. 14 A scoping review on nurses’ and midwives’ perceptions further highlighted the role of workload, communication gaps, and system design in the perpetuation of these errors. 15 However, these reviews have predominantly focused on prescribing errors or have examined medication errors across all stages without specifically isolating the administration phase. Moreover, the evidence on computer tools for pharmacovigilance monitoring underscores the importance of technological integration in reducing errors across the medication use process. 16 The factors that contribute to errors specifically during medication administration in hospitalized pediatric patients remain inadequately mapped.

The purpose of this scoping review was to map and synthesize the available literature on the factors associated with the occurrence of MAEs in hospitalized pediatric patients. By adopting a scoping review methodology, this study aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of the breadth of evidence, identify key themes and knowledge gaps, and inform future research priorities and clinical interventions targeting this critical patient safety concern.

Methods

Design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews 17 and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). 18 In line with EQUATOR network recommendations, the reporting of this scoping review conforms to the PRISMA-ScR statement, 18 and the completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided as Supplemental File S1. The review protocol was not prospectively registered in a formal registry; however, a structured protocol was developed a priori and adhered to throughout the review process, consistent with JBI guidelines.

Review question and PCC framework

The review question was structured using the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) framework: Population: hospitalized pediatric patients (aged 0 to 18 years); Concept: factors associated with medication administration errors; Context: hospital settings including pediatric wards, intensive care units, and emergency departments.

Search strategy

A three-step search strategy was employed. An initial limited search of PubMed and CINAHL identified relevant articles and index terms. A second search using all identified keywords and MeSH terms was then conducted across nine electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, LILACS, SciELO, Embase, and PsycINFO. The third step involved hand searching the reference lists of included studies. The search combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free text keywords using Boolean operators. The core search string included: (“medication errors” OR “drug administration” OR “medication safety”) AND (“pediatrics” OR “child” OR “infant” OR “neonatal”) AND (“hospital” OR “inpatient”). For the Spanish and Portuguese language databases (LILACS and SciELO), the search terms were translated using the corresponding Health Sciences Descriptors (DeCS,BIREME) equivalents (for example, “errores de medicación”, “erros de medicação”, “seguridad del paciente”, “pediatría”) combined with free text terms, to ensure capture of the non-English regional literature. The complete, database specific search strings, including all DeCS/MeSH terms and Boolean structure, are provided as Supplemental File S2 to support reproducibility. No date restrictions were applied; the initial search was executed in December 2023, and an updated search was run in July 2026.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they: (a) addressed medication errors specifically related to the administration phase in hospitalized children aged 0 to 18 years; (b) were published in English, Spanish, or Portuguese; and (c) employed quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods designs. Consistent with the NCC MERP definition, 7 administration phase errors were operationally defined as errors of omission, wrong dose, wrong route, wrong time, wrong drug, or unauthorized drug administration occurring during the medication delivery process from preparation to patient receipt.

Exclusion criteria comprised: studies focused exclusively on prescribing, transcribing, or dispensing errors without reporting administration phase data; outpatient or community settings; case reports; editorials; and conference abstracts.

Study selection

All identified records were exported to Rayyan, 19 a web-based platform for systematic review screening. The database search retrieved 999 records and hand searching of reference lists identified 13 further records (1,012 in total). After removing 129 duplicates, , 883 records were screened by title and abstract by two independent reviewers, of which 798 were excluded. Full text assessment was conducted on 85 articles, of which 62 were excluded: language restrictions (n = 4), focus on prescribing errors only (n = 22), outpatient setting (n = 15), non-original research (n = 12), and insufficient administration phase data (n = 9). A total of 23 studies met all inclusion criteria in the initial search. An updated search applying the same strategy across the nine databases in July 2026 identified two additional eligible studies,20,21 yielding a final total of 25 included studies (Figure 1).Figure 1. PRISMA-ScR flow diagram of study selection process.

Critical appraisal

Although critical appraisal is not mandatory in scoping reviews, JBI critical appraisal tools 22 were applied to assess methodological quality. Two reviewers independently appraised each study using the appropriate JBI checklist for its design. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Overall quality was moderate to high: 20 of 25 studies (80%) met at least 70% of applicable quality criteria. Common limitations included insufficient description of confounding variables in observational studies and lack of blinding in cross-sectional designs. No studies were excluded based on quality, consistent with scoping review methodology.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted using the JBI standardized data extraction instrument adapted to the review objectives, including author(s), year, country, study design, sample size, setting, age group(s), error types, contributing factors, and mitigation strategies. Given the heterogeneity of study designs and outcomes, a narrative synthesis approach was employed with thematic analysis of contributing factors and mitigation strategies.

Results

Search results and study selection

The systematic search identified 999 records across the nine databases. An additional 13 records were identified through hand searching reference lists. After removing 129 duplicates, 883 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 798 were excluded. Full text review of 85 articles resulted in the exclusion of four articles for language restrictions and 58 for other reasons (prescribing errors only, n = 22; outpatient setting, n = 15; non original research, n = 12; insufficient administration data, n = 9). Twenty-three studies met all inclusion criteria in the initial search. The updated search conducted in July 2026 identified two further eligible studies, giving a total of 25 included studies in the final synthesis. The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram is presented in Figure 1.

Medication administration errors by age group

Seven studies reported age-stratified data and two further studies provided supporting evidence on age related risk. Neonates and infants (0 to 12 months) were the most vulnerable, with the highest error rates attributed to complex weight-based calculations and narrow therapeutic indices.25,29,34 Preschool aged children (1 to 5 years) constituted the second most affected group, associated with formulation challenges and frequent dose manipulation.28,30,33 Two studies identified adolescents as a secondary risk group, potentially reflecting increased polypharmacy and transition of care complexities.26,38 Consistent with this, a large prospective direct observational study found that children aged ≥ 11 years were at significantly higher risk of administration errors than younger children, underscoring that older pediatric patients should not be overlooked when designing safety interventions. 21

Importantly, the nature of medication administration risk differs across developmental stages, and not only its magnitude. In neonates and infants, errors are dominated by dose calculation and preparation problems arising from weight-based and gestational-age-based dosing and from care that is entirely caregiver and clinician dependent. In older children and adolescents, error patterns increasingly reflect polypharmacy, the transition from weight-based to fixed adult dosing, and the growing (though still limited) autonomy of the patient, who may either contribute to error interception or, conversely, be assumed to require less verification. Recognizing these age-specific error profiles is a prerequisite for tailoring preventive strategies rather than applying uniform safeguards across the whole 0-to-18-year range.

The age-dependent vulnerability pattern reflects pharmacological principles: neonatal pharmacokinetics are characterized by immature hepatic enzyme systems (CYP3A4, CYP2D6), higher total body water proportions altering drug volumes of distribution, and reduced plasma protein binding, all of which narrow the margin of dosing safety and amplify the consequences of calculation errors. 3

Infants and preschool aged children present additional challenges related to the limited availability of age-appropriate formulations, often requiring extemporaneous compounding or dilution of adult strength preparations, each introducing further opportunities for concentration and volume errors.28,33

The interrelationship between these four categories of contributing factors merits emphasis. Human factors rarely operate in isolation; rather, knowledge deficits are exacerbated by environmental pressures (high workload, interruptions), which in turn are compounded by communication failures that prevent error interception. This cascading pattern is consistent with Reason’s Swiss Cheese Model, 43 wherein errors reach patients when multiple defensive barriers fail simultaneously. For example, a nurse with insufficient training in neonatal dose calculations (human factor) working during a high-census shift with frequent interruptions (environmental factor) may prepare an incorrect dose that goes undetected due to incomplete handoff communication (communication failure), ultimately resulting in a medication administration error.

Clinical settings

Nineteen studies reported clinical setting data. PICUs (11 studies) and NICUs (9 studies) were the highest-risk environments, where high-acuity patients, complex regimens, polypharmacy, and frequent intravenous medication use converge.25,29,32,33 General pediatric wards were referenced in eight studies, emergency departments in five, and surgical units in three. It is important to distinguish the neonate as a patient population from the NICU as a care environment: although the two overlap heavily, the elevated risk observed in neonatal care reflects both patient intrinsic factors (physiological immaturity and weight-based dosing) and setting intrinsic factors (high acuity, workload and intravenous therapy). Moreover, high error rates are not confined to intensive care; a recent direct observation study reported that 37% of administrations on general pediatric wards involved an error, of which one quarter were potentially serious. 21

Within PICUs, the error rate was consistently higher for intravenous medications compared to oral medications, reflecting the technical complexity of infusion preparation including reconstitution, dilution, and rate programming. Three studies specifically reported that medications requiring multi-step preparation (such as continuous infusions of vasoactive agents) had error rates two to three times higher than single-step administrations.25,32,33 In NICUs, the combination of extremely low-birth-weight patients, the need for dose calculations based on gestational age as well as weight, and the frequent use of medications at concentrations requiring further dilution created a uniquely high-risk environment. Emergency departments presented distinct challenges related to time pressure, unfamiliar patients, and the need for rapid weight estimation in acutely ill children. 38

Discussion

This scoping review mapped the extent and nature of evidence on factors associated with MAEs in hospitalized pediatric patients. The synthesis of 25 studies reveals a complex, multilayered landscape of interconnected factors that can be understood through the lens of Reason’s Swiss Cheese Model of organizational accidents 43 (Figure 3), wherein errors reach patients only when multiple system barriers simultaneously fail.Figure 3. Conceptual representation of the factors associated with medication administration errors in hospitalized children, based on Reason’s Swiss Cheese Model. 43 Each successive defensive layer (barrier) corresponds to one of the five mitigation strategies identified in this review (professional education, dual verification, unified non-punitive reporting, technology, and interprofessional communication), and the holes represent the four domains of contributing factors (human, environmental and workplace, communication, and medication and equipment). When the gaps align across layers, an error arising from the intrinsic vulnerability of the pediatric patient passes through and reaches the child (patient harm); when an intact defensive barrier blocks the trajectory, the error is intercepted and administration is safe.

Incorrect dosing: The cardinal error

The finding that incorrect dosing was the most prevalent error, reported in 72% of included studies, is consistent with the broader pediatric medication safety literature.12,13 This predominance reflects the inherent complexity of pediatric dosing, where calculations must account for weight, body surface area, gestational age, organ maturity, and condition specific variables. Through Reason’s framework, 43 dosing errors represent a convergence of latent conditions (absence of standardized weight-based protocols) and active failures (individual calculation mistakes). The clinical significance is amplified by the narrow therapeutic windows of many pediatric medications, where even small deviations can produce toxicity or therapeutic failure. Unlike adult dosing, where standard doses are often available, virtually every pediatric dose requires an individualized calculation, introducing an error opportunity at each step. Clinical decision support with standardized dose range checking has been associated with substantial reductions in medication errors; however, the frequently cited figures (for example, reductions of approximately 48 to 81% 14 ) largely reflect prescribing and ordering stage errors rather than administration phase errors specifically. Because the present review deliberately excluded prescribing errors, these estimates should be interpreted as upstream effects that may indirectly reduce downstream administration errors (for instance, by eliminating illegible or out of range orders before they reach the bedside) rather than as direct reductions in administration phase MAEs.14,29

The magnitude of the dosing error problem in pediatric settings is further contextualized by recent meta-analytic data. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that medication error rates in paediatric inpatient settings vary substantially by ward type, with the highest estimates reported in intensive care units and emergency departments, suggesting that current safeguards remain insufficient. 10 The mathematical complexity inherent in pediatric dosing requiring sequential calculations of weight-based dose, volume of reconstituted solution, and infusion rate creates a multi-step process where each calculation represents an independent error opportunity. Consistent with this, nursing experience and educational level have been shown to independently predict administration error rates, 37 highlighting the limitations of relying solely on human cognition for safety critical mathematical tasks.

Tartaglia et al. 14 proposed a framework for reducing pediatric medication errors that integrates standardization of drug concentrations, implementation of clinical decision support systems with real time dose range checking, and adoption of unit dose dispensing systems. The convergence of their recommendations with the findings of this scoping review suggests a robust evidence base for multi-component interventions targeting the dosing error pathway. Nevertheless, technology is not a panacea: a recent longitudinal pediatric study found that technology related errors persisted four years after CPOE implementation, cautioning against overstating the protective effect of digital tools and emphasizing the need for continuous optimization. 44 Consistent with a genuine administration phase benefit, a direct observational study reported that electronic medication management systems were associated with 45% lower actual harm from administration errors compared with paper charts, although in the same study eMM use was itself associated with a higher overall MAE rate and with errors more likely to be rated potentially serious. 21

Heterogeneity of error detection methods

A methodological consideration that materially affects the interpretation of the pooled error frequencies mapped above is the heterogeneity of error detection methods across the included studies. Direct observation studies systematically detect substantially more administration errors than chart review or voluntary incident reporting, which capture only a fraction of events. 6 This gap was demonstrated starkly in a pediatric multicenter study in which none of 2,883 clinically observed administration errors, including 40 associated with actual patient harm, had a corresponding incident report. 45 Consequently, the error types and frequencies synthesized in this review are shaped as much by how errors were ascertained as by their true underlying incidence: observation-based studies tend to surface preparation, timing and route errors that are largely invisible to reporting systems, whereas voluntary reporting studies over represent salient events such as wrong dose or wrong drug errors. The aggregated frequencies presented here should therefore be read as a map of what the current evidence base captures, not as unbiased prevalence estimates. This ascertainment heterogeneity is intrinsic to the source literature and reinforces the value of standardized, observation informed surveillance for future pediatric medication safety research.

Age-related vulnerability

The identification of neonates, infants, and preschool aged children as the most vulnerable populations aligns with pharmacological principles. Neonatal pharmacokinetics are characterized by immature hepatic metabolism (reduced CYP450 activity), variable renal clearance, higher body water to fat ratios, and lower plasma protein binding, all factors that demand precise dosing to avoid toxicity. 3 The additional finding that adolescents represent a secondary risk group may reflect polypharmacy associated with chronic conditions, the complexities of weight-based to standard dose transitions, and potential reduced adherence to verification protocols when patients approach adult size.26,38 This age-stratified vulnerability pattern suggests that mitigation strategies should be tailored: intensive verification protocols for neonates and infants, and specific transitional care protocols for adolescents.

The clinical implications of age-related vulnerability are substantial. For neonatal populations, the combination of pharmacokinetic immaturity, narrow therapeutic indices, and the predominant use of intravenous medications creates a risk profile that demands the highest level of medication safety vigilance. The implementation of standardized neonatal drug concentration protocols, pre calculated dosing charts, and mandatory pharmacist verification of all neonatal medication orders represents a minimum standard of care supported by the evidence synthesized in this review. For the adolescent subgroup, the development of specific protocols addressing the weight-based to standard dose transition, medication reconciliation during care transfers, and attention to polypharmacy management is warranted.

The human factor: Knowledge deficits as a modifiable determinant

The predominance of human factors, particularly knowledge deficits, as the most frequently reported contributors (52% of studies) highlights a critical but modifiable determinant. Sears et al. 37 provided important nuance, demonstrating that both nursing experience and educational level independently predicted error rates, suggesting that targeted competency programs could meaningfully reduce error incidence. Critically, knowledge deficits should not be interpreted as purely individual failures but rather as system-level indicators of inadequate training infrastructure, insufficient continuing education requirements, and lack of standardized competency assessment in pediatric medication administration. The consistent association across diverse healthcare contexts suggests a universal gap in pediatric pharmacotherapy education that demands curricular reform in nursing and medical training programs, with specific emphasis on weight-based dose calculation proficiency and high-alert medication management.

Intensive care as a high-risk nexus

The identification of PICU and NICU as the highest-risk settings is attributable to the convergence of multiple risk amplifiers: critically ill patients requiring complex, multi-drug regimens; predominance of intravenous medication administration with precise rate and concentration requirements; frequent use of off-label and unlicensed medications; high patient acuity demanding rapid clinical decisions; and elevated staff workload. This concentration of risk factors creates what may be characterized as a “perfect storm” for medication administration errors. The error rates reported in intensive care settings (up to 3-fold higher than general wards 29 ) underscore the need for targeted interventions including dedicated medication safety nurses, mandatory dual verification for all high-alert medications, and implementation of smart infusion pump technology with dose error reduction software.

A central interpretive caution, raised implicitly by the concentration of risk in intensive care, is that the elevated error rate in neonatal and pediatric critical care cannot be attributed unambiguously to any single cause. It may be driven by patient intrinsic factors (neonatal pharmacokinetic immaturity and complex weight-based dosing) or by setting intrinsic factors (high acuity, heavy workload, and the predominance of multi-step intravenous therapy), variables that are deeply confounded in the primary studies and cannot be separated with the available data. This distinction is not merely academic: if the environment predominates, structural interventions (safe staffing ratios, interruption free zones, smart pumps) should take priority, whereas if patient physiology predominates, dosing standardization and mandatory pharmacist verification should take priority. In practice both mechanisms almost certainly operate simultaneously, and comprehensive programs should address both.

The disproportionate risk in intensive care settings also reflects organizational factors. The 24-hour nature of critical care, with multiple nursing shifts and frequent handoffs, creates information discontinuity that may contribute to medication administration errors. Studies in this review documented that shift changes and incomplete handoff documentation were associated with increased error rates.36,41 The implementation of standardized medication reconciliation at each handoff, combined with electronic medication administration records that provide real time visibility of pending and completed doses, represents a systems-based approach to mitigating this risk.

Environmental and communication factors: System-level determinants

The contribution of environmental factors, particularly interruptions during medication preparation and administration, emerged as a significant system-level determinant. Westbrook et al. 40 demonstrated a dose-response relationship, with each interruption increasing clinical error risk by 12.7%. Zhao et al. 42 quantified an average of 6.3 interruptions per hour during medication rounds. These findings have direct implications for practice: designated interruption free zones during medication preparation and administration, standardized “do not disturb” protocols, and redesign of medication preparation areas to minimize distraction.

Communication failures, identified in 36% of studies, represent another system-level determinant extending beyond the individual nurse patient interaction to encompass the entire healthcare team. Structured communication tools such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation), standardized handoff protocols, and interprofessional medication reconciliation processes represent evidence-based strategies to address these breakdowns.30,38

The intersection of environmental and communication factors creates compounding risk. When nurses are interrupted during medication preparation, a scenario documented to occur an average of 6.3 times per hour in pediatric settings, 42 the cognitive load required to resume the task accurately increases substantially. If this interruption coincides with inadequate documentation or unclear verbal orders, the probability of error escalates multiplicatively rather than additively. This multiplicative risk model has important implications for intervention design: addressing interruptions alone or communication alone may yield modest improvements but addressing both simultaneously through bundled interventions (interruption free zones combined with structured communication protocols) is likely to produce synergistic safety gains.

Implications of mitigation strategies

The five complementary mitigation domains identified converge on a systems-based approach consistent with Reason’s model 43 : education strengthens the knowledge barrier, dual verification adds a human redundancy layer, reporting systems enable organizational learning, technology provides automated safeguards, and interprofessional communication ensures information continuity. The emphasis on non-punitive reporting in seven studies reflects a paradigm shift toward a safety culture approach consistent with Dekker’s just culture framework, 46 where system-level analysis takes precedence over individual blame. For clinical implementation, we recommend: (a) mandatory pediatric medication safety competency assessment at minimum annually; (b) evidence-based nurse to patient ratios not exceeding 1:3 in PICUs and 1:2 in NICUs for medication intensive patients; (c) institutional adoption of BCMA technology with pediatric specific dose range alerts; and (d) implementation of structured interruption free medication administration protocols.

The technological dimension of mitigation deserves particular attention in the context of contemporary healthcare. Computerized provider order entry (CPOE) systems with integrated clinical decision support have demonstrated reductions in medication errors of 48 to 81%, largely at the prescribing and ordering stage. 14 Barcode medication administration (BCMA) technology adds an additional verification layer at the point of care, ensuring that the right medication is administered to the right patient. Smart infusion pumps with dose error reduction software provide automated alerts when programmed rates or concentrations fall outside predefined safety parameters. The integration of these technologies creates a multi-layered safety net consistent with high reliability organization principles. However, it is important to acknowledge that technology implementation must be accompanied by workflow redesign, staff training, and ongoing evaluation to avoid unintended consequences such as alert fatigue or workarounds that may paradoxically introduce new error types. 16

The evidence from Echeverry et al. 16 regarding computer tools for pharmacovigilance monitoring reinforces the importance of technological integration throughout the medication use process. While their review focused broadly on pharmacovigilance systems, the principles of automated surveillance, real time signal detection, and systematic data analysis are directly applicable to the monitoring and prevention of medication administration errors in pediatric settings.

Addressing evidence gaps: Severity classification and global representativeness

Two evidence gaps identified in this review warrant specific, targeted action. First, the absence of a standardized severity classification across studies precluded any analysis of error severity and, therefore, any prioritization of prevention by clinical impact. Future studies and hospital registries should adopt harmonized severity taxonomies (for example, the NCC MERP severity categories) together with validated, reproducible harm assessment processes, 47 so that resources can be directed to the errors most likely to cause serious harm rather than to those that are merely most frequent. Second, the pronounced skew of the evidence toward high income countries limits generalizability; funders and investigators should prioritize adequately powered, observation-based studies in low- and middle-income settings, where medication safety infrastructure, formulation availability and staffing patterns differ substantially and where the burden may be greater. Finally, certain clinically important administration errors remain under characterized, in particular those involving intravenous fluids and electrolytes, which can precipitate or aggravate dehydration and electrolyte disturbances in small children; electrolytes and fluids were already among the medication classes implicated in the included studies, and they merit dedicated attention, alongside a broader consideration of the specific clinical contexts (such as fluid management) in which administration errors translate into patient harm.

Strengths

This review has several strengths. Adherence to JBI methodology and PRISMA-ScR reporting standards provides methodological transparency. The search across nine databases in three languages, using translated DeCS terms for the regional Latin American literature, maximizes the breadth of evidence captured. The inclusion of diverse study designs, spanning direct observation, chart review and incident reporting methods, enables a more complete picture of the phenomenon and allows the influence of ascertainment method to be examined explicitly.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The restriction to three languages may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages. The heterogeneity in error definitions and detection methods (direct observation versus chart review versus voluntary reporting) across studies limited direct comparison of error rates. Despite a search closing in July 2026, the most recent included study dates from 2024, which may reflect publication lag in the field. The predominance of observational studies limits causal inference. Finally, publication bias toward studies reporting higher error rates cannot be excluded.

Additionally, the inclusion of systematic reviews alongside primary studies in our synthesis may introduce a degree of overlap in the evidence base. However, this approach is consistent with scoping review methodology, which aims to map the breadth of available evidence rather than provide a definitive quantitative synthesis. The geographical distribution of included studies, while spanning 13 countries, was skewed toward high income settings, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to low- and middle-income countries where medication safety infrastructure may differ substantially. The absence of standardized severity classification across studies also precluded analysis of error severity distribution, which represents an important dimension for prioritizing prevention efforts.

Conclusions

This scoping review provides a mapping of the evidence on factors associated with medication administration errors in hospitalized pediatric patients. The synthesis of 25 studies reveals that these errors are driven by a multilayered interplay of human, environmental, communicative, and medication related factors, with incorrect dosing as the cardinal error and neonates/infants as the most vulnerable population. Effective prevention demands integrated, systems-based approaches that align with Reason’s Swiss Cheese Model: combining targeted education, standardized verification protocols, technological safeguards, non-punitive reporting systems, and interprofessional communication. These findings provide an evidence base for clinicians, educators, and policymakers to design targeted interventions that protect hospitalized children from preventable medication related harm.
