Background

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and microbiota-associated metabolites contribute to the development of sepsis and sepsis-related organ dysfunction, however, the key metabolites and host targets remain poorly understood. Diosmetin is a bioactive flavonoid that can be generated from dietary flavonoid glycosides through gut microbiota-mediated biotransformation. This study aimed to identify sepsis-associated microbiota-related candidate metabolites and to perform preliminary in vivo validation of diosmetin in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (AKI).

Methods and results

A network pharmacology framework was used to construct a sepsis-related microbiota–metabolite–target–pathway network. Pharmacodynamic analysis, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) evaluation, and molecular docking were performed to prioritize candidate metabolites and assess the potential interaction between diosmetin and AKT1. Diosmetin was evaluated in a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced mouse model of sepsis-associated AKI. Network analysis predicted 19 core metabolites and 45 sepsis-associated targets, with IL-6, TNF-α, AKT1, TP53, IL-1β, and PPAR-γ as central nodes. The network was enriched in inflammation- and immune-related pathways, particularly the Toll-like receptor, PI3K/AKT, and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways. ADMET assessments identified 12 metabolites with favorable predicted pharmacokinetic and safety profiles. Molecular docking predicted potential binding between diosmetin and AKT1. In CLP-induced septic mice, diosmetin alleviated renal histopathological injury, reduced serum IL-6, TNF-α and creatinine levels, and was associated with increased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation in kidney tissue compared with untreated CLP mice.

Conclusions

This study identified sepsis-associated microbiota-related candidate metabolites through network-based analysis. In vivo evidence suggested that diosmetin attenuated inflammatory responses in a sepsis-associated AKI model.

Introduction

Sepsis is a heterogeneous and life-threatening syndrome characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection, and contributes to more than 11 million deaths worldwide each year [1]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most frequent sepsis-related organ injuries with clinically consequential complications. Although bundled management strategies have improved outcomes, current treatment for AKI remains largely supportive, including antibiotic therapy, fluid resuscitation, and organ support. Mechanism-directed therapies have not yet produced consistent clinical benefits in sepsis, highlighting the need for a clearer understanding of AKI pathobiology [2].

Gut microbiota dysbiosis plays a central role in sepsis development, immune imbalance, and multi-organ injury [3, 4]. A healthy gut microbiota protects the host by maintaining mucosal barrier integrity and providing colonization resistance against pathogenic invasion. Infection and broad-spectrum antibiotics can disrupt this microbial equilibrium, transforming the gut into a major source of pathogenic translocation during sepsis [5, 6]. Organ dysfunction is a major determinant of poor prognosis in sepsis, and injury to critical organs is closely linked to gut microbiota dysbiosis. Restoration of microbial balance mitigates organ injury in septic mice, supporting the relevance of the gut–organ axis in sepsis [7–9]. In sepsis, microbiota dysbiosis and altered microbial metabolites may contribute to renal inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue injury through the gut–kidney axis. Despite these insights, clinical management of sepsis-associated AKI remains predominantly supportive rather than mechanism-directed.

Microbiota-associated metabolites serve as key mediators linking microbial dysbiosis to host immune responses and organ injury [10–12]. However, the spectrum of microbiota-related metabolites involved in sepsis remains incompletely characterized. Levels of aromatic metabolites, such as tryptophan and tyrosine, are reduced in patients with sepsis and correlate with disease severity and prolonged hospitalization [13–15]. Supplementation with short-chain fatty acids mitigates blood–brain barrier disruption and neuroinflammation, suggesting that microbial metabolites may regulate inflammation and protect organ function [9]. Diosmetin (3′,5,7-trihydroxy-4′-methoxyflavone) is a bioactive flavonoid aglycone that can be generated from dietary flavonoid glycosides through gut microbiota-mediated biotransformation. Diosmetin has been reported to exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in various pathological conditions [16–18]. However, its biological activity in sepsis-associated AKI remains unclear.

Network pharmacology integrates multidimensional biological information to explore relationships among metabolites, targets, pathways, and diseases, and provides a useful strategy for prioritizing candidates within complex microecological networks [19]. Although increasing evidence links gut microbiota dysbiosis and microbiota-related metabolites to sepsis-associated organ failure, the key microbiota-associated metabolites and their potential host targets remain poorly defined. In addition, few studies have combined microbiota-related metabolite prioritization with host-level in vivo validation in sepsis-associated organ injury.

In this study, we constructed a multi-level microbiota–substrate–metabolite–target–signaling pathway (MMTS) framework linking gut microbiota, candidate metabolites, host targets, and enriched signaling pathways in sepsis. Diosmetin was selected as a representative candidate for limited proof-of-concept in vivo validation in sepsis-associated AKI. The experimental component was designed to test whether a network-prioritized candidate showed biologically relevant host-level activity in vivo. Other prioritized metabolites identified by the same framework remain important leads for future validation.

Materials and methods

Selection of microbiota-associated metabolites and potential targets

Microbiota-associated metabolites and their corresponding target genes were retrieved from the gutMGene v2.0 database (https://bio-computing.hrbmu.edu.cn/gutmgene), which curates experimentally validated microbial genes, metabolites, and gene-metabolite interactions [20]. The Simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) format of each metabolite was obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Potential downstream targets were predicted using both the Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA, https://sea.kslab.org) and SwissTargetPrediction (STP, http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch).

Target genes for sepsis-related metabolites

Sepsis-related targets were retrieved by searching GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/) using “sepsis” as the keyword. Duplicate entries were removed, retaining only direct disease-associated targets. Target names were standardized via UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/). Potential targets of gut microbial metabolites in sepsis were then predicted through alignment in R 4.5.0 and cross-referenced with gutMGene entries to determine direct gene-metabolite associations.

GO and KEGG analysis of target genes

Core targets were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses using R 4.5.0, with results visualized via bubble and circle plots. GO analysis characterized targets across three domains: cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and biological processes (BP), while KEGG analysis predicted key signaling pathways associated with these targets.

Protein-protein Interaction network construction

Intersecting genes were uploaded to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to retrieve protein-protein interaction (PPI) data. Hub genes were predicted based on node connectivity and interaction degree. The resulting PPI network was visualized as a circular diagram using Cytoscape 3.10.4.

Construction of the MMTS network

Files detailing microbiota-metabolite, metabolite-target, and target-pathway interactions were imported into R 4.5.0 for key node analysis. The resulting microbiota-substrates-metabolites-targets-signaling pathways (MMTS) network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.4 to depict relationships among gut microbiota, substrates, metabolites, targets, and signaling pathways in sepsis. Pathway-metabolite-target connections are summarized in Table S1 and S2.

Evaluation of drug-likeness

The physicochemical properties, pharmacokinetics, drug-likeness, and medicinal chemistry friendliness of metabolites were assessed using the SwissADME tool (http://www.swissadme.ch/) [21]. Metabolites were screened according to molecular weight (MW), number of H-bond donors and acceptors, MlogP, Lipinski’s rule violations, bioavailability score, and topological polar surface area (TPSA).

Molecular docking of metabolites and targets

Drug-like metabolites were docked with target proteins to estimate potential binding. Three-dimensional metabolite structures were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and protein structures were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). Docking, visualization, and analysis were performed using CB-Dock (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php). Docking scores were reported in kcal·mol⁻¹. Potential binding sites were visualized.

Toxicological evaluation of metabolites

Drug-like metabolites were evaluated for absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) using ADMETlab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/) [22]. Eight toxicity endpoints were scored: hERG inhibition, drug-induced liver injury, carcinogenicity, respiratory toxicity, human hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity-DI, hematotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity-DI. Scores ranged from 0 to 0.1 (--), 0.1–0.3 (-), 0.3–0.5 (-+), 0.5–0.7 (+), 0.7–0.9 (++), to 0.9–1.0 (+++). A score of +++ indicates high predicted clinical risk.

Strategy for screening and prioritizing candidates for in vivo validation

A comprehensive prioritization strategy was applied to select candidate metabolites for in vivo validation. The following factors were considered: the degree of network association with key targets and enriched pathways, predicted docking affinity for core targets, drug-likeness and ADMET properties, predicted toxicity risks, biological plausibility based on previously reported anti-inflammatory activities, and the feasibility of in vivo administration.

Based on this integrated assessment, diosmetin was selected as the representative candidate for proof-of-concept validation. This selection was supported by its association with AKT1/PI3K-AKT-related module, relatively favorable predicted docking with AKT1, acceptable predicted pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles, and consistency between its reported anti-inflammatory activities and the inflammatory pathways prioritized in the network analysis. Therefore, diosmetin was used to provide preliminary biological validation of the proposed prioritization framework.

Construction of CLP-induced sepsis model

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Charles River Laboratories, China). After a 7-day acclimatization period, sepsis was induced using the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) procedure. All animal experiments were designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines, and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Approval No. 2025 − 293). Briefly, mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane. Following hair removal, a 1–1.5 cm midline laparotomy was performed. The cecum was exteriorized and ligated at approximately 50% of its distal portion using 5 − 0 silk sutures. The ligated cecum was punctured once or twice through both walls with a 22-gauge needle, and a small quantity of fecal content was gently expressed. The cecum was returned to the peritoneal cavity, and the abdominal wall was closed in layers using standard surgical techniques.

For the experimental design, mice were randomly assigned to the Control, CLP, or CLP+diosmetin groups. Five mice were included in each group. The Control group underwent sham surgery and received an equivalent volume of normal saline at the same time points as the treatment groups. The CLP group underwent CLP surgery and received an equivalent volume of normal saline immediately, 3 h, and 6 h after surgery. The CLP+diosmetin group underwent CLP surgery and received intraperitoneal injections of diosmetin at a dose of 100 µg/g immediately, 3 h, and 6 h after surgery. Diosmetin was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China; CAS No. 520-34-3; lot No. T22F7 × 9844; purity: 99.3%). A 10 mg/mL diosmetin working solution was freshly prepared before use. The injection volume was adjusted according to body weight to ensure a final dose of 100 µg/g for each mouse.

After surgery, all mice were monitored until the 24 h endpoint. Twelve hours after CLP, mice were evaluated for characteristic signs of sepsis, including hunched posture, markedly reduced activity, lethargy, decreased responsiveness, piloerection, and diarrhea. Septic status after CLP was determined based on these qualitative observational signs. No deaths occurred in the Control, CLP, or CLP+diosmetin groups during this period. Blood and organ samples were collected 24 h after surgery for further analyses.

Histomorphological analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 0.6% ammonia water, counterstained with eosin, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Slides were scanned using a Pannoramic Scan 150 system. Renal histopathological injury was evaluated using a semi-quantitative tubular injury score. H&E-stained kidney sections were independently assessed by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to group allocation. The percentage of injured tubules in each field was graded as follows: 0, no obvious injury; 1, < 10% injured tubules; 2, 10%–25% injured tubules; 3, 26%–50% injured tubules; 4, 51%–75%; and 5, > 75%. Five randomly selected non-overlapping fields from each kidney section were evaluated, and the mean score was used for statistical analysis.

Assessment of inflammatory cytokines

Serum concentrations of IL-6 (Elabscience, Wuhan, China, CAS No. E-EL-M0044), TNF-α (Elabscience, Wuhan, China, CAS No. E-EL-M3063), and creatinine (Bio-swamp, Wuhan, China, CAS No. MU30834) were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. Serum levels were determined according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Each sample was measured in triplicate. A standard curve was generated, and R² > 0.99 was considered acceptable for quantitative analysis.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors (MCE, USA). Samples were homogenized on ice and centrifuged to obtain clarified lysates. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were denatured at 100 °C, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence using a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as integrated optical density (IOD). Phosphorylated PI3K and phosphorylated AKT were normalized to their corresponding total protein levels, and the ratios of p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT were used for statistical analysis. Total PI3K and total AKT were normalized to β-actin when applicable. Normalized values were expressed relative to the mean value of the Control group. The original western blot images and detailed antibody information are provided in Figure S1 and Table S3, respectively.

Quantitative and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data normality was confirmed prior to analysis. Comparison between two groups was performed using Student’s t-test. Comparison between multiple groups was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post-hoc pairwise analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of microbiota-related metabolites

In total, 206 gut microbiota-related metabolites with corresponding SMILES structures were retrieved from the gutMGene database. Target prediction using the SEA and STP databases yielded 1291 and 797 potential targets, respectively, among which 570 were shared. Overall, 1518 potential targets associated with microbiota-related metabolites were predicted (Fig. 1A, Table S4).

Prediction of core targets in sepsis

In total, 1559 sepsis-related targets were retrieved from the GeneCards and CTD databases (Table S4). Among these, 367 targets were closely associated with gut microbiota metabolites (Fig. 1B–C). After cross-referencing with gut microbiota-related targets, 45 target genes were predicted as potential links between microbiota-associated metabolites and sepsis (Fig. 1D).

Functional enrichment analysis and core target screening

We investigated the functional roles of the 45 sepsis-associated target genes by performing GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. GO analysis showed that the targets were primarily involved in immune and inflammatory processes, including responses to bacterial components, regulation of inflammation, and apoptotic signaling. At the cellular level, the targets were enriched in membrane microdomains, vesicles, luminal compartments, and inflammation-related complexes. In terms of molecular function, the targets were associated with transcription regulation, receptor and enzyme activities, and protein binding (Fig. 2A–D). KEGG enrichment analysis suggested associations with immune- and inflammation-related signaling, with Toll-like receptor, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK pathways among the top modules (Fig. 3A–B).

A protein–protein interaction network was constructed using the STRING database, and comprised 45 nodes and 532 edges. Within this database-derived network, six hub genes were predicted based on node connectivity: IL-6, TNF, AKT1, TP53, IL1B, and PPARG (Fig. 3C–D).

Microbiota–substrate–metabolite–target–signaling pathways network in sepsis

Based on these enrichment results and hub target screening, subsequent analyses focused on interactions among gut microbiota, metabolites, and core signaling pathways. The MMTS network comprised 136 nodes and 175 edges, including 45 microbial nodes, 32 substrates, 53 metabolites, and six core targets (Fig. 4). Network analysis indicated that three major pathways, Toll-like receptor, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling, were linked to multiple metabolites and core targets. Representative hub gene-metabolite links are provided in Table S1, and pathway-hub gene connections are provided in Table S2. These metabolites and their corresponding targets were selected for subsequent prioritization and experimental evaluation.

Molecular docking analysis

After filtering, 19 key metabolites and four core targets were retained for molecular docking. The complete docking results are summarized in Table S5. Docking was used to estimate potential ligand–target binding between prioritized metabolites and candidate targets. These computational scores do not establish direct binding, functional target engagement, or biological activity, and therefore require validation in dedicated experimental systems. The predicted binding sites are presented in Fig. 5.

Toxicological evaluation of metabolites

The nineteen candidate metabolites were assessed for toxicity using ADMETlab to complement the evaluation of their overall safety profiles. Twelve metabolites exhibited relatively favorable predicted toxicological profiles and were therefore retained as higher-priority candidates for subsequent consideration. (Table 2). Several candidates showed potential safety concerns: arachidonic acid, doconexent, and (10-[(Acetyloxy)methyl]-9-anthryl)methyl acetate showed hepatotoxicity; N-Acetyl-L-aspartic acid showed predicted nephrotoxicity; luteolin and linoleic acid showed predicted skin sensitization, and icaritin showed predicted respiratory effects. Although luteolin showed a predicted docking score with AKT1 comparable to that of diosmetin, its less favorable predicted skin sensitization profile reduced its priority for the current in vivo validation stage.

Diosmetin reduces inflammatory responses and is associated with increased PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in sepsis-associated AKI

Based on the integrated prioritization results, PI3K/AKT phosphorylation was selected as a representative pathway-associated readout for preliminary in vivo validation of diosmetin (Fig. 6A). Serum concentrations of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were markedly increased in septic mice, whereas diosmetin administration significantly reduced these levels. Serum creatinine levels increased after CLP and decreased after diosmetin treatment (Fig. 6B). Semi-quantitative histological scoring confirmed the renal pathological changes observed in H&E-stained sections. The renal tubular injury score was significantly increased in the CLP group compared with the Control group, whereas diosmetin treatment significantly reduced this score compared with the CLP group (Fig. 6C). Western blot analysis showed that phosphorylated PI3K and AKT levels were reduced in septic mice compared with sham controls. Diosmetin treatment was associated with higher PI3K and AKT phosphorylation than in untreated CLP mice (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Sepsis is a life-threatening systemic inflammatory syndrome that frequently leads to multi-organ dysfunction. Gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to its onset and progression by promoting opportunistic pathogen overgrowth, disrupting immune homeostasis, and altering microbial metabolite profiles. In this study, we integrated gut microbiota databases with network pharmacology to characterize microbiota-related metabolites and their potential downstream targets. Twelve drug-like metabolites with relatively favorable predicted toxicological profiles were prioritized, and six core targets were identified. These metabolites were mainly linked to Toll-like receptor, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways. In vivo evaluation provided preliminary host-level evidence that diosmetin reduced systemic inflammatory cytokines and attenuated renal histopathological injury, accompanied by increased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation.

The network pharmacology workflow used in this study depended on public databases and target-prediction platforms. Target prediction algorithms are largely based on chemical similarity, known ligand–target relationships, and previously curated bioactivity data; therefore, they may generate false-positive associations or miss context-specific targets that are active only under septic conditions. Similarly, disease databases are influenced by literature and annotation bias, whereby extensively studied inflammatory genes may be overrepresented. As a result, highly investigated targets may be preferentially identified as hub nodes, and enrichment analysis may amplify well-characterized inflammatory pathways.

The MMTS network yielded a set of drug-like, and toxicologically favorable metabolites linked to multiple pathways relevant to sepsis biology. Network and docking analyses results also identified testable leads in addition to diosmetin. For example, IL-1β, IL-6, and TP53 were predicted as highly connected targets, and representative metabolite-target pairs were identified in the docking analysis and are summarized in Table S5 and Fig. 5. These candidates align with the pathway clusters enriched in TLR, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling.

ADMETlab-based toxicity assessment was used as a computational screening tool to support candidate prioritization rather than as definitive evidence of in vivo or clinical toxicity. Several metabolites showed predicted safety liabilities, including hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, skin sensitization, or respiratory toxicity. These predicted risks reduced their priority for the present in vivo validation stage. These in silico toxicity results remain predictive and require confirmation through dose–response studies, organ-specific toxicity evaluation, and long-term safety assessment.

Intestinal dysfunction is a major contributor to sepsis-associated multiple organ dysfunction. Understanding changes in the gut microbiota and their metabolites is therefore an important focus in critical care medicine. A healthy gut microbiota protects the host through several mechanisms, including colonization resistance, microbial metabolite-mediated regulation, and the maintenance of immune homeostasis [23]. Both the underlying disease process and clinical interventions have been found to play important roles in shaping microbial dysbiosis. Patients with sepsis often show reduced commensal bacterial abundance, overgrowth of pathogenic or drug-resistant organisms, and decreased levels of tryptophan- and tyrosine-derived metabolites; these changes correlate with disease severity [24–26]. Experimental studies further support the role of gut microbiota in modulating host responses during sepsis [27]. Short-chain fatty acids, such as acetate, exert pronounced anti-inflammatory and organ-protective effects by inhibiting NOX2/ROS signaling and restoring the intracellular oxidant–antioxidant balance in T cells [28, 29]. Several metabolites prioritized in this study are implicated in neuroimmune regulation or other organ systems, supporting the network-level nature of predicted candidates. Organ-specific validation is still required.

The gut microbiota maintains immune homeostasis through interactions between intestinal epithelial cells and Toll-like receptors expressed on immune cells. Among the TLR family, TLR4 is a key pattern-recognition receptor whose activation triggers an inflammatory cascade via the downstream NF-κB signaling pathway, compromises intestinal barrier integrity, and exacerbates sepsis-associated acute lung injury [30]. Other members of the TLR family, including TLR5, TLR7, and TLR9, also modulate gut microbial diversity and contribute to anti-inflammatory responses and the maintenance of immune homeostasis [31–33].

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is the major intracellular signaling cascade that regulate cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, autophagy, and immune responses. Metabolites produced by probiotic genera may activate PI3K/AKT signaling, promote epithelial repair gene transcription, and maintain epithelial barrier integrity [34–38]. In this study, PI3K/AKT phosphorylation was selected as a representative pathway-associated readout for preliminary in vivo validation of diosmetin. This selection was based on the identification of AKT1 as a hub target, the predicted diosmetin–AKT1 docking profile, and the known relevance of PI3K/AKT signaling to inflammation and organ injury responses. Diosmetin treatment was associated with increased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation in CLP mice. However, the current data do not exclude contributions from other inflammatory pathways.

In the MMTS network, several metabolites showed favorable predicted docking and safety profiles. Diosmetin was identified as a microbiota-associated candidate metabolite based on gutMGene annotations and showed a relatively favorable predicted docking score with AKT1, acceptable predicted toxicological characteristics, and reported anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activitie [39, 40]. Diosmetin was selected as a proof-of-concept candidate because its integrated profile aligned with the inflammatory and host-response pathways prioritized in the network analysis. In addition, 3-(4-Hydroxyphenyl)propionic acid showed predicted affinity for TP53, a potential MAPK pathway-associated target. In vivo studies have indicated that this metabolite mitigates inflammation by modulating MAPK and NF-κB signaling while maintaining a favorable safety profile [41].

In summary, this study used network pharmacology to prioritize sepsis-associated microbiota-related candidate metabolites and potential host targets. The findings provide a hypothesis-generating framework for subsequent validation. However, further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to confirm the predicted metabolite–target interactions, clarify organ-specific mechanisms, and evaluate the translational relevance of candidate metabolites.

This study has several limitations. First, the network pharmacology analysis was based on public databases and target-prediction platforms. These database-driven analyses are inherently static and cannot fully capture the dynamic and complex biological processes involved in sepsis. Therefore, the predicted metabolite–target–pathway relationships should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. Second, the target-retrieval strategy used “sepsis” as the disease keyword to capture the broader host-response network associated with sepsis. Thus, the computationally predicted targets should be interpreted as sepsis-associated rather than AKI-specific targets. Future studies using sepsis-associated AKI-specific datasets and kidney-focused omics data are needed to refine the organ-specific target network. Third, the animal experiment did not include fecal microbiota profiling or metabolite abundance measurement. Accordingly, the current in vivo data reflect host-level biological activity after exogenous diosmetin administration and do not establish that the observed effects were microbiota-mediated or dependent on microbial metabolism. Future studies incorporating fecal 16 S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, metabolomics, antibiotic-treated or germ-free animal models, and fecal microbiota transplantation are required to clarify microbiota-dependent effects. Fourth, no standardized quantitative severity score was applied after CLP, which may affect model reproducibility and interpretation of treatment effects. Fifth, the experimental validation was limited to a single candidate metabolite, one organ injury model, and a short 24-h endpoint. The renal findings should therefore be interpreted primarily as histopathological, inflammatory, and early functional evidence rather than definitive evidence of durable renal protection. Finally, the molecular docking protocol was not fully validated by re-docking co-crystallized ligands for all protein targets, and the absence of pathway-specific inhibition, genetic knockdown, or other loss-of-function experiments prevents causal assessment of whether PI3K/AKT activation is required for the observed effects of diosmetin.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a network pharmacology framework to identify sepsis-associated microbiota-related candidate metabolites and potential host targets. Twelve metabolites were prioritized based on predicted pharmacokinetic, toxicological, and pathway-related profiles. Among them, diosmetin was selected for preliminary in vivo validation in a CLP-induced sepsis-associated AKI model. Diosmetin reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, attenuated renal histopathological injury, and was associated with increased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation compared with untreated CLP mice. These findings provide preliminary host-level evidence supporting diosmetin as a candidate metabolite for further investigation.
