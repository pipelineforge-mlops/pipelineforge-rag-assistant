Objective

Burnout is a persistent issue among healthcare professionals, impairing both patient care and provider well-being. This pilot feasibility study evaluated the preliminary effects of a brief, mobile app–based mindfulness training (MT) program, developed using a user-centered design approach, in reducing burnout and improving psychological outcomes among registered nurses and resident physicians.

Methods

Two single-arm pilot trials were conducted. Participants completed the digital MT intervention and online assessments at pre-intervention, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up. Study 1 included 27 registered nurses; Study 2 included 29 resident physicians. While the intervention consisted of seven digital modules, the average completion time was 28 days (SD = 15) for nurses and 30 days (SD = 16) for residents, reflecting a self-paced delivery model. The primary outcome was cynicism, with secondary outcomes including emotional exhaustion, anxiety, depression, intolerance of uncertainty, self-compassion, nonreactivity, non-judgment, worry, sleep disturbances, and emotion regulation difficulties.

Results

Among nurses, significant reductions were associated with participation for cynicism (27%) and emotional exhaustion (28%), with decreased scores maintained at follow-up. Additional post-intervention improvements included reduced anxiety (46%), depression (52%), worry (7%), sleep disturbances (16%), and emotion regulation difficulties (11%). Increases were also found in self-compassion (13%), nonreactivity (8%), and non-judgment (9%). Among residents, significant reductions in cynicism did not persist after correction for multiple comparisons. However, residents showed significant reductions in emotional exhaustion (19%), anxiety (32%), intolerance of uncertainty (14%), emotion regulation difficulties (13%) and sleep disturbances (15%) at follow-up as well as early increases in self-compassion (21%) and nonreactivity (19%) and reduction in worry (13%). Emotional exhaustion and anxiety were consistently correlated across time points in both groups.

Conclusions

These findings replicate and extend previous evidence among physicians, supporting the feasibility and preliminary benefits of brief and self-paced app-based mindfulness training (MT) in addressing burnout and anxiety among both nurses and residents. These pilot results provide a basis for future randomized controlled trials to further evaluate the intervention's effectiveness.

Highlights

• User-centered design helped optimize app-based burnout intervention.

• Mobile mindfulness training was associated with reduced burnout in nurses and resident physicians.

• Psychological improvements were maintained at one-month follow-up.

1 Introduction

It's 3:47 a.m. The hospital corridor glows under fluorescent lights. Exhausted, a resident physician rubs their eyes, trying to stay awake and focused while finishing patient notes. A nurse ends their twelfth consecutive hour on their feet, hungry and without having seen their family. This is not an unusual night, but it reflects the daily reality faced by thousands of healthcare workers pushed to their limits.

1.1 Background

The chronic stress behind the scenes in healthcare is part of a larger, and well-documented crisis. Burnout, defined as an occupational syndrome marked by emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment (WHO, 2019), remains a persistent and critical issue in healthcare, worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic (Shanafelt et al., 2022). In 2024, 43.2% of physicians reported experiencing at least one symptom of burnout, a decrease from 48.2% in 2023 and 53% in 2022 (Berg, 2025). While this trend may reflect progress from systemic efforts, experts caution against premature optimism (Berg, 2025). Specific initiatives have begun to address the root causes of clinician distress, such as the Dr. Lorna Breen Health Care Provider Protection Act, which provides federal grants for employee education and helps remove stigmatizing mental health questions from state licensing applications (Sindhu and Adashi, 2022). Additionally, health systems have increasingly adopted the AMA's ‘Joy in Medicine’ framework, which directs organizations to appoint Chief Wellness Officers and implement ‘efficiency of practice’ reforms to reduce administrative tasks (AMA, 2025). Despite these gains, burnout rates remain significantly higher than the 38% pre-pandemic baseline (Shanafelt et al., 2022).

Though much of the literature emphasizes physician burnout, evidence shows that nurses and resident physicians—who often have less autonomy and higher frontline exposure—are equally, if not more, affected. In 2025, 65% of nurses reported high stress and burnout levels (Galoustian, 2025). A national U.S. survey found burnout prevalence at 47% among nurses compared to 32% among physicians (Aiken et al., 2023). Common drivers include chronic understaffing, long and demanding shifts, inadequate compensation, poor clinical supervision and leadership, and a lack of autonomy or respect (Dilig-Ruiz et al., 2018; Galanis et al., 2023; Specchia et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2020). Nurses frequently manage large patient loads, emotionally charged interactions, and limited organizational support (Bruyneel et al., 2024, Bruyneel et al., 2025; Friese et al., 2024; Lasater, 2024).

Resident physicians, though at a different career stage, face similar challenges. In 2024, 34.5% of residents reported burnout symptoms (Berg, 2025). Like nurses, residents work on the front lines of care, often in high-pressure environments and rapidly changing clinical situations (Phillips and Dalgarno, 2017). They manage complex patients while working long hours, frequent on-call shifts, and with minimal control over their schedules, all while making critical decisions (Berg, 2025; Ng et al., 2024, Ng et al., 2025; Phillips and Dalgarno, 2017). However, residents also navigate unique stressors related to their transitional role from student to independent clinician, such as exams, research, teaching, and administrative tasks (Ng et al., 2024, Ng et al., 2025). Additionally, personality traits such as perfectionism, reluctance to delegate, and workaholism also intensify burnout risk (Ng et al., 2025; Schaufeli et al., 2009).

1.2 The high cost of burnout

The consequences of burnout are extensive. It is linked to deteriorating personal well-being and relationships, increased medical errors, reduced quality of care, lower job satisfaction, absenteeism, and high turnover (Dyrbye et al., 2020; Galanis et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Low et al., 2019). For instance, a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 85 studies, including 288,581 nurses from 32 countries, found burnout associated with lower safety climate and more adverse events, such as more frequent nosocomial infections, patient falls, medication errors, patient safety incidents and missed care as well as lower patient satisfaction (Li et al., 2024). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have consistently shown that nurses and resident physicians who experience high levels of burnout also frequently report comorbid symptoms, including anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances (Buivydienė et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2021; Membrive-Jiménez et al., 2022). Rather than existing as isolated experiences, the core burnout dimensions of emotional exhaustion and cynicism often coexist with and exacerbate anxiety in medical professionals (Roy et al., 2021). This relationship is often reciprocal. Theoretical models suggest that as emotional exhaustion and cynicism diminish a clinician's perceived self-efficacy, their capacity to manage high-stakes clinical environments weakens, potentially leading to increased anxiety (Roy et al., 2021).

Worsening the issue is a critical global shortage of healthcare workers. The World Health Organization estimates that by 2030, there will be a shortfall of 10 million healthcare workers (WHO, 2024). Among them, nurses alone face a global deficit of over 5.7 million, expected to persist for years (WHO, 2024). Physician shortages are also widespread internationally, with many countries struggling to train and retain doctors (OECD, 2023). In the U.S., the Association of American Medical College projects a shortage of up to 86,000 physicians by 2036 (AAMC, 2024). While these gaps are primarily driven by structural factors, such as an aging population with increased clinical needs and statutory limits on residency training capacity, burnout acts as a critical exacerbating lever. By increasing early retirement and reducing clinical work hours, burnout further depletes the available workforce (Dall et al., 2024). In the U.S. alone, the annual cost of physician turnover and reduced clinical effort is estimated at approximately $4.6 billion (Han et al., 2019). Consequently, addressing burnout—particularly among nurses and resident physicians— is not only a matter of provider well-being but a necessary economic and public health strategy to mitigate these projected shortages.

1.3 Mindfulness as a pathway to recovery

Burnout is a systemic issue that demands both institutional and individual-level solutions. While organizational strategies address structural factors like workload, staffing, and supportive work environments, individual approaches focus on building resilience through personal development and coping tools (Luberto et al., 2017; Ruotsalainen et al., 2015; West et al., 2016).

Among strategies for clinician burnout, mindfulness training (MT) has emerged as one of the most promising (Cohen et al., 2023; Hsu et al., 2024). Mindfulness, as defined by Kabat-Zinn, involves intentionally paying attention to the present moment in a nonjudgmental way (Kabat-Zinn, 1991). One of the earliest and most influential studies in this area was conducted by Krasner et al. in 2009, who demonstrated that an MT program for primary care physicians led to significant improvements in burnout, mood disturbance, and empathy (Krasner et al., 2009). Meta-analytical evidence supports the efficacy of mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) in clinical settings (Fendel et al., 2021; Lomas et al., 2018). For instance, a systematic review by Lomas et al. (2018) involving 3805 healthcare professionals found that MBIs consistently improve well-being and reduce psychological distress (Lomas et al., 2018). Furthermore, Fendel et al. (2021) confirmed in a meta-analysis of 25 studies including 925 physicians that MBIs yield significant reductions in burnout and stress, with benefits maintained at follow-up (Fendel et al., 2021).

Recent research has highlighted the value of individual-focused interventions, particularly mindfulness and coaching, for nurses and resident physicians (Cohen et al., 2023; Hsu et al., 2024; Kiratipaisarl et al., 2024; Lu and Ratnapalan, 2022; Musker and Othman, 2024). For example, an umbrella review of nine systematic reviews and two meta-analyses, encompassing 131 studies, found that strategies such as mindfulness, yoga, and professional development significantly reduce burnout and occupational stress (Hsu et al., 2024). Similarly, a 2024 meta-analysis of 33 studies involving 2536 resident physicians showed that individual interventions like coaching and meditation significantly decreased emotional exhaustion and cynicism (Kiratipaisarl et al., 2024). By enhancing a clinician's capacity for mindfulness, these interventions may foster the self-compassion necessary to protect against empathy fatigue and long-term exhaustion (Bian et al., 2025; Neff, 2003; Wasson et al., 2020).

1.4 Bridging the gaps through co-design

Despite the established efficacy of mindfulness, a significant gap remains in its clinical implementation. Traditional programs are often prohibitively time-intensive, typically requiring eight-week commitments with 2.5-hour weekly sessions, 45 min of daily home practice, and a full-day retreat (Krasner et al., 2009; Scheepers et al., 2020), a structure that risks overwhelming already burdened clinicians. To improve accessibility, research has shifted toward digital mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs). A 2021 meta-analysis of 97 randomized controlled trials found that online MBIs were associated with significant improvements in depression, anxiety, stress, and mindfulness across diverse populations (Sommers-Spijkerman et al., 2021). Notably, these digital formats may offer benefits comparable to traditional face-to-face interventions while significantly increasing accessibility and scalability for healthcare workforces facing high clinical demands (Sommers-Spijkerman et al., 2021). The transition to digital formats alone, however, does not guarantee clinical engagement. The key contribution of the pilot study lies in its methodological shift toward a co-design and participatory framework where clinicians are active partners in the development process rather than passive recipients of a generic tool.

By involving healthcare workers in the design phase, the intervention is specifically tailored to the “user-environment” constraints of clinical practice, ensuring preliminary accessibility, user relevance, and engagement (Quezada et al., 2021; Swensen et al., 2016). Beyond digital delivery, a distinctive feature of this intervention is its emphasis on participant autonomy. By allowing for a self-paced progression, rather than enforcing a rigid daily schedule, the program is designed to enhance perceived control and reduce the burden of participation. This inherent flexibility is theoretically relevant for healthcare workforces, as it acknowledges the unpredictable nature of clinical shifts and allows for a more sustainable integration of mindfulness practices into daily life.

Our team utilized this user-centered approach to develop a brief digital MT intervention with direct input from a multidisciplinary group of attending physicians, nurses, psychologists, and social workers (Antico and Brewer, 2025). We subsequently evaluated its impact on attending physician burnout through two independent single-arm studies. Results from both studies suggested that participation in the digital MT intervention was associated with significant reductions in burnout, anxiety, and intolerance of uncertainty, with corresponding increases in self-compassion, nonreactivity, and non-judgment of internal experiences. Additionally, physicians reported improvements in sleep and emotional regulation, as well as reduced levels of worry (Antico and Brewer, 2025). Notably, these studies also found that burnout scores correlated positively with anxiety scores, further supporting the intertwined relationship between anxiety and burnout (Antico and Brewer, 2025).

2 Study 1 overview

To assess the feasibility and preliminary impact of our digital MT program across a wider range of clinicians, we conducted a preregistered pilot feasibility study with registered nurses. Using the same Unwinding app (Behavioral Health team at Sharecare), participants completed web-based surveys at baseline, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up (Antico and Brewer, 2025). As a pilot investigation, designed as a prevention strategy, this study aims to provide the preliminary evidence and effect size estimates necessary to power future randomized controlled trials.

The primary outcome was cynicism, a key indicator of empathy fatigue, with emotional exhaustion as a secondary outcome. While the MBI traditionally includes a third dimension (personal accomplishment), we focused on these two components as they represent the ‘core’ of burnout in medical professionals and were the primary targets of our MT intervention (IsHak et al., 2009; Thomas, 2004). Other secondary outcomes included anxiety, depression, mindfulness (specifically nonreactivity and non-judgment of inner experience), intolerance of uncertainty, worry, sleep disturbances, and difficulties in emotion regulation.

We evaluated the following hypotheses:Hypothesis 1 Nurses would report reduced cynicism post-intervention compared to baseline.

Hypothesis 2 Nurses would show improvement in secondary outcomes: lower emotional exhaustion, anxiety, depression, intolerance of uncertainty, worry, sleep disturbances, and difficulties in emotion regulation; and higher nonreactivity, non-judgment, and self-compassion.

Hypothesis 3 Cynicism and emotional exhaustion would positively correlate with anxiety.

3 Methods

3.1 Ethical considerations

The research was approved by the Brown University Institutional Review Board (Protocol 2022003296) and was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines for studies involving human subjects. Eligible participants were provided with an online informed consent form via Qualtrics. After consenting by selecting “yes” (a waiver for documentation of the informed consent was obtained), they were directed to complete the online survey. Participants were compensated with a $25 Amazon gift card for each survey completed, for a total of $75 for completing all three surveys. All data collected were de-identified, with each participant assigned to a unique alphanumeric code. To ensure confidentiality, the master key linking participant identifiers to these codes was stored in a password-protected file in a secure location separate from the study data. While identifiers and IP addresses were used for recruitment, rapport, and data quality screening, they were removed from all final datasets and destroyed upon study completion. All statistical analyses were conducted on this de-identified data. Outcomes and hypotheses were preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06145425). In alignment with CONSORT extension for Pilot and Feasibility Trials reporting standards, we note that the exploratory outcome of difficulties in emotion regulation was added after the pre-registration, as an exploratory measure.

3.2 Participants

Nurses were recruited from multiple sites across the United States, representing a diverse range of both academic medical centers and non-academic community institutions. Recruitment and data collection were conducted between April 2023 and October 2024. Participants were reached via digital flyers and hospital newsletters. Using a convenience sampling strategy appropriate for a feasibility design, eligible participants had to be currently employed as a registered nurse providing direct patient care, fluent in English, willing to listen to a mindfulness audio course for 15 min a day for one week, and willing to both complete three online surveys. In line with a prevention approach, no baseline burnout score thresholds were required for inclusion, allowing for an evaluation across a broad spectrum of the clinical workforce. The online surveys were distributed via Qualtrics, before and after the training, as well as one month after the end of the training. Participants were excluded from the study if their psychotropic medication dose had changed in the six weeks prior to enrollment. Participant recruitment and flow were conducted in alignment with the CONSORT extension for Pilot and Feasibility Trials. 3.3 Intervention

The mindfulness-based training program was designed to prevent burnout and empathy fatigue in healthcare professionals and was delivered via Unwinding by Sharecare mobile application (Antico and Brewer, 2025). While the platform provides a broad library of standalone guided meditations (e.g., breathing, noting), this study evaluated a specifically curated seven-module curriculum tailored to the unique occupational stressors of clinicians. Following the mHealth Evidence Reporting and Assessment (mERA) guidelines (Agarwal et al., 2017), the technology platform is characterized as a standalone digital therapeutic that utilizes an evidence-based habit-change theoretical framework to identify and alter unhelpful habit loops through reinforcement learning (Brewer, 2019).

The intervention consists of seven 15-minute modules, encompassing educational content, practical mindfulness exercises, and a concluding summary. This ultra-brief format, totaling approximately 1.75 h of content was specifically chosen to address the chronic time constraints of healthcare professionals. The curriculum is delivered as an asynchronous, self-paced audio course, enabling participants to integrate the training into daily transitions, such as commuting or breaks, without the requirement for a fixed schedule. Within this accessible format, the program utilizes an evidence-based habit-change theoretical framework to identify and alter unhelpful clinical habit loops through reinforcement learning (Brewer, 2019). The curriculum focuses on preventing empathy fatigue by distinguishing between empathy and compassion, managing intrusive thoughts and self-judgment with kindness, reducing stress, and preventing emotional contagion through curiosity. Each module integrates real-world clinical scenarios and strategies for breaking unhelpful behavioral patterns. While the broader app offers additional guided meditations—such as noting, breathing, and loving-kindness—these were optional and not mandatory for study participation (Antico and Brewer, 2025).

We developed the intervention through a user-centered design (UCD) approach, incorporating clinician feedback at each stage to ensure the curriculum was tailored to face the realities of clinical practice. After identifying primary burnout factors from the literature and interviews, a physician with over twenty years of clinical experience developed a “minimal viable product.” This initial version underwent two rounds of iterative testing with forty clinicians to ensure content fit, clarity, and feasibility. In the first round, ten physicians provided feedback via open-ended and closed-ended questions, evaluating how well the modules met their professional needs and the clarity of the pragmatic exercise instructions. Themes and real-world vignettes extracted from this feedback were incorporated into the curriculum to enhance authenticity. A second round involving thirty clinicians was then conducted to confirm the refined content's suitability. These refinements ensured that the final curriculum was specifically calibrated to the feasibility requirements and professional needs of the workforce (Antico and Brewer, 2025). Further technical specifications and mERA checklist compliance details are provided in Supplementary material (A).

3.4 Intervention orientation and surveillance

No strict time frame was set for completing each module, as this pilot study served as a feasibility and proof-of-concept phase to observe real-world engagement and the time required for clinicians to complete the program. Participants were instructed to complete the program at their own pace, ideally finishing one module per day, and were cautioned not to exceed one module per day. This pacing was intended to ensure they did not rush through the material and had sufficient time to implement the pragmatic exercises into their clinical practice. Furthermore, every two weeks, the instructor reached out to participants to investigate and address any technical issues of the app. While the application itself tracked module completion, the research team did not have access to this usage metadata during the study. Adherence was instead monitored via a manual verification process: participants were required to email the research team upon completing the seven modules to receive the link for the post-intervention survey. This approach ensured that participants had engaged with the full program content prior to the following assessments and allowed the research team to provide a controlled transition to the post-test phase.

3.5 Measures

Three online surveys took place before and after the training, as well as one month after the end of the training, to assess primary and secondary measures.

3.5.1 Primary outcome

Cynicism is one of the key dimensions of burnout and was selected as the primary outcome because the intervention was specifically tailored to address “empathy fatigue” and the interpersonal components of burnout. It was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) (Maslach et al., 1997). To minimize participant burden, we utilized a single item asking participants how often “they have become more callous toward people since they took this job,” with responses ranging from “never” to “every day.” The validity of this abbreviated MBI item compared to the complete questionnaire has been demonstrated by West and colleagues (West et al., 2009, West et al., 2012). These single-item measures have demonstrated strong concurrent validity with the full MBI and are highly predictive of physician well-being and clinical performance (West et al., 2009, West et al., 2012). Scores on the 7-point Likert scale range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater cynicism (Maslach et al., 1997).

3.5.2 Secondary outcomes

Emotional exhaustion, another key dimension of burnout, was assessed using the MBI (Maslach et al., 1997). Together with cynicism, these two dimensions are considered the “core” of the burnout syndrome in medical professionals (West et al., 2009, West et al., 2012). We utilized a single item asking participants how often “they felt burned out from their work,” with responses ranging from “never” to “every day.” Scores on the 7-point Likert scale range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater emotional exhaustion (Maslach et al., 1997).

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a 7-item questionnaire assessing symptoms of anxiety over the past week (Spitzer et al., 2006). Scores on the 4-point Likert scale range from 0 to 3, corresponding to “not at all” to “nearly every day,” with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. Anxiety severity is categorized as mild (5–9 points), moderate (10–14 points), or severe (15–21 points). In the current sample, internal consistency was good (Cronbach's α = 0.86).

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2), a 2-item measure evaluating how often participants in the past two weeks “had little interest or pleasure in doing things” and “felt down, depressed, or hopeless” (Kroenke et al., 2001). Scores on the 4-point Likert scale range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms (current sample α = 0.91).

Intolerance of uncertainty was assessed using the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale-12 (IUS-12), an abbreviated version containing 12 items from the original 27-item IUS (Carleton et al., 2007). Scores range on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 to 5, corresponding to “not at all characteristics of me” to “entirely characteristic of me.” Higher scores indicate greater intolerance of uncertainty, particularly in relation to ambiguous situations or an uncertain future (current sample α = 0.92).

Self-compassion was assessed using the Self-Compassion Scale Short Form (SCS-SF), a 12-item abbreviated version of the original 26-item SCS (Raes et al., 2011). Scores are measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, corresponding to “almost never” to “almost always”. Higher scores indicate greater self-compassion (current sample α = 1.0).

Mindfulness was assessed using the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ), which refers to their ability of nonreactivity and non-judgment of emotions and thoughts (Baer et al., 2008). The questionnaire for nonreactivity consists of 7 items and the questionnaire for non-judgment of 8 items from the original 39-item FFMQ, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. This scale ranges from 1 (indicating “never true”) to 5 (indicating “always true”). The total scores for nonreactivity range from 7 to 35, the total scores for non-judgment range from 8 to 40. Nonreactivity (current sample α = 0.84); Non-judgment (current sample α = 0.96).

Worry was assessed using the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ) (Meyer et al., 1990). The PSWQ consists of 16 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all typical of me”) to 5 (“very typical of me”). Total scores range from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher levels of worry (current sample α = 0.79).

Worry-related sleep disturbances were assessed using the Patient-reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) (Buysse et al., 2010; Yu et al., 2012). A short form with five preselected items was used, selected for their direct assessment of worry interfering with sleep (Yu et al., 2012). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“very much”). Total scores range from 5 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater sleep disturbances and sleep-related impairment (current sample α = 0.95).

Difficulties in emotion regulation were measured using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS), a 36-item questionnaire. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“almost never”) to 5 (“almost always”) (Gratz and Roemer, 2004). Total scores range from 36 to 180, with higher scores indicating greater difficulties in emotion regulation (current sample α = 0.94).

3.6 Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a per-protocol (PP) approach; only participants who completed the curated seven-module program and all three assessment points were included in the final analysis. Missing data from participants who did not complete the intervention or the follow-up surveys were handled via listwise deletion (complete-case analysis). Primary longitudinal analyses were performed using Repeated Measures ANOVA to evaluate overall changes in scores across the three points, for all primary and secondary outcomes. Given the non-normal distribution of some variables, non-parametric Friedman tests were also conducted as sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of the results. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with Benjamini-Hochberg corrections applied for multiple comparisons. Spearman's correlation coefficient was employed to assess the relationship between burnout dimensions and other variables.

Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (ηp2) for Repeated Measures ANOVA test, Kendall's W for Friedman tests and as Pearson's r for pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. For ηp2 values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 represented small, medium, and large effects. For both W and r, thresholds of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 were used to indicate small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively (Cohen, 1992).

5 Study 2 overview

To further evaluate the preliminary impact and feasibility of our MT program, we conducted a pre-registered study with resident physicians. This allowed us to assess the signal of effect for doctors at different career stages, who face both common stressors shared with attending physicians and nurses and unique challenges, such as longer shifts, more on-call hours, and greater work-related stress due to their training status, described above (West et al., 2018).

The study's aims and design mirrored Study 1. We used the same MT intervention, delivered via the Unwinding by Sharecare app, and assessed outcomes at baseline, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up, focusing on the same primary and secondary outcomes. As this was a pilot investigation, we focused on evaluating the consistency of the intervention's feasibility across different clinical cohorts.

We evaluated the following hypotheses:Hypothesis 1 Residents would report lower cynicism post-intervention compared to baseline.

Hypothesis 2 Residents would show improvements in secondary outcomes, including reductions in emotional exhaustion, anxiety, depression, intolerance of uncertainty, worry, sleep disturbances, and difficulties in emotion regulation; and increases in nonreactivity, non-judgment, and self-compassion.

Hypothesis 3 Cynicism and emotional exhaustion would positively correlate with anxiety.

6 Methods

6.1 Ethical considerations

As with Study 1, this study was approved by the Brown University Institutional Review Board (Protocol 2022003296) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines for research involving human subjects. Participants followed the same consent process as in Study 1, with identical compensation totaling $75. All data were anonymized and handled with strict confidentiality. As with Study 1, this study was preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06145425), except for the exploratory outcome of difficulties in emotion regulation that we added later.

6.2 Participants

This study followed the same single-arm pilot design and recruitment protocols as Study 1, but focused exclusively on residents. Data was collected from March 2023 to July 2024. Participant flow and attrition were tracked in accordance with CONSORT feasibility guidelines.

6.3 Intervention and surveillance

The same mindfulness-based intervention as in Study 1 was utilized. Similar to Study 1, this served as a proof-of-concept phase to observe real-world engagement patterns among residents. There was no strict time frame for completing the modules, but participants were cautioned to complete no more than one module per day.

6.4 Outcome measures

The same protocol as in Study 1 was followed, with measurements taken at three time points: baseline, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up. The same primary and secondary outcomes were applied as described in Study 1. In the current sample, internal consistency at baseline was as follows: anxiety (α = 0.90), depression (α = 0.67), intolerance of uncertainty (α = 0.94), self-compassion (α = 0.97), mindfulness nonreactivity (α = 0.82), mindfulness non-judgment (α = 0.93), worry (α = 0.93), sleep disturbances (α = 0.92), and difficulties in emotion regulation (α = 0.96).

6.5 Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in a manner analogous to Study 1.

8 Discussion

8.1 Principal findings

This study builds on our previous research suggesting the feasibility and preliminary signal of effect of digital mindfulness training (MT) in reducing physician burnout (Antico and Brewer, 2025). To examine whether these preliminary benefits extend beyond attending physicians, we conducted two independent single-arm pilot studies with registered nurses and resident physicians. To better understand the magnitude of these effects, we contextualized our findings relative to US population norms.

Findings among nurses largely replicated earlier results in attending physicians, with participation in the MT intervention being associated with MT leading to significant observed reductions in burnout, anxiety, depression, intolerance of uncertainty, worry, sleep disturbances, and difficulties in emotion regulation. Regarding clinical meaningfulness, emotional exhaustion moved from an average baseline frequency of “once a week” to “once a month or less,” while anxiety severity shifted from the “mild” clinical range to a “minimal” or sub-clinical level. Increases in self-compassion, nonreactivity, and non-judgment were also observed. Effect sizes ranged from medium to large at both post-intervention and follow-up assessments.

Among resident physicians, participation in the MT intervention was associated with observed reductions in several outcomes; however, the timing and robustness of these changes varied. A significant reduction in emotional exhaustion, anxiety, intolerance of uncertainty, and difficulties with emotion regulation emerged by the one-month follow-up rather than immediately post-intervention. Emotional exhaustion moved toward a “monthly” rather than “weekly” burden, and anxiety scores approached the sub-clinical threshold. Notably, no statistically significant reductions were maintained for cynicism after correction for multiple comparisons. Interestingly, certain psychological benefits were observed immediately post-intervention and were sustained over time, specifically increased self-compassion and nonreactivity and reduced worry. In both cohorts, depressive symptoms remained within the same sub-clinical category throughout the study, as baseline levels were already below the threshold for clinical risk.

These results replicate our previous findings in attending physicians (Antico and Brewer, 2025) and extend them in nurses and resident physicians across diverse specialties. By comparing these results to the latest US general population data (Terlizzi and Zablotsky, 2024; West et al., 2020), it is evident that while these clinicians entered the study with an initial burden significantly higher than the US working-class average, the intervention helped bridge this “health gap.” The consistency and magnitude of these preliminary effects across psychological domains highlight the potential of the digital MT as a scalable strategy to support well-being in healthcare settings affected by chronic stress.

The novelty of this approach is centered on its delivery architecture, which combines clinician-led co-design with an asynchronous, self-paced “micro-dosing” model. Importantly, our MT program is designed so that it can be completed in only seven 15-minute sessions (~1.75 h in total) and delivered in a format that is suitable for working professionals. The curriculum is delivered as a flexible audio course, enabling participants to integrate the training into daily transitions, such as commuting or breaks, without the requirement for a fixed daily schedule. Within this accessible format, the program utilizes an evidence-based habit-change theoretical framework to identify and alter unhelpful clinical habit loops through reinforcement learning (Brewer, 2019). While primarily a digital intervention, it also includes a minor supportive component in the form of bi-weekly instructor check-ins to address technical issues and maintain motivation. By contrast, traditional Mindfulness-Based Interventions (MBIs), including the standard 8-week MBSR course with weekly 2.5-hour sessions and a retreat, typically require 26 to 75 h of group and independent practice (Krasner et al., 2009). While face-to-face interventions have long been the gold standard, our findings align with a growing body of literature—including meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials—suggesting that digital mindfulness formats can produce comparable improvements in psychological distress and well-being (Hsu et al., 2024; Sommers-Spijkerman et al., 2021). In fact, some evidence indicates that digital delivery may even enhance engagement for certain populations by reducing the logistical barriers and chronic time constraints often experienced by clinicians (Spijkerman et al., 2016). By achieving significant improvements with a substantially lower time commitment, our results support the role of digital MT as a high-value alternative that maintains the core benefits of traditional mindfulness while meeting the practical needs of healthcare environments.

Moreover, our effects mirror those identified in recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses showing that person-centered, self-care interventions, such as MBIs, coaching, and self-compassion training delivered in mental health settings, are among the most effective approaches to mitigating burnout and improving well-being in healthcare professionals (Hsu et al., 2024; Kiratipaisarl et al., 2024; Lu and Ratnapalan, 2022).

Consistent with earlier studies (Antico and Brewer, 2025; Roy et al., 2020), we also found significant positive correlations between burnout and anxiety at all time points in both studies. This finding highlights how intertwined these constructs are, where the progression of emotional exhaustion and cynicism likely erodes a clinician's ability to cope, creating a reciprocal cycle that sustains high anxiety levels (Roy et al., 2021). Consequently, interventions should specifically measure and target both anxiety and burnout to effectively break this cycle.

8.2 Patterns observed in resident physicians: potential interpretations

The patterns observed among residents in Study 2, specifically the emergence of certain benefits only at follow-up, may suggest that for this cohort benefits potentially accumulate over time or require a longer integration period. However, as this study did not directly compare residents to nurses or attending physicians, these observations remain descriptive and cannot be used to conclude that the intervention works differently across professions. Instead, these findings highlight the preliminary nature of the results in this specific trainee population. Residents occupy a distinct role within the healthcare team, characterized by their transitional status as medical trainees (Ng et al., 2025; Phillips and Dalgarno, 2017). Unlike fully licensed physicians and experienced nurses, residents are still in training, balancing the dual demands of clinical responsibilities and academic progression. This stage of training is often marked by a heavy workload, uncertainty in clinical decision-making, and the development of a professional identity. These factors may contribute to distinct burnout risks and influence how residents engage with interventions or institutional initiatives (Phillips and Dalgarno, 2017). Furthermore, individual differences in psychological flexibility or baseline distress may be more influential than professional roles in determining these outcomes.

Additionally, the intervention used in these studies was not co-developed with resident input. As a result, it may not have fully captured their unique perspectives or addressed the practical realities of their day-to-day clinical experience. This lack of direct involvement in the design phase may have influenced the specific patterns of response observed in this cohort. Residents' specific stressors may affect how they perceive and respond to feedback or changes in clinical workflows, particularly if those changes were not designed with their specific needs in mind. These findings suggest that future refinements of the intervention should involve residents in the design process to ensure relevance, feasibility, and potential efficacy within their specific professional context.

8.3 Improvements in psychological factors associated with burnout

Our digital training program is grounded in a reinforcement-learning model (Brewer, 2019), which helps clinicians recognize and change unhelpful habitual behaviors that perpetuate anxiety and burnout. Over the modules, clinicians learn to manage intrusive thoughts and self-judgment with kindness, reduce stress and anxiety driven by uncertainty, and curb emotional contagion by cultivating curiosity. Consistent with our prior research with physicians (Antico and Brewer, 2025), nurses and resident physicians reported increased self-compassion and nonreactivity post-intervention. These findings align with a meta-analysis indicating that mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) significantly enhance self-compassion among healthcare professionals (Beaumont et al., 2021). Self-compassion involves treating oneself with kindness, particularly after clinical errors or perceived failures, and reducing harsh self-criticism, a trait often observed in clinicians and a key factor in burnout (Neff, 2003). Several studies suggest that high levels of self-compassion help alleviate empathy fatigue and promote compassionate care toward others (Chu, 2024; Crego et al., 2022; Kabakçı and Altun, 2022; McDonald et al., 2021). Additionally, enhanced nonreactivity enables clinicians to respond to challenging situations with greater equanimity rather than reactive distress. MBI practices support the acceptance and evaluation of one's current experience in a kind and non-judgmental manner (Wasson et al., 2020).

Furthermore, our findings indicate a reduction in intolerance of uncertainty among both nurses and residents following the intervention. This improvement may enable clinicians to better tolerate distress associated with uncertainty without becoming overwhelmed. Mindfulness training (MT) aids clinicians in developing greater tolerance for the unknown and diminishes the compulsive need for certainty or control, factors that can fuel anxiety and burnout (Bergin and Pakenham, 2016).

Understanding the role of these variables in relation to clinician burnout may provide important guidance for refining mindfulness interventions to address clinicians' day-to-day needs and to target specific cognitive and emotional vulnerabilities within healthcare workers, potentially increasing the program's clinical utility.

8.4 Implications for intervention design

These findings underscore the potential of this digital mindfulness training as a scalable and accessible tool to support clinician well-being across diverse healthcare roles. Combined with our previous results in attending physicians, these findings highlight the potential value of prioritizing participant autonomy and involving users in the design of such interventions. While this study did not involve a comparative test of design methodologies (i.e., UCD vs. non-UCD), the high levels of engagement and significant improvements observed across cohorts suggest that utilizing a UCD-informed program is a promising approach (Brewer and Antico, 2025; Gunawan et al., 2025).

The UCD approach was used for tailoring content, delivery, and scheduling to the unique workflows and stressors of each professional group, with the aim of enhancing both adoption and sustained use (Quezada et al., 2021). In the context of digital mindfulness programs for healthcare burnout, UCD can help identify and proactively address barriers such as time constraints, technological limitations, or skepticism about mindfulness's relevance. It also supports the inclusion of flexible features, such as brief modules, asynchronous access, and reminders, that accommodate demanding clinical schedules. Notably, the program's self-paced model prioritizes participant autonomy. The inclusion of bi-weekly instructor check-ins characterizes the intervention as a hybrid model rather than a purely automated tool. Evidence suggests that such “guided” digital interventions often yield higher adherence and larger effect sizes than unguided versions (Baumel et al., 2019; Linardon et al., 2019). We speculate that this hybrid approach, combining flexibility with human support, may have helped clinicians navigate the unpredictable and overwhelming demands of the clinical environment. However, as the current study did not directly measure the specific impact of autonomy on adherence, further research is required to formally test these mechanisms of engagement.

Importantly, involving diverse user groups may promote equity by ensuring interventions meet the needs of clinicians across specialties, career stages, genders, and cultural backgrounds. Ultimately, integrating UCD principles may support clinician ownership and empowerment, key factors for sustaining mental health initiatives within healthcare organizations (Swensen et al., 2016). Future efforts to address burnout could benefit from embedding participatory design as a central element of intervention development, evaluation, and refinement.

8.5 Limitations and future research

These findings are encouraging and build upon our earlier work. As proof-of-concept studies, they offer preliminary evidence of potential effectiveness and provide valuable guidance for designing future randomized controlled trials, including estimates of expected effect sizes. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged.

First, both studies used single-arm designs without control groups and had relatively short follow-up periods (one-month post-intervention). To more rigorously evaluate long-term efficacy, future research should incorporate randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with extended follow-up. Causal relationships cannot be reliably inferred from single-arm designs, as observed changes may result from confounding factors such as time, placebo effects, or other external influences (Cucherat et al., 2020). Future trials should utilize active control groups, such as time-matched digital health education or stress-management apps lacking a mindfulness component, to differentiate the specific effects of MT from non-specific factors like “digital placebo” effects or general self-care engagement. Furthermore, future RCTs should be designed to examine the psychological mechanisms through which MT exerts its potential benefits. Based on our findings, nonreactivity, self-compassion and intolerance of uncertainty are strong candidates for mediation analysis. Investigating these pathways would clarify whether the reduction in burnout is driven by a change in how clinicians relate to internal distress (nonreactivity) or a shift in their cognitive appraisal of clinical ambiguity (intolerance of uncertainty), or a move toward treating oneself with kindness rather than harsh self-criticism following clinical challenges (self-compassion). Such analyses would provide a more robust theoretical rationale for the intervention's impact and its ability to break the cycle of clinician distress.

Second, sample sizes were modest, and participants were not demographically representative; most were White and female. While this limits generalizability, it is noteworthy that women tend to report higher levels of burnout and anxiety, potentially making them more responsive to such interventions (Catuzzi and Beck, 2014; Hoff and Lee, 2021; Lyubarova et al., 2023). Nonetheless, future studies should aim for greater diversity in gender, race, and professional roles.

Third, these pilot studies did not employ specific burnout score cut-offs as inclusion or exclusion criteria. We intentionally adopted this approach to evaluate the intervention as a universal prevention and health promotion tool, observing its feasibility across the full spectrum of clinician well-being. However, the lack of a baseline severity threshold makes it difficult to distinguish between the program's primary preventive effects and its therapeutic utility for those already in distress. Future research should consider employing specific score thresholds to determine whether the intervention is more effective as a broad resilience-building tool or as a targeted treatment for high-risk individuals.

Fourth, these studies did not include a formal, systematic measurement of potential harms or adverse events, such as transient increases in anxiety or emotional discomfort during the use of the program. While no such events were spontaneously reported by participants during the bi-weekly instructor check-ins, the lack of a structured assessment means that subtle or transient negative experiences may have gone unrecorded. Future large-scale trials should incorporate standardized safety monitoring and reporting protocols to more comprehensively evaluate the safety profile of digital mindfulness interventions.

Another limitation is the selection bias that our use of a per-protocol analysis and convenience sampling may have introduced. By only including participants who completed the full course (n = 27 for nurses; n = 29 for residents), our results reflect the most motivated clinicians. Moreover, clinicians who enrolled may have had preexisting interest in or positive attitudes toward mindfulness, potentially biasing the results. This raises concerns about how effective the program would be among those less inclined to engage with mindfulness-based approaches. While this approach is appropriate for a feasibility study to see how the “full dose” works, it may overestimate the program's impact compared to a real-world setting.

In addition, the absence of resident involvement in the program's initial development may have contributed to their different outcomes compared to professional groups who participated in co-design. Future efforts should prioritize resident input to enhance program relevance and impact.

Furthermore, outcome measures relied exclusively on self-report and focused primarily on individual-level changes. Yet, both individual and organizational factors contribute to burnout. Future studies should include organizational-level outcomes such as work engagement, team functioning, and perceived leadership support. Integrating mindfulness programs with organizational strategies (e.g., staffing improvements, leadership training) may reveal synergistic effects.

Additionally, while the application tracked module completion, the research team did not have access to this usage metadata. Consequently, adherence was monitored via manual verification rather than objective digital engagement metrics. This prevents a granular analysis of potential dose-response relationships, examining whether higher levels of app engagement correlate with stronger clinical effects. Future research should aim to integrate these objective behavioral metrics to better understand intervention adherence and specific mechanisms of change.

Finally, while hard clinical outcomes like mortality or complication rates are difficult to measure across sites, more feasible proxies, such as patient adherence, provider–patient relationship quality, or compassionate care behaviors, could offer valuable insights. Incorporating objective biomarkers (e.g., cortisol, heart rate variability) and behavioral metrics may also deepen understanding of the physiological impact of mindfulness and its potential translation into clinical practice (Kiratipaisarl et al., 2024; Luberto et al., 2017). Understanding these broader systemic effects is essential for developing effective, sustainable strategies to reduce burnout and improve care quality.

9 Conclusions

Our findings suggest that digital mindfulness training is a feasible and promising approach that may be associated with reduction in burnout and anxiety, not only among attending physicians, as previously shown, but also among nurses and resident physicians across different specialties. In the nurse cohort, improvements were observed across multiple psychological domains. Among resident physicians, observed benefits appeared more gradually; specifically, significant reductions in the emotional exhaustion—but not cynicism—and anxiety emerged at the one-month follow-up.

Overall, these pilot results reinforce the need for comprehensive burnout strategies that address both individual well-being and systemic contributors and the importance of prioritizing participant autonomy and involving clinicians in the design process to ensure interventions are relevant, practical, and sustainable in real-world healthcare settings. While these preliminary findings are encouraging, they should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of a control group. Future randomized controlled trials are necessary to establish the definitive efficacy of this digital intervention.

To the resident rubbing tired eyes at dawn, the nurse enduring her twelfth hour on aching feet, and the attending physician still reviewing one last lab result, this pilot investigation suggests that such tools could represent a feasible step toward more sustainable care for those who care for others.
