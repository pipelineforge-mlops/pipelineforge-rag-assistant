Purpose

This review aimed to explore how artificial intelligence can be integrated with global genomic resources and to examine its implications for advancing precision medicine, with particular attention to population diversity, predictive modeling, and clinical translation.

Methods

A narrative review design was adopted, drawing on literature from major databases and supplementary search sources including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, Embase, and Google Scholar. Studies were selected based on relevance to artificial intelligence applications in genomic data analysis and precision medicine. Key information from included studies was extracted using a structured narrative extraction framework and synthesized thematically to identify key patterns and emerging insights.

Results

A total of 54 studies were included in this review. The synthesis identified five recurring application areas: genomic data integration, variant and disease association detection, disease susceptibility and risk prediction, treatment response prediction, and clinical decision support. Findings indicate that artificial intelligence can support the integration and analysis of multi-omics data, support the identification of genetic variants and disease associations, and improve predictive modeling for precision medicine. Incorporating diverse population data was also reported to improve model generalizability and reduce bias. However, challenges related to data standardization, interoperability, ethical governance, overfitting in small or biased cohorts, limited prospective clinical validation, reproducibility, and clinical implementation remain significant barriers.

Conclusion

The integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources holds substantial promise for advancing precision medicine by enabling more accurate, inclusive, and individualized precision medicine. However, this promise should be interpreted cautiously because many AI genomic models remain dependent on retrospective datasets, limited external validation, and variable reproducibility.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of genomic science has fundamentally reshaped our understanding of human biology, revealing an extraordinary level of genetic diversity across populations worldwide.1 Over the past two decades, large-scale initiatives have generated diverse global genomic resources, including population biobanks, reference datasets, disease-specific repositories, and genomic databases, rather than a single unified atlas.2 In this review, the term global genomic resources are used to refer collectively to heterogeneous large-scale population genomic databases and biobank-based resources rather than one specific named atlas. These resources provide a comprehensive view of how genetic differences are distributed across populations and how they contribute to health and disease.3,4 However, the sheer scale and complexity of such data present substantial analytical challenges.5 Traditional statistical approaches, while valuable, are often insufficient to capture the intricate, multidimensional relationships embedded within genomic datasets.6 As a result, there is a growing need for advanced computational frameworks capable of interpreting these complex patterns in a meaningful and clinically relevant manner.7 Multi-omics integration can improve AI-based genomic interpretation, but it also raises challenges related to harmonization, interoperability, and bias in underrepresented populations.8

Artificial intelligence has emerged as a powerful enabler in this context, offering innovative tools to process, integrate, and analyze large-scale genomic data.9 Techniques such as machine learning and deep learning are particularly well suited for identifying hidden structures and associations within high-dimensional datasets.10 By leveraging these approaches, researchers can move beyond linear models and uncover nonlinear interactions between genetic variants, environmental factors, and disease phenotypes.11 This capability is especially important in the era of precision medicine, where the goal is to tailor healthcare interventions based on individual genetic profiles.12,13 The integration of artificial intelligence with genomic resources therefore represents more than a technological advancement; it reflects a paradigm shift toward data-driven and individualized healthcare systems that are responsive to biological complexity.14

At the same time, the concept of global genomic resources underscores the importance of population diversity in genomic research.15 Human genetic variation is not evenly distributed, and differences across populations can significantly influence disease susceptibility, drug response, and clinical outcomes.16,17 Historically, many genomic studies have been disproportionately focused on specific populations, leading to gaps in knowledge and potential biases in clinical applications.18,19 The integration of diverse datasets into a unified analytical framework is essential for ensuring that genomic insights are broadly applicable and equitable.20 Artificial intelligence provides a means to harmonize and analyze such heterogeneous data, enabling the identification of population-specific patterns while maintaining a global perspective.21 This approach not only enhances scientific understanding but also supports the development of more inclusive and representative precision medicine strategies.22,23

Although several reviews have examined artificial intelligence in precision medicine, cancer genomics, multi-omics integration, pharmacogenomics, and genomic data interpretation,2,4,7,9,10 the existing review literature remains relatively fragmented across specific diseases, computational techniques, or clinical applications. In contrast, this review focuses on the broader integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources, with particular attention to how population diversity, large-scale genomic variation, predictive modeling, and clinical translation collectively shape precision medicine. This focus provides a timely synthesis that extends beyond technique-centered or disease-specific discussions by emphasizing the role of diverse genomic resources in developing more generalizable and equitable AI-supported precision medicine frameworks.

Despite these promising developments, the integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources is not without challenges. Issues related to data standardization, interoperability, and quality remain significant barriers to effective data integration.24 Furthermore, the use of sensitive genetic information raises important ethical and legal considerations, including concerns about privacy, consent, and data security.25 Addressing these challenges requires a multidisciplinary effort that brings together expertise from genomics, computer science, clinical medicine, and ethics.26,27 Accordingly, this narrative review was guided by the following question: how can artificial intelligence be integrated with global genomic resources to support precision medicine, and what implications does this integration have for population diversity, predictive modeling, clinical translation, and ethical governance? Specifically, the review aims to synthesize current evidence on AI-enabled genomic data integration, examine the contribution of diverse population data to model generalizability and equity, identify major applications in disease risk prediction and treatment response, and discuss key barriers affecting responsible clinical implementation.

Methods

Study Design and Conceptual Orientation

This narrative review was conducted to explore the integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources and human genetic variation and its implications for precision medicine. An interpretive and integrative approach was adopted to address the interdisciplinary nature of the topic, allowing the synthesis of evidence from genomics, computational science, and clinical research. This design provided a flexible yet structured way to examine how artificial intelligence supports the analysis and application of complex genomic data. The conceptual framework guiding this review is illustrated in Figure 1, which presents the integration process as a continuum from diverse data inputs such as genomic, multi omics, and electronic health record data through an artificial intelligence layer incorporating machine learning, deep learning, graph-based models, and explainable AI. These approaches support key processes including data integration, pattern recognition, and predictive modeling, ultimately leading to clinical outputs such as disease prediction, risk stratification, personalized treatment, and decision support. This framework provides a structured basis for synthesizing the literature and interpreting how artificial intelligence enables the translation of complex genomic data into clinically meaningful insights, while also emphasizing the importance of scalability, diversity, and real-world applicability in precision medicine. A narrative and integrative review design was selected instead of a systematic or scoping review because the objective was to synthesize concepts, applications, methodological patterns, and translational implications across heterogeneous evidence rather than to answer a narrowly defined intervention question or map a single bounded evidence field. The search was designed to capture studies at the intersection of artificial intelligence and genomic variation. Terms such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, genomic diversity, population genomics, global genomic resources, and precision medicine were used in various combinations. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To ensure the relevance and quality of the included literature, clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were established. Studies were included if they addressed at least one of the following areas: application of artificial intelligence in genomic data analysis, development or use of large-scale genetic variation datasets, or implications of such integration for personalized or precision medicine. Both original research studies and high-quality review articles were considered, as they provided complementary perspectives on the topic. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on genetic variation without any computational or analytical integration, or if they discussed artificial intelligence in contexts unrelated to genomics or healthcare. Non-scholarly sources, such as blog posts or unverified reports, were also excluded to maintain academic rigor. The criteria were applied consistently throughout the selection process.

Study Selection Process

The process of selecting studies involved multiple stages to ensure a systematic yet flexible approach. Initially, titles and abstracts of retrieved articles were screened to identify those that appeared relevant to the research objectives. This step helped to narrow down the large pool of studies to a more manageable number. Subsequently, full-text articles were reviewed in detail to confirm their eligibility. During this stage, particular attention was paid to how well each study aligned with the central theme of integrating artificial intelligence with genomic variation for clinical applications. When uncertainties arose, studies were re-evaluated to determine their contribution to the overall narrative. This iterative process allowed for thoughtful inclusion of diverse perspectives while maintaining coherence and focus. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by independent full-text assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and unresolved cases were adjudicated by a third reviewer. Because this was a narrative review, no formal inter-rater agreement statistics were calculated.

Data Extraction Strategy

A structured data extraction approach was adopted to systematically capture key information from the selected studies. For each article, details such as study objectives, type of genomic data used, artificial intelligence techniques applied, and reported outcomes were recorded. Additional information regarding study design, population characteristics, and clinical implications was also documented. The extraction process emphasized clarity and consistency, ensuring that comparable data could be analyzed across studies. Rather than simply listing findings, the extracted data were organized into meaningful categories, enabling deeper interpretation and synthesis. This approach helped to identify recurring patterns and relationships within literature.

Quality and Risk-of-Bias Consideration

Because the included studies used heterogeneous designs, a structured narrative quality and risk-of-bias consideration was conducted to guide interpretation of the evidence. This appraisal was not used to exclude studies but to distinguish more robust empirical evidence from preliminary or exploratory findings. For quantitative and model-development studies, attention was given to study design, dataset size and transparency, population representation, validation strategy, external or prospective testing, reporting of model performance, and potential risks of overfitting or bias. For qualitative stakeholder studies, appraisal considered the relevance of participants, clarity of data collection, transparency of analysis, and credibility of interpretation. For proof-of-concept studies, bibliometric analyses, educational evaluations, and conceptual papers, emphasis was placed on methodological transparency, relevance to AI-genomics integration, and whether conclusions were proportionate to the evidence presented. This approach allowed the synthesis to retain the breadth of a narrative review while interpreting findings according to the strength and maturity of the supporting evidence.

Thematic Analysis and Synthesis

The analysis of the extracted data was conducted using a thematic synthesis approach, informed by the quality and risk-of-bias consideration described above. Studies were grouped according to key themes that emerged during the review process. Themes were derived through repeated reading of extracted study characteristics and findings, followed by grouping of recurring concepts related to genomic data integration, population diversity, predictive modeling, clinical translation, and ethical governance. The synthesis compared consistencies and limitations across studies, with attention to validation quality, population representation, and translational maturity. These themes included the role of artificial intelligence in genomic data integration, the importance of population diversity in global genomic resources, advancements in predictive modeling, and challenges related to clinical translation. Each theme was examined in detail to understand how different studies contributed to the broader narrative. The synthesis process involved comparing findings across studies, identifying consistencies and discrepancies, and interpreting their implications for precision medicine. This approach allowed for a nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the topic, moving beyond simple description to critical interpretation.

Results

Summary of Included Studies

A total of 54 studies were included in this review (Figure 2), representing a diverse and multidisciplinary body of evidence on the integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources and human genetic variation. The included studies comprised a range of research designs, including retrospective cohort studies, machine learning model development and validation studies, multi-omics integration analyses, and database or knowledge-graph construction, alongside a smaller number of conceptual investigations (Table 2). Across these studies, a wide spectrum of genomic data types was utilized, including whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, genome-wide association study data, and transcriptomics, often integrated with proteomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics (Figure 3). Notably, many studies linked genomic datasets with electronic health records and large-scale biobank data, particularly from multi-ethnic populations, underscoring the growing emphasis on incorporating global genetic diversity to improve the representativeness and clinical relevance of findings. From a clinical and translational perspective, the findings highlighted the significant potential of artificial intelligence to advance precision medicine. AI-driven models supported personalized treatment selection, pharmacogenomic decision-making, and real-time clinical decision support across various disease domains, including cancer, neurological disorders, and rare genetic conditions. Several studies also demonstrated that incorporating diverse population data improved model generalizability and reduced bias, contributing to more equitable healthcare outcomes. However, challenges related to data heterogeneity, lack of standardization, interoperability issues, and ethical concerns regarding data privacy and governance were frequently reported. Overall, the included studies indicate that while artificial intelligence is transforming genomic research and enabling more precise and individualized care, the current evidence remains constrained by limited prospective clinical validation, inconsistent external testing, and reproducibility concerns across published machine learning genomic models.

Role of Artificial Intelligence in Integrating Genomic Data

Artificial intelligence has emerged as a potentially important force in the integration of large-scale genomic datasets, particularly those derived from global genomic resources.28 Across the reviewed literature, AI-driven approaches such as machine learning, deep learning, and probabilistic modeling were consistently applied to manage and interpret highly complex genomic information. These techniques demonstrated the ability to process multi-layered datasets, including whole genome sequencing, transcriptomics, and epigenomic profiles, thereby enabling the identification of subtle patterns that would be difficult to detect through conventional statistical methods.29 Studies highlighted that AI algorithms could efficiently harmonize heterogeneous datasets collected from diverse populations, overcoming challenges related to data fragmentation and variability.30,31 This integration has facilitated a more unified understanding of genetic variation at a global scale, supporting the development of comprehensive genomic resources that are both scalable and adaptable.32

In addition to technical integration, artificial intelligence contributed significantly to enhancing the interpretability of genomic data.33 Several studies reported the use of explainable AI frameworks to uncover biologically meaningful associations between genetic variants and disease phenotypes.34,35 These models were particularly effective in identifying rare variants and population-specific mutations that are often underrepresented in traditional datasets.36 By enabling dynamic data integration and continuous learning, AI systems were shown to improve the accuracy of genomic interpretation over time.37 The findings suggest that the integration of AI with global genomic resources not only improves data processing efficiency but also deepens biological insight, thereby laying the foundation for more informed clinical decision-making in precision medicine.80 Nevertheless, these benefits depend strongly on data quality, cohort diversity, and transparent model development; otherwise, AI systems may amplify existing biases or produce findings that are difficult to reproduce.

Population Diversity and Representation in Global Genomic Resources

A prominent theme across the reviewed studies was the critical importance of population diversity in the construction and utilization of global genomic resources.38,39 The results revealed that many existing genomic databases have historically been skewed toward populations of European ancestry, leading to gaps in knowledge regarding genetic variation in underrepresented groups.40,41 Artificial intelligence played a pivotal role in addressing this imbalance by enabling the inclusion and analysis of diverse datasets from multiple geographic and ethnic populations.42 AI-driven clustering and classification techniques were used to identify population-specific genetic signatures, thereby improving the representativeness and inclusivity of genomic resources.43 These advancements have contributed to a more equitable understanding of human genetic diversity and its implications for health and disease.44

Furthermore, the integration of diverse population data into AI models significantly enhanced the generalizability and robustness of predictive algorithms. Studies demonstrated that models trained on multi-ethnic datasets performed better in identifying disease risk and treatment responses across different populations compared to those trained on homogeneous data.45–47 This finding underscores the importance of diversity not only as an ethical consideration but also as a scientific necessity.48 The results indicate that incorporating global genetic variation into AI frameworks may reduce bias and improve the clinical applicability of precision medicine.34,49 At the same time, models trained on underrepresented or unevenly sampled populations may still produce biased predictions, and diversity alone does not eliminate the need for rigorous validation across ancestry groups.29 However, challenges related to data accessibility, ethical governance, and cross-border data sharing were also highlighted, suggesting the need for collaborative efforts to ensure equitable representation in future genomic research.50 Together, these findings show that population diversity is central to both the scientific validity and equity of AI-supported precision medicine. The reviewed literature also showed geographic and institutional clustering, with many studies relying on large biobanks, institutional datasets, or high-resource research settings. This may limit the applicability of AI-genomics models in underserved populations and resource-limited health systems.

Clinical Translation and Decision Support Systems

The translation of AI-integrated genomic insights into clinical practice was a key focus of the reviewed studies. Results indicated that decision support systems powered by artificial intelligence are increasingly being used to assist clinicians in interpreting complex genomic data.60,61 These systems integrate patient-specific genetic information with clinical guidelines and evidence-based knowledge to provide actionable recommendations.62 In many cases, AI tools were embedded within electronic health record systems, allowing seamless access to genomic insights during clinical workflows.63 This integration has the potential to improve the efficiency and accuracy of clinical decision-making, particularly in areas such as oncology, rare diseases, and pharmacogenomics.64 However, many systems remain at the development or retrospective validation stage rather than being prospectively tested in routine clinical care.

Despite these advancements, the studies also highlighted several challenges in the clinical implementation of AI-driven systems. Issues related to data standardization, interoperability, and user trust were frequently reported.65,66 Clinicians expressed concerns about the transparency of AI algorithms and the potential for over-reliance on automated recommendations.63,64 To address these challenges, some studies emphasized the importance of developing explainable AI models and providing adequate training for healthcare professionals.67,68 Overall, the results suggest that while AI has significant potential to enhance clinical decision support, its successful integration into practice requires careful consideration of technical, ethical, and human factors. Particular attention is needed to ensure that model outputs are reproducible, clinically validated, and not used as substitutes for professional judgment.

Ethical, Legal, and Social Implications

The integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources raises important ethical, legal, and social considerations, which were extensively discussed in the reviewed literature. One of the primary concerns was the protection of individual privacy and the secure handling of sensitive genomic data.69 AI systems require access to large datasets to function effectively, but this raises the risk of data breaches and unauthorized use.70 Studies emphasized the need for robust data governance frameworks, including encryption, anonymization, and controlled access mechanisms, to ensure the security of genomic information.71,72 Additionally, the potential for misuse of genetic data in areas such as insurance and employment discrimination was highlighted as a significant ethical challenge.73

Another key issue identified was the need for equitable access to AI-driven precision medicine.74 While technological advancements have the potential to improve healthcare outcomes, there is a risk that these benefits may not be distributed equally across populations.75 Studies pointed out that resource-limited settings may face barriers in adopting AI technologies due to infrastructure and cost constraints. Furthermore, the lack of representation of certain populations in genomic datasets could exacerbate existing health disparities.76,77 The findings underscore the importance of developing inclusive policies and international collaborations to ensure that the benefits of AI-integrated genomic research are accessible to all. Ethical considerations should be integrated into every stage of research and implementation to promote fairness and social responsibility. Therefore, responsible governance should not be treated as a separate concern but as a core requirement for equitable AI-genomics integration.

Challenges and Future Directions in AI-Genomics Integration

The reviewed studies identified several technical and methodological challenges that continue to hinder the full realization of AI integration with global genomic resources. One of the most important challenges is the complexity and scale of genomic data, which requires advanced computational resources and sophisticated algorithms.38,45 Issues related to data quality, missing information, and heterogeneity were frequently reported, affecting the reliability of AI models.41 These limitations can contribute to overfitting, poor transportability across populations, and inconsistent performance when models are applied outside the original training setting. Additionally, the lack of standardized data formats and interoperability between different systems poses a barrier to effective data integration.78 Researchers emphasized the need for the development of unified frameworks and standards to facilitate seamless data exchange and analysis.79

Looking forward, the studies highlighted several promising directions for future research and development. Advances in federated learning and decentralized AI were identified as potential solutions for addressing data privacy concerns while enabling collaborative analysis across institutions.69,80 The integration of multi-omics data, including proteomics and metabolomics, was also suggested to enhance the depth and accuracy of genomic insights.73 Furthermore, the incorporation of real-world data, such as electronic health records and wearable device data, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of patient health.81 However, future research should prioritize prospective validation, external replication, standardized reporting, and transparent evaluation before AI genomic models are implemented clinically. The findings suggest that continued innovation and interdisciplinary collaboration will be essential for overcoming current challenges and unlocking the full potential of AI in precision medicine.

Discussion

The integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources represents an important but still developing shift in how biomedical knowledge is generated and translated into clinical practice.82 Rather than functioning solely as a computational tool, artificial intelligence is increasingly shaping the way researchers conceptualize genetic complexity and disease mechanisms.83,84 Existing literature suggests that conventional analytical approaches are often insufficient to capture the multidimensional nature of genomic data, particularly when dealing with large-scale and heterogeneous datasets.85,86 In contrast, artificial intelligence can introduce adaptive and iterative learning processes that allow for continuous refinement of biological insights.87 However, these advantages should be interpreted cautiously, as model performance may be overestimated when training data are small, biased, or insufficiently separated from validation data. This aligns with broader developments in biomedical informatics, where learning health systems are designed to evolve through ongoing data integration and feedback.88,89 The findings of this review therefore reflect not only technical progress but also the need for rigorous validation, reproducibility, and careful clinical interpretation within data-driven precision medicine frameworks.

A key aspect of this transformation is the increasing emphasis on population diversity in genomic research.90 Numerous studies have demonstrated that limited representation of diverse populations can lead to biased findings and reduced generalizability of predictive models.91,92 Artificial intelligence offers a practical solution by enabling the integration and analysis of data from multiple populations, thereby improving the inclusiveness and robustness of genomic research.93 Nevertheless, the inclusion of diverse datasets does not automatically eliminate bias, particularly when sample sizes remain uneven across ancestry groups or when models are not externally validated in underrepresented populations. However, the importance of diversity extends beyond methodological considerations. It is closely linked to global disparities in research participation and access to healthcare innovation.94 Prior studies have highlighted that underrepresentation of certain populations is often driven by structural inequalities, including limited research infrastructure and funding in low- and middle-income regions.95–97 Consequently, while artificial intelligence can facilitate the technical inclusion of diverse datasets, addressing the root causes of inequity requires coordinated global efforts, policy interventions, and investment in capacity building.98

The advancement of predictive modeling through artificial intelligence has significant implications for the evolution of precision medicine.99 Research across various clinical domains indicates that machine learning models can enhance the prediction of disease risk, progression, and treatment response by integrating genomic and clinical data.100,101 These developments are contributing to a shift from reactive to proactive healthcare, where interventions are tailored based on individual risk profiles.102 The findings of this review support this transition, particularly in the context of incorporating global genetic variation into predictive frameworks.103 However, this transition remains constrained by important current limitations, including overfitting, inconsistent external testing, limited prospective clinical validation, and variable reproducibility across published machine learning genomic models. The discussion should also consider the challenges associated with model interpretability and clinical acceptance.104 Studies have consistently reported that healthcare professionals are more likely to adopt predictive tools when the underlying mechanisms are transparent and align with clinical reasoning.105,106 This underscores the importance of explainable artificial intelligence, which aims to provide insights into how predictions are generated and to enhance trust in algorithmic outputs.107 Even explainable models require cautious interpretation, as transparency alone does not guarantee clinical validity or reproducible performance across different settings.

Translating artificial intelligence-driven genomic insights into routine clinical practice remains a complex and multifaceted process.108 While decision support systems have shown promise in improving diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning, their implementation is often hindered by practical challenges.109 These include issues related to data interoperability, integration with existing electronic health record systems, and variability in digital literacy among healthcare providers.110,111 Furthermore, the introduction of artificial intelligence into clinical workflows raises important questions about responsibility and decision-making authority.112 When algorithms provide recommendations based on complex genomic analyses, clinicians should balance these insights with their professional judgment.113 This is particularly important because many AI genomic tools remain at the retrospective development or internal validation stage rather than being tested prospectively in real-world clinical workflows. Previous research suggests that successful integration requires not only technological innovation but also organizational readiness, training, and clear governance structures.114,115 Without these elements, the potential benefits of artificial intelligence may not be fully realized in clinical settings.

Ethical, legal, and social considerations are central to the responsible integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic data.1,116 The use of large-scale genetic information raises concerns related to privacy, data security, and informed consent.2,13,117 Studies have emphasized the need for robust data governance frameworks that ensure the protection of sensitive information while enabling meaningful research.7,8,118 At the same time, there is a growing recognition that the benefits of artificial intelligence-driven precision medicine should be distributed equitably.119 There is a risk that technological advancements may disproportionately benefit populations with greater access to healthcare resources, thereby widening existing disparities.120,121 These concerns are closely linked to methodological issues, such as biased cohorts, poorly documented training datasets, and non-reproducible models that can reinforce existing inequities in genomic medicine. Addressing these challenges requires commitment to inclusive research practices, transparent policies, and international collaboration. Ultimately, the integration of artificial intelligence with genomic data should be guided not only by scientific and clinical objectives but also by principles of fairness, accountability, reproducibility, validation rigor, and social responsibility.

Implications for Practice and Policy

The integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources has significant implications for clinical practice, particularly in advancing personalized and data-driven care. Healthcare providers can leverage AI-enhanced genomic insights to improve diagnostic precision, identify disease risks earlier, and tailor treatment strategies based on individual genetic profiles. However, clinical use should be approached cautiously and limited to models that have undergone adequate external and prospective validation, with clear evidence of reproducibility and fairness across populations. Practice and policy recommendations should therefore be weighted toward studies with stronger empirical designs, transparent model reporting, external validation, and clinically relevant outcomes. Findings from qualitative stakeholder studies, proof-of-concept demonstrations, bibliometric analyses, educational evaluations, and conceptual papers should be interpreted as preliminary or supportive rather than as direct evidence for implementation. This shift toward proactive and individualized care requires clinicians to develop competencies in interpreting AI-supported outputs and integrating them into routine decision-making. Clinicians should also remain aware of the risks of overfitting, biased training cohorts, and limited generalizability when interpreting AI-generated genomic predictions. In addition, healthcare systems should invest in interoperable digital infrastructures that enable seamless integration of genomic data with electronic health records. Training programs for healthcare professionals are essential to ensure that AI tools are used effectively and responsibly, fostering trust and improving patient outcomes across diverse clinical settings.

From a policy perspective, the findings highlight the need for comprehensive frameworks that address data governance, equity, and cross-border collaboration. Policymakers should establish clear regulations for data privacy, consent, and ethical use of genomic information while promoting standardized data formats to enhance interoperability. Equally important is the need to ensure equitable access to AI-driven precision medicine, particularly in low-resource settings where disparities may be exacerbated. Investment in infrastructure, capacity building, and international data-sharing agreements will be critical to support inclusive genomic research. Policies should also encourage the development of transparent and explainable AI systems to enhance accountability and public trust. In addition, regulatory and institutional frameworks require documentation of training data composition, validation strategy, overfitting controls, external testing, and reproducibility before clinical deployment. These coordinated efforts will be essential to translate technological advancements into sustainable and equitable healthcare improvements.

Conclusion

The integration of artificial intelligence with global genomic resources represents a promising but still evolving advancement in precision medicine, offering new opportunities to understand complex biological systems and improve individualized healthcare. By enabling the analysis of large-scale, diverse genomic datasets, artificial intelligence can enhance disease prediction, support targeted therapeutic interventions, and facilitate more inclusive research practices. However, current AI applications in genomics should not be interpreted as fully established clinical solutions, as many models remain limited by overfitting risks, small or biased cohorts, insufficient external validation, limited prospective clinical testing, and variable reproducibility. These conclusions should also be interpreted in light of the methodological heterogeneity of the included studies, which varied in design, data sources, validation strategies, and translational maturity. The successful translation of these innovations into clinical and public health contexts therefore depends on addressing persistent challenges related to data quality, interoperability, ethical governance, equitable access, model transparency, validation rigor, and reproducibility. A collaborative and multidisciplinary approach that aligns technological development with policy and clinical readiness is essential to maximize the benefits of this integration. Ultimately, the future of precision medicine will depend on the ability to harness artificial intelligence in a manner that is scientifically robust, ethically sound, clinically validated, reproducible, and globally inclusive.
